You open your laptop. You type: “I want to retire in 20 years with $1.5 million and I can save $5,000 monthly.”

Two seconds later, an algorithm tells you your odds: 100%.

This is not financial advice. This is not a human financial advisor. This is Mycroft, a system built by Darshan Rajopadhye—a student in Nik Bear Brown’s course at Northeastern University—that claims to translate your rambling financial anxieties into cold mathematical certainty. In 2.34 seconds. With a confidence score of 0.85.

The question is not whether it works. The question is what happens when it does.

The Architecture of Certainty

The system operates on a brutal simplicity. You speak. It calculates. You act.

Behind that simplicity: a FastAPI backend making 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations per goal, pulling real market data from Yahoo Finance, running a Large Language Model called llama3.1 through Ollama, and spitting out success probabilities with percentile breakdowns from the 5th to the 95th percentile. The median outcome. The worst-case scenario. The expected shortfall.

All rendered in interactive charts built with Recharts and React, styled with Tailwind CSS, packaged in a Vite frontend that loads in milliseconds.

Consider what this means. The traditional path to investment planning: Schedule appointment. Drive to office. Sit across from advisor. Fill out risk tolerance questionnaire. Wait for analysis. Schedule follow-up. Repeat in six months. Total time invested: 4-6 hours. Total cost: potentially hundreds or thousands of dollars.

The Mycroft path: Type sentence. Get analysis. Adjust parameters. See new projections. Iterate until satisfied. Total time: under five minutes. Total cost: zero dollars (if you run it locally).

The Prompt That Became a Portfolio

Here’s what happens when you type your goal into the system:

The text hits an endpoint: POST /goals/extract. The Ollama client checks health, builds an extraction prompt with few-shot examples, calls the LLM with a temperature of 0.1 (low randomness, high consistency), parses the JSON response, validates it against Pydantic schemas, extracts entities (amounts, dates, keywords), assigns confidence scores, and returns structured data.

Your typos don’t matter. Your grammar doesn’t matter. “I want to retire in 20 years with 1.5” works the same as “Retirement goal: $1,500,000 target, 20-year timeline.” The LLM handles natural language understanding. It infers risk tolerance from context. It detects multiple goals in a single paragraph. It assigns priority levels.

Then comes the simulation. The system calls POST /simulation/simulate with your extracted goals. It fetches historical market data: SPY for stocks (10% annual return, 16% volatility), AGG for bonds (4.5% return, 6% volatility), ^IRX for cash (3% return, 1% volatility). If Yahoo Finance fails, it uses synthetic data based on 70 years of historical statistics.

The Monte Carlo engine—vectorized with NumPy for efficiency—runs your scenario 1,000 times. Each simulation: starting with your current savings, applying monthly contributions adjusted for 3% inflation, generating random returns from the portfolio’s statistical distribution, rebalancing annually, calculating the final outcome after 240 months.

The results: a success probability (what percentage of simulations hit your target), a median outcome ($2.145 million in the demo), a worst-case 10th percentile ($1.65 million), a best-case 90th percentile ($2.85 million), and specific recommendations.

Processing time for 1,000 simulations across two goals: under one second.

The Democratization Problem

This is where the narrative gets uncomfortable.

Financial literacy in America is a disaster. According to the National Financial Educators Council, the average American lost $1,819 to a lack of financial knowledge in 2023. Fifty-seven percent of Americans are financially illiterate. Thirty-three percent have no retirement savings whatsoever.

The financial advisory industry charges 1% of assets under management annually. On a $500,000 portfolio, that’s $5,000 per year. Over 20 years at 10% growth, that 1% fee doesn’t cost you $100,000—it costs you $304,000 in compound returns.

Now consider: Mycroft runs locally. No subscription. No percentage. No minimums. The entire backend is open-source on GitHub. The documentation includes curl commands for every endpoint. The API is decoupled—you could integrate it into Excel, a mobile app, a chatbot, a Slack bot, any backend service.

The traditional model: expertise as a scarce resource, access controlled by credentials and fees, advice dispensed in scheduled intervals.

The Mycroft model: expertise as a commodity, access controlled by nothing but internet connectivity, advice generated on-demand at computational speed.

You see the problem. Or maybe you see the solution. Depends on which side of the 1% fee you’re on.

The Confidence Score

The system returns a confidence score: 0.85 for the retirement goal in the demo.

What does that mean? That the LLM is 85% confident it extracted the right information from your sentence? That there’s a 15% chance it misunderstood “retire in 20 years” or “$1.5 million”? That you should trust this number 85% of the time?

The documentation doesn’t say. The code doesn’t specify. The video demo doesn’t explain.

This is the seam in the system. The place where natural language processing meets financial planning and nobody has defined the error bars. You typed a sentence. An algorithm parsed it. A simulation ran. A probability emerged. At what point did certainty enter the process?

Traditional financial advisors undergo Series 65 licensing. They carry errors and omissions insurance. They’re subject to fiduciary duty. They can be sued for negligence.

An LLM running on your laptop carries none of these obligations. It’s a tool. Like a calculator. If you input the wrong numbers, you get the wrong answer. If you misunderstand the output, that’s on you.

Except it doesn’t feel like a calculator. It feels like a conversation. You describe your goal in plain English. It responds with structured advice. The interface design—clean, modern, interactive charts—conveys authority. The speed conveys confidence. The precision (success probability: 99.9%) conveys certainty.

This is the UX of financial automation: making complex mathematics feel simple, making probabilistic forecasting feel deterministic, making computational guesswork feel like expert analysis.

The Second Goal Problem

In the demo video, Darshan adds a second goal mid-simulation. Target amount: unclear from the audio. Timeline: 15 years. Current savings: $45,000.

The success rate drops to zero.

The system recommends: “Increase contribution by $3,500.”

He increases it to $4,000. Runs the simulation again. Success rate: 99.9%.

This is the moment where the system’s power becomes visible. Not in the first goal—everyone knows $5,000 monthly for 20 years compounds to well over $1.5 million at 10% returns. That’s basic exponential math.

The power is in the iteration. The instant feedback loop. The ability to test scenarios in real-time. What if I saved $4,000 instead of $5,000? What if I extended the timeline to 25 years? What if I shifted allocation from 70% stocks to 80% stocks? What if I assumed 4% inflation instead of 3%?

Traditional financial planning: ask these questions, wait for your advisor to run the numbers, schedule another meeting, get your answers.

Mycroft: ask these questions, watch the numbers update in real-time, iterate until you find a plan that works.

The difference isn’t just speed. It’s agency. The system doesn’t tell you what to do—it shows you what happens if you do it. You remain the decision-maker. The algorithm is just the calculator, scaled up to handle thousands of probabilistic scenarios simultaneously.

The Fallback Mechanism

Here’s a detail from the documentation that reveals something important:

“Fallback Strategy: If yfinance fails, uses synthetic data based on historical statistics.”

Translation: if the system can’t fetch real market data from Yahoo Finance, it generates fake data that looks statistically similar to real data.

This is either reassuring or terrifying, depending on your perspective.

Reassuring: the system is robust, it won’t crash if an API goes down, it provides continuity of service.

Terrifying: you might be making life-altering financial decisions based on synthetic data without knowing it, because the interface doesn’t tell you whether the returns are real or simulated.

The documentation mentions this. The API response doesn’t flag it. The frontend charts don’t indicate it. You get the same clean visualizations, the same precise percentiles, the same confident recommendations whether the underlying data came from 70 years of S&P 500 history or from a random number generator tuned to match historical means and standard deviations.

This is the production-grade implementation of a student project. Clean separation of concerns. Error handling. Fallback mechanisms. But somewhere in that engineering elegance, the epistemological status of the output became ambiguous.

The Decoupled Architecture

The documentation emphasizes this point repeatedly: “These REST API endpoints are completely decoupled from the front end, which means that they can be integrated into any application.”

The examples: mobile app, Excel integration, chatbot integration, other backend services.

The implication: this isn’t just a demo. This is infrastructure. The goal extraction endpoint doesn’t care what calls it. The simulation endpoint doesn’t care where the goals came from. The health check endpoint is waiting for something to check it.

Darshan built this as coursework. But the architecture suggests something more ambitious: a financial planning microservice that could plug into anything. Imagine it running inside a bank’s mobile app. Inside a payroll system. Inside a benefits enrollment platform. Inside a real estate search engine that calculates how much house you can afford while still hitting your retirement goals.

The traditional financial services stack: proprietary systems, closed data, manual processes, human intermediaries.

The Mycroft architecture: open APIs, public documentation, automated analysis, machine intermediaries.

FastAPI even auto-generates interactive documentation at /docs. You can test every endpoint directly in your browser without writing a single line of code. The barrier to integration isn’t technical complexity—it’s the decision to integrate.

The 4,567.89 Milliseconds

The simulation endpoint returns processing time in milliseconds. In the demo’s JSON response: 4,567.89 ms to run 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations across one goal.

That’s 4.6 seconds. That’s 219 simulations per second. That’s one complete 20-year financial projection—240 months of compound returns, inflation adjustments, rebalancing events—every 4.6 milliseconds.

For context: the average American spends 13 hours per year managing their finances. That’s 780 minutes. At 4.6 seconds per complete simulation, you could run 10,174 different financial scenarios in the time you currently spend managing your money.

You won’t. Nobody will. But the technical capability exists. The constraint isn’t computational—it’s cognitive. The system can generate scenarios faster than you can evaluate them. The bottleneck is human decision-making, not algorithmic processing.

This is the asymmetry at the heart of automated financial planning. The algorithm scales infinitely. Your attention doesn’t. The system can show you 10,000 possible futures. You can only live one of them. All the computational power in the world can’t solve the problem of choosing which future to pursue.

The Missing Disclaimer

The documentation includes sections on architecture, endpoints, schemas, setup, deployment, security, and troubleshooting.

It does not include a section on liability.

It does not include text saying “This is not financial advice.”

It does not include warnings about market risk, model limitations, or the difference between historical returns and future performance.

It does not require users to acknowledge that past performance doesn’t predict future results.

It does not ask for your investment objectives, time horizon, or risk tolerance before running simulations.

It just... runs them.

This is technically accurate. The system is a tool. Like a spreadsheet. You wouldn’t expect Excel to disclaim that formulas might contain errors. You wouldn’t expect a calculator to warn you that multiplication doesn’t guarantee correct answers if you input wrong numbers.

But Mycroft doesn’t feel like Excel. The natural language processing creates an illusion of understanding. The clean visualizations create an illusion of authority. The precise probabilities create an illusion of certainty. The system doesn’t just calculate—it interprets, recommends, guides.

Somewhere in that transformation from tool to advisor, legal and ethical questions emerge. Questions that a student project doesn’t have to answer. Questions that a production financial service absolutely does.

The Open Source Question

The entire backend is on GitHub. The repository is public. The documentation is comprehensive. The code is clean, commented, properly structured with type hints and Pydantic schemas.

Anyone can fork it. Anyone can deploy it. Anyone can modify it. Anyone can integrate it into their own service.

This is either the democratization of financial technology or the proliferation of unlicensed financial advice systems, depending on your regulatory perspective.

The traditional financial services model depends on controlled access to expertise. If financial planning tools become free, open-source commodities that anyone can deploy and customize, what happens to the business model? What happens to the regulatory framework built around licensed professionals providing personalized advice for a fee?

Darshan didn’t build Mycroft to disrupt an industry. He built it as coursework for a class at Northeastern. But the architecture suggests capabilities that extend far beyond academic demonstration. The documentation’s repeated emphasis on the decoupled API, the integration flexibility, the deployment instructions—this is a system designed to be embedded into other services.

Whether intentionally or not, he’s created infrastructure for a world where financial planning tools are as ubiquitous and accessible as weather forecasts. The question is whether that world is one we want to live in.

The Percentile Outcomes

The simulation returns seven percentile outcomes: p5, p10, p25, p50, p75, p90, p95.

For the retirement goal demo: $1.52M (5th), $1.65M (10th), $1.89M (25th), $2.145M (median), $2.42M (75th), $2.85M (90th), $3.1M (95th).

Here’s what this means: if you run your exact scenario in a thousand parallel universes, in 50 of them you end up with less than $1.52 million. In 950 of them, you end up with more than $3.1 million. The median outcome—the middle of all possibilities—is $2.145 million, or 43% more than your $1.5 million target.

The system considers this a success. Success probability: 100%. The recommendation: “✅ GOOD: 100% probability of achieving all goals.”

But look at that range: $1.52M to $3.1M. That’s a $1.58 million spread. That’s more than your entire target amount. That’s the difference between comfortable retirement and generational wealth. That’s the difference between downsizing at 65 and buying a vacation home.

The traditional financial advisor would spend time explaining that range. Discussing sequence-of-returns risk. Exploring what might cause the bottom 5% scenario versus the top 5% scenario. Helping you understand that “100% success probability” doesn’t mean “guaranteed $2.145 million”—it means “999 out of 1,000 simulations hit $1.5 million or higher.”

Mycroft just shows you the chart. The interpretation is up to you.

This is the deal: democratized access to sophisticated financial modeling, coupled with the responsibility to understand what the numbers actually mean. No gatekeepers. No intermediaries. Also: no hand-holding. No explainers. No verification that you understand the difference between a median and a guarantee.

The Two-Second Promise

Goal extraction processing time: 2,340.5 milliseconds.

That’s how long it takes for natural language to become structured financial data. Two and a third seconds. Fast enough that it feels instantaneous. Slow enough that you can see the system thinking.

In those 2.34 seconds:

  • Pydantic validates your input

  • The Ollama health check confirms the LLM is running

  • The extraction prompt gets built with few-shot examples

  • llama3.1 processes your sentence

  • JSON gets parsed from the LLM’s response

  • Goals get validated against schemas

  • Entities get extracted (amounts, dates, keywords)

  • Confidence scores get assigned

  • The structured response gets returned

What’s missing from that list: human judgment. Financial expertise. Contextual understanding of your specific situation. Knowledge of your actual risk tolerance beyond what’s inferrable from a single sentence. Awareness of your other assets, debts, obligations, or goals not mentioned in this prompt.

The system makes reasonable assumptions. It uses industry standards. It applies historical data. It generates statistically valid projections. What it doesn’t do—what it can’t do—is know you.

This is the fundamental limitation of any automated financial planning system. It can process the information you give it with perfect consistency. It can’t tell you whether you’ve given it the right information.

You type: “I want to retire in 20 years with $1.5 million and I can save $5,000 monthly.”

The system doesn’t ask: Is $1.5 million enough? Have you accounted for healthcare costs? What about your current debt? What if you lose your job? What if your industry collapses? What if you have kids? What if your parents need care? What if inflation averages 6% instead of 3%? What if the next 20 years look nothing like the last 70?

It just calculates. And in 2.34 seconds, gives you an answer with 85% confidence.

The Recommendation Engine

When the simulation shows a 0% success probability, the system generates recommendations. In the demo: “Increase contribution by $3,500.”

Simple. Actionable. Mathematically correct. Also: potentially impossible.

Maybe you don’t have an extra $3,500 per month. Maybe you’re already stretching to save what you’re saving. Maybe increasing contribution means cutting expenses you can’t cut, or earning income you can’t earn, or taking risks you can’t take.

The algorithm doesn’t know. It just knows the math doesn’t work at your current contribution level, and it knows what contribution level would make the math work. The gap between those two numbers is your problem to solve.

This is what happens when you remove humans from the advisory loop. You get perfect mathematical optimization without contextual reasonableness checks. You get technically correct recommendations that may be practically impossible. You get a system that treats “increase your savings by 70%” as equivalent to “shift your allocation from 70% stocks to 75% stocks” because both are just parameter adjustments.

A human advisor would ask how you might increase that contribution. Would probe whether it’s realistic. Would explore alternatives. Would maybe suggest extending the timeline instead of increasing the contribution, or reducing the target instead of increasing the savings, or accepting a lower success probability instead of demanding certainty.

Mycroft doesn’t ask. It calculates what works, and tells you what works, and leaves the question of feasibility entirely to you.

The Sentence You Type

Let’s return to where we started. You open your laptop. You type a sentence about your financial future.

What happens next depends entirely on whether you understand what you’re asking for.

If you think you’re asking an AI to tell you what to do, you’re going to be disappointed when markets don’t follow historical patterns, when your life circumstances change, when the 5th percentile outcome becomes your actual outcome.

If you think you’re using a sophisticated computational tool to model possible futures based on historical data and probabilistic simulation, you might find it tremendously useful for exploring scenarios and understanding tradeoffs.

The system doesn’t distinguish between these two interpretations. It processes your input the same way regardless. The interface doesn’t know whether you’re a sophisticated investor doing sensitivity analysis or someone who’s never opened a brokerage account hoping an algorithm will solve their retirement anxiety.

Mycroft runs 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations. It fetches real market data. It applies proper statistical methods. It generates valid probability distributions. It does all of this correctly, efficiently, and reliably.

What it doesn’t do is ensure you understand what any of it means.

And maybe that’s appropriate. Maybe financial tools should be neutral—capable of sophisticated analysis, available to anyone, agnostic about how they’re used. Maybe the alternative—restricting access, requiring credentials, imposing barriers—does more harm than good by keeping powerful planning tools in the hands of those who can afford professional advice.

Or maybe there’s a reason financial advice is regulated. Maybe there’s wisdom in requiring human advisors to be licensed, insured, and bound by fiduciary duty. Maybe the speed and accessibility that makes Mycroft powerful is exactly what makes it dangerous.

The algorithm can’t answer that question. It just runs the simulation and returns the results.

The rest is up to you.
