What We’re Looking At

The spreadsheet in front of you represents something that didn’t exist before: a merged intersection of three U.S. government databases that were never designed to talk to each other. Column A is the company name. Column B tells you what they do—biotech, software, medical devices, AI. Column C is their funding stage: Seed, Series A, Series B, sometimes Series D+. Column D is how much money they raised most recently. Column E is when they raised it. Columns F through M contain their address, phone number, founding year, executive officers, board members.

Then the columns split. For 1,558 companies—5.1% of the total—you’ll see H-1B approval rates, median salaries offered to visa holders, top job titles they’ve sponsored, total approvals, total denials. These are the companies that show up in the Department of Labor’s LCA disclosure data and the USCIS H-1B Employer Data Hub. They have a documented track record. You know they sponsor because the government published the receipts.

For the other 28,800 companies, those columns are blank. Null values. No H-1B history. No trace in the visa databases. Which means one of four things: they’ve never hired an international candidate, they hired through a subsidiary we couldn’t match, they sponsor other visa categories we’re not tracking, or they just raised their Series A six months ago and haven’t had time to build an immigration pipeline yet.

That last category is the point.

How This Got Built

We started with SEC Form D filings—the legal documents companies must file when they raise private capital. The SEC makes these public through their EDGAR API. We pulled every filing from the last 12 months, flattened the JSON into structured rows, and extracted the fields that matter: company name, industry, headquarters city and state, total capital raised, most recent funding amount and date, funding stage, executive names, board members. That gave us 27,876 companies with confirmed venture funding.

Then we went to the Department of Labor. The DOL publishes quarterly files containing every Labor Condition Application filed for H-1B, H-1B1, and E-3 visas. These records include employer name, job title, salary, and work location for each petition. We ingested every LCA from the past three years.

Then USCIS. The H-1B Employer Data Hub provides aggregated statistics at the company level: petitions filed, approved, denied, broken down by initial employment versus continuing employment. This gave us approval rates and sponsorship volume.

The hard part was matching. “Laminr Technologies, Inc.” in the SEC data might appear as “Laminr Technologies” in DOL records and “Laminr Tech Inc” in USCIS data. Legal names, DBAs, subsidiaries, typos, punctuation differences—entity resolution across three independent government databases is genuinely difficult. We used fuzzy matching and manual validation. The output is a 30,370-row CSV where each company gets one row, and the columns from different sources are merged where matches were found.

For the 2,494 companies without SEC funding data, they showed up in the DOL or USCIS files but we couldn’t find a corresponding Form D. Either they’re bootstrapped, foreign-owned, or raising through structures that don’t trigger a Form D filing. We kept them in the dataset because they have documented sponsorship activity, which means they’re relevant to international job seekers even if we can’t confirm their funding status.

Then we built a web scraper. For every company in the dataset, we generated probable URLs from their name, programmatically tested which ones resolve, and scraped the full text of every accessible public page. Company descriptions, team pages, product information, job listings, contact details. All stored as plain text, linked back to the company record. This gives you the ability to search not just by funding stage or location, but by keywords in the scraped content—”machine learning engineer,” “clinical trial coordinator,” “seeking PhD candidates.”

What You Can Do With It

Let’s say you’re a mechanical engineering PhD on OPT with 10 months left before you need to convert to H-1B. You’re in Boston. You want to work in medical devices or robotics. You open the CSV. Filter by city: Boston. Filter by industry: Biotechnology, Medical Devices, Other Technology. Filter by funding stage: Series A or Series B (companies with enough cash to hire but still small enough that you can talk to founders). Filter by funding recency: money raised in the last 6 months (they’re hiring right now).

You get 47 companies. None of them appear on LinkedIn’s “companies that sponsor H-1B” filter because that filter is built from historical data. These 47 companies have never sponsored before. But 18 of them raised between $10M and $50M in the last 90 days. They’re scaling. They need people. You pull their websites from the scraped data. You find founder emails. You write cold outreach explaining that you’re on OPT, which means they don’t need to sponsor you yet, and you have 1-3 years of work authorization before H-1B even becomes relevant.

You just bypassed every ATS that would have rejected you.

Or you’re a computer science master’s student who wants to work in AI. You don’t care about location. Filter by industry: Other Technology, Computers. Filter by funding: $5M+. Sort by funding date, newest first. Export the top 200. These are startups that just raised capital and are building their engineering teams. Some will have posted jobs on their websites. Some won’t have posted anything yet but are hiring through founder networks. You reach out.

Or you’re a biotech PhD whose advisor says “only Moderna and Pfizer sponsor.” Filter for Biotechnology companies with Series B+ funding in the past 12 months. You get 127 companies you’ve never heard of. Cross-reference with the H-1B approval data—some of them have sponsored before, but they’re not famous enough for your advisor to know about them. Others are blank. No history. That doesn’t mean they won’t. It means you’re the first to ask.

What This Reveals

The structural problem isn’t that startups won’t sponsor. It’s that they don’t know they should. 75% of employers don’t understand OPT rules. They think “requires visa sponsorship” means “we have to pay legal fees and file paperwork immediately.” They don’t realize that F-1 OPT holders are work-authorized for 12-36 months before H-1B becomes necessary, and that employers who hire OPT students are exempt from FICA taxes, which saves roughly 7.65% in payroll costs.

The result: international students apply to the same 100 big companies with established immigration pipelines, while thousands of funded startups that desperately need talent sit on the sidelines because no one told them hiring an international candidate is actually cheaper than hiring domestically for the first 1-3 years.

You’re going through the human.

The Gaps

The data is only as fresh as the last time we ran the pipeline. SEC filings, DOL records, and USCIS stats are all published on their own schedules. Right now this is a point-in-time snapshot from early February 2025. Companies’ hiring needs change. Funding status changes. Contact information goes stale. We’re building an automated refresh pipeline to re-scrape and re-merge every week, but that’s infrastructure work that hasn’t happened yet.

Entity matching is imperfect. We got most of it right. We probably missed some. False negatives mean companies that should be matched across datasets aren’t, so their H-1B data doesn’t show up even though it exists. False positives mean we linked the wrong records. We’re working on secondary identifiers like EIN (Employer Identification Number) to improve accuracy, but EINs aren’t consistently available across all three sources.

The null values are ambiguous. 28,800 companies with no H-1B history could mean 28,800 companies that have never considered international candidates, or 28,800 companies that hire through subsidiaries, or 28,800 companies that are too new to have filed LCAs yet. You can’t tell from the data alone. That ambiguity is both the dataset’s greatest limitation and its greatest opportunity—these are companies no one else is looking at.

And a CSV with 30,370 rows is not a usable tool for most job seekers. You need technical proficiency with spreadsheet software or database tools to filter and query it effectively. We’re building a web interface—a searchable platform where you can filter by location, industry, funding stage, sponsorship history, and keyword-match against scraped job listings—but that doesn’t exist yet. Right now, this is a dataset for people who know how to use datasets.

What Happens Next

We’re running manual validation. One team member is using the CSV to identify companies, verify their information, and attempt real outreach. This human-in-the-loop testing will tell us whether the data actually leads to employment conversations or whether we’ve built an elegant spreadsheet that doesn’t help anyone.

The goal is to move from “CSV you have to download and manipulate” to “website where you type ‘Boston biotech Series A’ and get a filtered list with one click.”

We’re establishing a refresh cadence. The data needs to update weekly to stay relevant. That means automating the SEC scraping, DOL ingestion, USCIS sync, entity matching, and website re-scraping. It’s not a nice-to-have. It’s core infrastructure.

And we’re defining success metrics. The ultimate goal is facilitating one employment match that preserves someone’s visa status within 80 days. But that’s a lagging indicator. We need leading indicators: how many people download the data, how many companies they reach out to, what the response rate is from employers, whether the contact information is accurate. These metrics will tell us by Day 20 or Day 40 whether we’re on track, not just at Day 80 when it’s too late to course-correct.

What’s Inside

This dataset combines three U.S. government data sources that were never designed to talk to each other:

SEC Form D Filings (27,876 companies with funding data)

  • Company firmographics: name, industry, headquarters location

  • Funding indicators: total capital raised, most recent round amount and date

  • Funding stage: Seed, Series A, Series B, Series C, Series D+

  • Leadership: executive officers and board members

  • Contact information: phone, year incorporated, company age

DOL LCA Disclosure Data (H-1B, H-1B1, E-3 applications)

  • Historical visa sponsorship records

  • Job titles sponsored

  • Salary ranges offered to visa holders

  • Work locations

USCIS H-1B Employer Data Hub (1,558 companies with sponsorship history)

  • H-1B approval rates (initial vs. continuing employment)

  • Total approvals and denials

  • Median salaries

  • Top sponsored job titles

Web-Scraped Content (30,370 company websites)

  • Full text from all accessible public pages

  • Company descriptions, team pages, product information

  • Job listings not posted to major job boards

  • Contact details and founder information

The Problem This Data Solves

The employment landscape for international students and skilled workers in the U.S. is defined by information asymmetry. Research shows:

  • Only 44.6% of international students secure employment after graduation vs. 62.1% of domestic students (same degrees, same schools)

  • International students submit 2x as many applications (45 vs. 22 average) but receive 30% fewer offers

  • 75% of U.S. employers don’t understand OPT/CPT work authorization rules

  • Traditional job platforms filter by historical H-1B sponsorship data, making recently funded startups invisible

The structural mismatch: Thousands of venture-backed startups have fresh capital, urgent hiring needs, and resources to sponsor visas—but zero visibility to international candidates because they’ve never appeared in visa databases.

What this dataset reveals: The 28,800 companies with funding but no H-1B history represent an enormous untapped pool of potential employers whose sponsorship capacity has never been surfaced to the candidates who need it most.

Data Collection Methodology

SEC EDGAR API

We extracted Form D filings from the SEC’s public EDGAR database using their REST API. Form D is required when companies raise capital through private offerings (Regulation D exemptions). We parsed the XML/JSON responses to extract:

  • Company identification (CIK, entity name, address)

  • Offering details (amount, date, exemption claimed)

  • Related persons (executives, directors, promoters)

  • Industry classification (manual categorization from filer descriptions)

Limitations: Form D is self-reported and not all private funding requires filing. Foreign companies, bootstrapped startups, and certain exempted transactions may not appear.

DOL LCA Disclosure Data

The Department of Labor publishes quarterly CSV files containing every Labor Condition Application filed for H-1B, H-1B1, and E-3 visa categories. We downloaded the most recent 12 quarters and extracted:

  • Employer name and location

  • Job title and SOC code

  • Prevailing wage and actual wage offered

  • Employment start date and duration

  • Case status (certified, denied, withdrawn)

Limitations: Employer names in DOL data are not standardized. “Company X Inc.” may appear as “Company X” or “CompanyX Inc.” Entity resolution is imperfect.

USCIS H-1B Employer Data Hub

USCIS provides aggregated employer-level statistics showing total petitions filed, approved, and denied, broken down by initial employment vs. continuing employment. We scraped the public data hub interface to extract:

  • Employer name (as recorded by USCIS)

  • Approval rates

  • Total approvals and denials

  • Petition volume by year

Limitations: Data is aggregated by fiscal year and may reflect corporate acquisitions, name changes, or subsidiary structures inconsistently.

Entity Resolution

The most technically challenging step: matching company names across three datasets with inconsistent naming conventions. Our approach:

  1. Fuzzy string matching (Levenshtein distance, token set ratio) with threshold tuning

  2. Address-based validation (city/state match for ambiguous names)

  3. Manual review of high-confidence and low-confidence matches

  4. Null preservation where no confident match exists (94.9% of records)

Known issues: Subsidiaries may not match parent companies. Legal name variations (”Inc.” vs. “Incorporated” vs. “LLC”) introduce errors. Estimated match precision: 85-90% for companies with H-1B data; unknown for null records.

Web Scraping

For each company with a valid website URL:

  1. Generated candidate URLs from company name

  2. Verified URL resolution programmatically

  3. Scraped all accessible public pages (robots.txt compliant)

  4. Extracted plain text content, stripping HTML/CSS/JS

  5. Stored as searchable text field linked to company record

Limitations: Dynamic content (JavaScript-rendered pages) may be incomplete. Paywalled or login-required content is not captured. Websites change frequently; scraped content reflects point-in-time snapshot.

Known Limitations & Biases

Temporal Lag: SEC filings may be submitted weeks after funding closes. DOL LCA data is published quarterly. USCIS stats are aggregated annually. The dataset reflects a lagging indicator, not real-time hiring.

Geographic Bias: Startups in major tech hubs (SF, NYC, Boston, Austin) are over-represented in venture funding data. Companies in smaller markets may be undercounted.

Industry Bias: Biotechnology, software, and fintech startups are more likely to raise venture capital and file Form D. Hardware, consumer goods, and service companies may be underrepresented.

Survivorship Bias: Companies that shut down, were acquired, or pivoted may still appear if they filed Form D during their operational period. No explicit “active/inactive” flag exists in the data.

Null Value Ambiguity: The 94.9% of companies with no H-1B history could mean (a) never sponsored, (b) too new to have filed LCAs, (c) hired through subsidiaries with different legal names, or (d) sponsor other visa types (O-1, L-1, etc.) not captured here. You cannot definitively conclude “will not sponsor” from a null value.

Entity Resolution Errors: False negatives (missed matches) mean some companies with H-1B data are incorrectly marked as null. False positives (incorrect matches) mean some companies are attributed data from a different employer with a similar name. We estimate 10-15% error rate.

Selection Bias for H-1B Data: Only companies that filed at least one LCA appear in DOL data. Companies that hire international workers on other visas (OPT, CPT, F-1, O-1) are invisible unless they also filed H-1B petitions.
