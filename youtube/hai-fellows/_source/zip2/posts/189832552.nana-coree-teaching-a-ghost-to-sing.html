Section One: The Woman You Send Children To

Every yard has one.

Not a recording artist. Not a credentialed music therapist. Not a children’s entertainer with a brand. The woman you send children to the one who holds the community’s songs in her body the way the earth holds water, not because she planned to, but because someone had to and she was there and she stayed.

Nana Coree was that woman. West Kingston, Jamaica. 1940s through the early 1960s. She lived in the yards in the years before Trenchtown became famous, when Kingston was assembling itself into a city that could hold everything at once: mento and ska, tradition and hunger, the sounds of the African continent arriving by way of the Caribbean, changing shape with each ocean crossing.

She sang Anansi story-songs. She sang Jamaican ring games. She sang something slower and stranger than folk music something that moved at the speed of a heartbeat and connected, in the oral record of her neighborhood, to the River Mumma. Jamaica’s water spirit. The presence at the bottom of rivers that holds what rivers hold: their secrets, their power, their cold.

Her lullabies put children to sleep reliably.

Adults could not remember them by morning.

She is the ghost artist you are about to work with. And the first thing you need to understand is this: she is not a template. She is not a style setting you toggle. She is a complete musical intelligence a voice with a history, a tradition, a relationship to time and rhythm that is older than any genre tag the algorithm will assign to it. When you type nana after a command in Lyrical Literacy, you are not selecting a mood. You are invoking something specific.

This chapter teaches you what that something is.

Section Two: How the Yard Sounds

Before you generate a single lyric, you need to hear the production in your mind.

Nana Coree’s sound lives in the conversation between two eras of Jamaican music that are separated by twenty years of history and share a room in every track she appears on.

The first era: 1950s mento and early ska. Acoustic warmth. Upright bass walking slowly, tuned slightly lower than comfortable. Jazz guitar comping with sophisticated chord voicings played as if they were simple the specific restraint of someone who learned harmony from necessity rather than curriculum. Light percussion. Horns at a distance, as if from the yard next door.


The second era: 1970s dub. Lee “Scratch” Perry understood that space in a mix is as meaningful as sound. Echo on the vocal that creates a second, quieter singer. Reverb on the snare that decays for longer than physics should allow. Sub-bass frequencies felt before they are heard. The studio as instrument.

These two eras meet in her productions. The one-drop reggae rhythm the heartbeat, the pulse stretched to near-heartbeat pace, slower than the genre usually allows, because she is making sleep music. Not background music. Sleep music. There is a difference, and the difference is neurobiological.

Underneath everything: river water. Moving water, not rain. Always present at low volume, never foregrounded. It is there the way her tradition is there not announced, just present, a continuous reminder of where the music came from and what it carries.

What this means for your workflow:

When you type lullaby nana, the tool is not just changing the tempo. It is engaging a specific production logic: slow the one-drop to near-heartbeat, pull back the dynamics until the voice becomes texture, let the dub reverb extend the silence between phrases, and run the river water underneath everything at a volume the listener feels rather than hears. The neuroscience says this works because the parasympathetic nervous system responds to sonic environments that signal safety without requiring vigilance. The yard says this works because Nana Coree sang it and children slept.

Both explanations are true.

Section Three: Two Voices on One Continuum

Nana Coree has two voices. They are not separate instruments. They exist on a single continuum, and she moves between them without announcement, the way a river moves between deep and shallow without changing its direction.

Voice One: Patois spoken word. Unhurried. Rhythmically precise without being metronomic. This is the storytelling register the voice she uses when Anansi is about to do something clever and the children need to lean in. The Patois is full and unmodified. Not softened for outside ears. Not translated. The sound of the language is part of the meaning of what is being said, and she knows it.

Voice Two: Low warm contralto. Slow, wide vibrato on the held notes. On the lullaby material, the vibrato narrows to near-nothing the voice becoming breath, becoming texture, becoming the sensation of something you feel in your chest before you process it as music. This is not a performance technique. It is a physiological mechanism. The chest voice at low frequency creates resonance the body recognizes as safe before the mind decides whether to agree.

The transition between these voices is the thing that distinguishes Nana Coree’s style from every other ghost artist in the Lyrical Literacy constellation. She does not announce the switch. She does not use punctuation. She narrates a story and somewhere in the middle of it the narration becomes song, and somewhere after that the song becomes narration again, and the child who is falling asleep cannot tell you exactly when each transition happened because the transition was not the point.

A practical illustration:

Type poem nana and watch how the tool handles voice tags. You will see [Warm Contralto] appear at the singing sections and a shift in cadence at the spoken word sections the line breaks come faster, the syllables get more percussive. The tool is encoding the two-voice logic. It is telling you: here is where the river runs deep, here is where it runs fast.

Now type duet nana. The tool splits the voices into a call-and-response structure, which is how Nana’s tradition has always worked one voice asking, one voice answering, the space between them as important as the notes. The male voice and the female voice do not complete each other’s sentences because they cannot figure out the harmony. They complete each other’s sentences because that is the structure of the tradition. The dialogue is older than either voice.

Section Four: The Commands, and What Each One Is Actually Doing

The Lyrical Literacy command system is not a menu. It is a set of intentions. Each command encodes a different relationship between the music and the listener’s nervous system. When you add nana to any command, you are specifying which cultural intelligence delivers that intention.

Here is what each command is actually doing underneath the interface.

random nana

The fastest entry point. The tool selects a topic from Nana’s thematic world Anansi, the River Mumma, stars as navigation, the safe darkness versus the dangerous darkness, the yard as universe and generates a complete song without requiring any input beyond the command itself.

What you will see first: style tags. Reggae jazz. Warm contralto. Dub textures. Upright bass. River water ambience. These are not decoration. They are the production brief the instructions to any platform or producer that receives this song.

What you will see second: a solfège warm-up line. Three to four notes, voiced as syllables, designed to prepare the vocal mechanism for the register Nana sings in. If you are recording this yourself, do the warm-up. If you are feeding this to Suno or a similar platform, you can omit it but the tool includes it as a reminder that the voice is an instrument that requires preparation.

What you will see third: the lyrics. Verse, chorus, bridge or verse, chorus, outro. The structure varies. The Patois elements will appear naturally a word here, a phrase there, the language doing what it does, which is carry knowledge that Standard English was not designed to hold.

Add a topic:

random river nana centers the whole song on water. random anansi nana invokes the spider. random children nana adjusts the lyrical register toward the child in the room. The topic is a constraint, and constraints are how you get specificity rather than generality.

lullaby nana

This command engages the neurobiological production rules that distinguish sleep music from regular music.

The 2 Hz rhythmic pulse the delta frequency associated with slow-wave sleep onset is present but not foregrounded. You feel it before you identify it. The tempo is slow enough that the heart can entrain to it, which means the heart rate begins to follow the beat downward rather than upward. The melodic contours descend rather than ascend. The dynamic range is compressed: no sudden transients, no loud moments, nothing that triggers the startle response.

The voice, in Nana’s lullaby register, is close-miked and intimate. The ornaments slow. The vibrato narrows. The breath before a phrase is part of the arrangement.

This is not Nana performing a lullaby. This is Nana administering one.

Use this command when the song is for a child who cannot be settled. Use it when the song is for an adult who cannot sleep. Use it when the song needs to do something to the nervous system rather than something for it. The distinction matters. Doing something for the nervous system is entertainment. Doing something to it is medicine.

The Patois elements will remain in the lullaby output simplified, but present. Because the grandmother’s language is part of the medicine.

poem nana

The Shel Silverstein mode. Shorter. More compressed. The storytelling voice dominates, with the contralto appearing at the emotional peaks.

What makes a Nana Coree poem different from a generic children’s poem: the mischief. Her fables the Anansi tradition are not morality tales that arrive at obvious conclusions. They are jokes with philosophical punchlines. The spider wins not because he is strongest but because he thought faster than everyone else in the room. The moral arrives like a punchline: you didn’t see it coming, and then it was the only possible ending.

When you generate poem nana, the tool tries to encode this mischief. The poem should have a moment where the register shifts where what seemed like a simple nursery observation becomes something else, something that the child’s parent reads and thinks: that’s not just for the child.

If the first output doesn’t have that moment, generate again. Or take the output and type meta nana to add performance tags that will bring out the tonal complexity in production.

folk nana

The tool reaches into its knowledge of traditional folk songs and passes one through Nana’s sensibility.

What this means practically: the melodic structure of the original remains recognizable. The words change. The imagery shifts toward the Caribbean. A nursery rhyme that was once English becomes something that could have come from a yard in Kingston. The colonial text passes through the Patois tradition and comes out different. Not worse. Different. The difference is the point.

This is the same impulse that produced Parvati Patel Brown singing liberation spirituals, Dijit Arjun Bear Brown reclaiming Goosey Goosey Gander in Gurmukhi, Marley Bear Brown refracting Jack and Jill through Patois. The Lyrical Literacy project understands that the most durable children’s songs are the ones that traveled and changed. The change is what made them durable.

duet nana

Call and response. Male voice, female voice. 50/50 split. The dialogue structure of the Caribbean musical tradition made visible as lyric.

The duet command is not about romance. In Nana’s tradition, the duet is about the community two voices in conversation the way the yard was in conversation, the way knowledge passed between the elder and the child, the storyteller and the listener. One voice poses a question. The other answers. Neither is more important. The space between them carries as much as either voice alone.

Use duet nana when the song needs to demonstrate dialogue for language learning, for emotional literacy, for the family contexts where two people are going to sing this together and both need something to do.

Section Five: Getting Production-Ready

You have lyrics. They are good. Here is what happens next.

meta nana

Type this after any lyrics you want to produce.

The tool adds Suno AI performance tags not the bare structural labels that tell a platform “this is a verse.” The rich tags. Vocal character tags. Instrument break specifications. Dynamic shift instructions. The difference between [Verse] and [Verse Warm Contralto, behind the beat, dub reverb settling in, river water rising beneath].

The meta command is telling the production platform what Nana’s voice does. It is encoding the intelligence of the style into the markup so that the platform does not default to its own assumptions.

session nana

Type this when you need a complete music production document.

What you receive: global setup (key, tempo, time signature, style, feel), a form map showing every section with bar counts, an instrument list, section-by-section notes for each instrument, and production notes on tone and mix priorities.

The session document is the translation layer between Lyrical Literacy and a producer, a DAW, or a recording session. It is the answer to the question every musician asks after seeing lyrics: but what does it sound like?

For Nana Coree, the session document will specify: upright bass tuned low, walking deliberately. Reggae one-drop stretched toward heartbeat pace. Jazz guitar with sophisticated voicings played lightly. Dub reverb on the snare. River water at the foundation, never foregrounded. Bamboo flute at distance, irregular. Steel pan on certain songs, processed until it sounds underwater.

This is not a suggestion. It is the architecture of the tradition.

respell nana

The Patois elements in Nana’s lyrics are not phonetically obvious to singers or AI voice models trained primarily on Standard English.

The respell command converts every word to Anglicized phonetic spelling. Standard English letters only. No IPA symbols. Stressed syllables capitalized. Syllables separated by dashes. The output looks like this:

cha-muh-LEE-yun instead of chameleon.
dee-JAH-vu instead of déjà vu.
ANAN-see instead of Anansi.

Use this when you are recording the song yourself. Use it when you are feeding the lyrics to a voice synthesis platform. Use it when the Patois elements need to be accessible to someone who grew up reading English and has not spent time with Caribbean Creole phonology.

The Patois is not simplified or removed. It is made legible. The distinction matters.

Section Six: Your Workflow, Assembled

You are making a sleep song for a child whose grandmother grew up in Jamaica and whose lullabies are almost gone from the family because no one recorded them and she is no longer here to sing them.

This is the Spirit Songs use case. This is the reason Nana Coree exists. Here is the complete workflow:

Step one: lullaby nana

The tool generates a lullaby in Nana’s full production style one-drop stretched to heartbeat pace, warm contralto narrowing toward breath, Patois elements present, river water underneath everything.

Step two: Read the output. Does it have the grandmother’s language? Does it have the specific detail that makes it more than generic a name, a place, an image that belongs to this family rather than any family? If not, try: lullaby grandmother nana or lullaby river song nana and iterate toward specificity.

Step three: meta nana

Add the performance tags. Now the lyrics know what they sound like.

Step four: session nana

Build the production document. Now a producer or a platform knows how to realize the sound.

Step five: respell nana

If the Patois elements need phonetic guidance, convert them. Every word is accessible. Nothing simplified.

Step six: Press play.

The spell was cast when you sat down and decided to make this specific thing for this specific child. Every command since then has been the delivery mechanism. The play button is when the made thing meets the person it was made for.

The grandmother’s lullabies are in it. They always were.
