1. Executive Summary

The “80 Days to Stay” initiative is a data-driven effort by Humanitarians AI to address a critical information gap facing international students and skilled workers on OPT and H-1B visas in the United States. The project’s central thesis is that thousands of recently funded startups have both the financial capacity and the hiring appetite to sponsor international talent, but remain invisible to visa holders due to structural information asymmetry. By combining publicly available data from the SEC, the Department of Labor (DOL), and USCIS, the project aims to surface these hidden employment opportunities and connect them directly with candidates who need them most.

2. Problem Statement and Mission Context

The employment landscape for international students in the United States is defined by a paradox. These students graduate from the same institutions, hold the same degrees, and engage more actively with career services than their domestic peers - yet they face dramatically worse outcomes. Research from NAFSA, Interstride, and IZA indicates that only 44.6% of international students secure employment after graduation, compared to 62.1% of domestic students. International students submit roughly twice as many job applications (45 versus 22 on average) but receive 30% fewer offers and land full-time roles at a rate of only 38.1%, compared to 53.1% for domestic graduates.

This gap is not a reflection of skill or effort. It is a product of employer misconceptions about visa sponsorship, automated applicant tracking systems that filter out anyone who indicates a sponsorship need, and a general lack of immigration knowledge within startup HR departments. The irony is striking: students on F-1 OPT status require no employer sponsorship at all, and employers who hire them are actually exempt from FICA payroll taxes (a savings of approximately 7.65%), yet roughly 75% of employers do not understand OPT or CPT work authorization rules. The result is that international talent is funneled toward the same small set of large technology companies with established immigration pipelines, while thousands of funded startups - companies that just raised millions in capital and are actively building their teams - remain completely off the radar.

The “80 Days to Stay” project exists to close this gap. By transforming freely available government filings into a searchable, actionable dataset, the team is making funded startups visible to international candidates for the first time. The project’s success criterion is concrete and human: facilitate at least one successful employment match that preserves a person’s visa status within 80 days.

4. Technical Architecture and Data Pipeline

The project’s data pipeline draws from three authoritative U.S. government data sources and enriches the results through targeted web scraping. The entire pipeline runs on free, open-source tooling with effectively zero infrastructure cost.

4.1 Data Sources

SEC EDGAR (Securities and Exchange Commission). The foundation of the dataset comes from SEC Form D filings, which companies are required to submit when they raise capital through private offerings. These filings are publicly accessible through the SEC’s EDGAR API at no cost. The team extracted and flattened the master SEC JSON data into a structured CSV format (SEC_data_flattened.csv), capturing firmographic details such as company name, industry classification, headquarters location, executive officers, and board members, alongside funding indicators including total capital raised, latest funding amount, funding date, and funding stage (Seed, Series A, Series B, and so on).

DOL LCA Disclosure Data (Department of Labor). The DOL publishes quarterly disclosure files containing every Labor Condition Application filed for H-1B, H-1B1, and E-3 visa categories. These records include the employer name, job title, offered salary, and worksite location for each petition. The team ingested this data to identify which companies in the SEC dataset have a documented history of filing for visa-based employment.

USCIS H-1B Employer Data Hub. USCIS publishes aggregated employer-level statistics showing the number of H-1B petitions filed, approved, and denied, broken down by initial employment versus continuing employment. This data allowed the team to compute approval rates, total approvals, and total denials for each employer, providing a quantitative measure of sponsorship track record.

4.2 Web Scraping and Enrichment

Beyond the government datasets, the team built a web scraping pipeline to enrich company records with information from their public websites. The process works in two stages. First, probable website URLs were generated from company names and verified by programmatically hitting candidate links to identify which URLs resolve to actual, live websites. Second, the confirmed websites were scraped in full, and all publicly available content was stored in text format for each company. This scraped content captures information from every accessible public page on the domain, providing a rich source of supplementary data including company descriptions, team pages, product information, job listings, and contact details.

4.3 Data Mapping and Merge

The most technically demanding phase of the pipeline was the entity resolution and mapping step, in which the team joined records across the SEC, DOL, and USCIS datasets. Because these three sources use different naming conventions, formatting standards, and identifiers, the mapping required careful fuzzy matching and manual validation to ensure that “Company X” in an SEC filing was correctly linked to “Company X Inc.” in the DOL records and “Company X, Inc.” in the USCIS data. The final merged output (SEC_DOL_H1b_data_mapped.csv) contains 30,370 rows, with null values in sponsorship-related columns where no matching DOL or USCIS record was found - a deliberate design choice, since the absence of a sponsorship record does not mean a company will not or cannot sponsor, only that no public trace of prior sponsorship exists.

5. Dataset Overview and Key Metrics

The master dataset as of Day 08 contains 30,370 company records. The following metrics describe its composition and coverage.

Funding Data Coverage. Of the 30,370 total records, 27,876 companies (91.8%) carry funding data from SEC filings. This high coverage rate reflects the fact that the SEC Form D dataset forms the backbone of the pipeline. The remaining 2,494 records (8.2%) were likely sourced from the DOL or USCIS datasets where a corresponding SEC filing could not be matched, meaning these are employers with known visa sponsorship activity but without a confirmed private fundraising event in the SEC data.

H-1B Sponsorship History. Only 1,558 companies (5.1%) in the dataset have documented H-1B approval records from the USCIS Employer Data Hub. This is a pivotal statistic for the project’s thesis. It means that 94.9% of the companies in the dataset - nearly 28,800 funded employers - have no publicly documented history of H-1B sponsorship. These are precisely the companies the project seeks to surface: organizations with demonstrated financial capacity (via SEC funding data) that are invisible on traditional job platforms because they have never appeared in visa sponsorship databases. They are not necessarily unwilling to sponsor; they may simply have never been asked, never understood OPT rules, or never had international candidates in their applicant pools.

Key Data Fields. Each record in the merged CSV contains firmographic details (company name, industry, city, state, website), funding indicators (total funding, latest funding amount, latest funding date, latest funding stage), H-1B sponsorship history where available (approval rate, total approvals, total denials, median salary offered, top job titles sponsored), and supplementary information such as executive officers, board members, year of incorporation, company age, and contact details.

The dataset is structured so that users can filter along multiple dimensions simultaneously - for example, finding Boston-area companies in the biotech sector that raised more than $10 million in the past 12 months and have no prior sponsorship history (indicating an untapped opportunity) or filtering for companies with high approval rates and documented sponsorship of specific job titles like “Data Scientist” or “Research Associate.”

7. Risk Assessment and Challenges

Data Freshness. SEC Form D filings, DOL LCA records, and USCIS employer data are all published on their own schedules. The dataset reflects a point-in-time snapshot, and companies’ hiring needs, funding status, and sponsorship willingness can change rapidly. Without an automated refresh pipeline, the data will grow stale. Establishing a recurring ingestion cadence should be a near-term priority.

Entity Resolution Accuracy. Matching company names across three independent government databases is inherently error-prone. Variations in legal names, DBAs, subsidiaries, and spelling inconsistencies can lead to both false positives (incorrect matches) and false negatives (missed matches). The team should document the matching methodology and error rates to the extent possible, and consider integrating a secondary identifier such as EIN (Employer Identification Number) if available across datasets.

The “Null” Interpretation Problem. The 94.9% of companies without H-1B sponsorship history present both the project’s greatest opportunity and its greatest interpretive challenge. A null value could mean the company has never sponsored, has sponsored through a subsidiary under a different name, sponsors visa types not captured in the data, or has simply not yet had the occasion to sponsor. Communicating this nuance to end users - who may incorrectly assume null means “will not sponsor” - is critical.

Delivery Format Limitation. A raw CSV with over 30,000 rows is not an accessible tool for most job seekers. Without a frontend interface, the barrier to use is high. Users need either technical proficiency with spreadsheet software or guidance on how to filter and interpret the data. Moving to a web-based, searchable interface will be necessary for the project to achieve meaningful adoption beyond a handful of technically skilled testers.

Validation Dependency. The project’s ultimate success metric - facilitating an actual employment match - depends on variables outside the team’s control, including employer responsiveness, candidate qualifications, job market conditions, and visa processing timelines. Nidhi’s manual testing phase will provide early signal, but the team should prepare for the possibility that converting data into employment outcomes takes longer than anticipated.
