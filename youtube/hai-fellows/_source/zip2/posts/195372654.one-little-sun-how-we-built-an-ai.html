There’s a specific kind of satisfaction that comes from watching a child count on their fingers for the first time.

Not recite numbers count. Actually connect the word “three” to three real things in the world: three fingers, three birds, three treats on a plate. That moment of connection, abstract symbol meeting concrete reality, is one of the foundational leaps of early childhood. And for decades, music has been one of the most reliable ways to help children make it.

We built “Numbers” with that leap in mind.

Start With the Learning

Before we touched a single tool, we spent time with a simple question: how do children between three and five actually learn to count?

The research is consistent. Young children don’t anchor to numbers as abstract concepts they anchor to things. “Four” doesn’t mean much on its own. “Four yummy treats” means something a child can see, touch, and want. The concreteness is the point. Every verse in “Numbers” follows the same structure: name the number, pair it immediately with something familiar and physical.

One little sun in the sky so bright. Two little shoes, step left and right. Three little birds go tweet-tweet-tweet. Four yummy treats are fun to eat.

Sun, shoes, birds, treats. Each one is something a small child has already encountered in the world. The number arrives alongside something they already love or recognize, and the association sticks.

The second principle we built around is participation. The final chorus isn’t just a singalong it’s a physical invitation:

Let’s count, come along! Clap your hands and sing this song!

Children who clap, move, and mouth words while learning encode information differently than passive listeners. The body is part of the lesson. When we asked kids to clap through 1 to 10, we weren’t adding a fun flourish. We were adding a learning mechanism.

The third principle is rhythm as scaffolding. The repetitive, predictable structure of a counting song isn’t lazy songwriting it’s developmental design. Predictability builds cognitive confidence. When a child can anticipate what’s coming next, they’re not just following along; they’re beginning to lead. By verse three, most children aren’t waiting for the song to tell them what comes after eight. They already know.

Nine little stars begin to shine. Ten little toes yours and mine!

“Yours and mine” does something small but important there. It makes ten personal. It puts the child in the song.

The Aesthetic Choice

We made one design decision early that shaped everything that followed: “Numbers” would look handmade.

Not polished. Not slick. Not the kind of high-contrast, hyper-saturated 3D animation that dominates most children’s media. We wanted something that felt closer to a storybook soft clay textures, felted paper surfaces, pastel earthy tones, the kind of gentle visual warmth that doesn’t overstimulate a developing brain.

This was a deliberate choice about what young children need from a screen. A lot of children’s content is designed to grab attention. We wanted to hold it differently through comfort rather than stimulation. When a child sees the number “1” shaped like soft clay next to a smiling sun, the mathematical symbol stops being abstract. It becomes warm. It becomes safe. It becomes something worth returning to.

That aesthetic also guided the audio. We didn’t want an overwhelming synthetic beat. We went with acoustic guitar undertones, handclaps, and a clear vocal track the kind of sound that invites a child to sing along rather than just watch.

How We Built It

“Numbers” was produced using a modular AI pipeline four tools, each handling a distinct part of the production.

Lyrics and pedagogy first. We worked with a language model configured with early childhood learning principles to develop the lyric structure. The goal wasn’t just to write a catchy song. It was to ensure that every number paired with the right kind of concrete noun familiar, visual, appealing to a young child and that the rhythmic pattern stayed consistent enough to support repetition-based learning. The structure you hear in the final song reflects that process: predictable meter, simple vocabulary, physical imagery throughout.

Music via Suno AI. Once the lyrics were set, we used Suno AI to generate the musical arrangement. We experimented with several directions before landing on the folk-pop acoustic feel that fits the song. The priority was warmth and clarity a vocal track a child could follow and imitate, with instrumentation that supported rather than competed with the words. The turnaround was fast, and the quality reached a bar that would have required significant studio time to hit through traditional production.

Visuals via Midjourney and Runway. Achieving a consistent claymation aesthetic across ten different numerical frames is a real technical challenge. Each frame features different objects and characters a sun, shoes, birds, crayons, smiling children’s faces but they all need to feel like they came from the same world. We solved this by locking in a core set of style parameters that traveled through every image generation call: stop-motion claymation, felted paper textures, pastel earthy tones, soft studio lighting. That prompt consistency acted like a human art director, enforcing visual coherence across the whole piece.

The static images then moved into video generation, where subtle animation gentle camera pans, character blinks, rolling clouds brought the frames to life with the specific imperfections that make stop-motion feel tactile and real.

Final assembly. All the pieces audio, visuals, timing came together in post-production, where the synchronization that makes the video actually work for young viewers gets locked in. A beat that doesn’t land on the right visual, or a transition that runs a half-second off, breaks the immersive quality that makes educational content effective. The final product needed to feel seamless even though it was built from discrete parts by a lean team using tools that didn’t exist a few years ago.

Why This Matters Beyond One Song

“Numbers” is a proof of concept for something we think about a lot at Humanitarians.AI: what happens when the production barrier between a great educational idea and a polished, distributable piece of content effectively disappears?

High-quality early learning media has always been expensive to produce. Professional animators, composers, voice talent, curriculum consultants each layer of expertise adds cost, and those costs have historically meant that the best content flows toward the schools and families that can afford it. Underfunded classrooms get the functional minimum. The kind of content that makes a four-year-old light up beautiful, warm, carefully designed has largely been a product of budget.

Generative AI changes that equation in a real way. A teacher, a nonprofit, a parent building supplemental learning materials for their community: any of them can now produce content that approaches the quality of professionally made media. And they can adapt it. Change the language. Adjust the visuals to reflect the local environment. Include characters who look like the children in the room.

That last part matters more than it might seem. Educational content works best when children see themselves in it when the objects, characters, and settings feel familiar rather than imported. The “treats” in verse one don’t have to be the treats we chose. In a different classroom, in a different cultural context, they might be mango, rice cakes, or something else entirely. The pipeline we used for “Numbers” accommodates that. The warmth of the aesthetic travels across the adaptation.

We’re open-sourcing this workflow because we believe that’s how it creates the most value not as a finished product we distribute, but as a set of tools and methods that educators and creators can take, adapt, and make their own.

What’s Next

“Numbers” is the second piece in a series we’re building for early learners. “Colors” came first. Shapes, letters, and basic emotions are on the list.

Each one follows the same philosophy: start with the developmental science, build the aesthetic around what young children actually need, and use AI tools to produce it at a quality and cost that makes it genuinely accessible.

We’re also listening. If there’s a foundational concept you’d like to see tackled next something you’ve seen children struggle with, something a classroom near you needs drop it in the comments. The best version of this project is the one that’s built in conversation with the people closest to the learning.

The tools to make beautiful, effective early learning content are no longer reserved for large production budgets.

We’re just getting started.
