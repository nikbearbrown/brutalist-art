My work at Humanitarians AI and Homes of Hope India covers a lot of ground, research, strategy, platform planning, storytelling. One slice of that work is writing social media content for both organizations. Two audiences. Two completely different voices. I somewhat lean on AI

That sentence used to feel like a confession. It doesn’t anymore. Here’s what changed, and what I’ve learned about making AI actually useful instead of just fast.

When the output sounds wrong, the tool might be wrong.

I use Claude AI to draft content. When I first started, everything it gave me sounded the same, polished, professional, hollow. Like a press release wearing a smile. The kind of language organizations use when they’re performing seriousness instead of showing vulnerability.

For Humanitarians AI, that tone actually lands. We need credibility alongside the emotional pull. Claude’s natural register fits that lane. I still read every article, understand the context, and shape the output, but the starting point is usually solid.

For Homes of Hope India, though? Completely different story. That audience responds to personal storytelling. They need to feel like a real person is talking to them about another real person, not like an institution is reporting outcomes. I kept prompting Claude to be warmer, more emotional, more human. It would get closer. But it still sounded like a professional writer doing an impression of warmth.

That’s when I found Wilkes.

Wilkes changed everything

Wilkes is a narrative journalism tool in the Humanitarians AI ecosystem, built in the spirit of Paul Wilkes. It writes with intimacy and specificity. It trusts the reader to feel something without being told what to feel. No preaching. No appeals. Just the honest, specific detail.

The first time I ran a Homes of Hope story through Wilkes, the difference was immediate. The draft read like someone had been in the room, not summarizing a situation but showing it. The emotional core came through without a single line that begged the reader to care.

That was the breakthrough. Not that one tool is better than the other — but that different tools are built for different jobs, and the real skill is knowing which one to reach for.

AI is not one thing

Here’s the shift that changed how I work: I stopped thinking ofAI as a single tool I either use or don’t. It’s an ecosystem. Claude is excellent at structured, credibility-driven content. Wilkes is built for intimate, witness-based storytelling. They’re different instruments, and they sound different when you play them.

Once I understood that, I stopped trying to force one tool to do everything. The question moved from “how do I make this tool do what I want”to “which tool already does what I need?”

How I actually optimize AI output

This is the part most people skip over. Using AI well isn’t about finding the perfect prompt, it’s about what you do before and after the prompt.

Before you touch the tool:

  • Read the source material. All of it. If you’re writing about someone’s story, understand it deeply enough that you could tell it yourself without any tool at all.

  • Decide the tone before the tool does. Are you going for warmth? Authority? Urgency? If you don’t know, the AI will guess, and it’ll guess wrong.

  • Know your audience. A donor on LinkedIn and a first-time visitor scrolling Instagram need completely different entry points into the same story.

When you’re working with the output:

  • Listen for the wrong note. AI drafts often sound almost right, which is more dangerous than sounding completely wrong. Train yourself to catch the moment where polished language is covering for a lack of real feeling.

  • Don’t just edit — diagnose. When a draft feels off, ask yourself why. Is the tone wrong? Is the structure wrong? Or is the tool itself wrong for this job? That diagnosis is the skill.

  • Layer your tools. I’ll sometimes start a piece in one tool for the emotional core, then move to another to tighten the structure. The final version isn’t from any single AI, it’s from my judgment about what each one does best.

The prompt itself matters less than people think. A mediocre prompt given to the right tool with strong source material will outperform a brilliant prompt given to the wrong tool every time.

Match the channel to the job

This same thinking shaped my platform strategy this week. We currently focus on Substack and LinkedIn, but pure awareness, getting people to know Homes of Hope exists, needs broader reach. LinkedIn serves the professional and donor audience well. But Instagram, YouTube Shorts, and Facebook are where people spend their unguarded time, scrolling without an agenda. That’s where emotional storytelling lands hardest.

So the emerging strategy is a split: awareness-first content on discovery platforms, credibility - driven content on LinkedIn for the audience ready to act. Same principle as the tool question, match the channel to what it does best.

The bottom line

The narrative around AI usually falls into two camps: it’s going to replace everyone, or it’s a gimmick that produces generic slop. Neither is true. AI produces exactly what you’re equipped to ask for, and if you don’t understand the story, the audience, and the emotional stakes, no tool will save you.

The tools are powerful. But they are only as good as the person choosing which one to pick up and knowing when to put it down and pick up a different one.
