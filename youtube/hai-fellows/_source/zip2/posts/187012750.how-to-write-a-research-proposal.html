Most research proposals fail before anyone reads them.

Not because the idea is bad. Not because the reviewer is hostile. But because the writer treated the proposal as paperwork instead of thinking.

You answered the questions on the rubric. You hit the word count. You included citations. And yet, when someone finishes reading, they have no idea what you’re actually proposing to do, why it matters, or whether you can pull it off.

This guide exists because filling out a form is not the same as making a case.

If you’re writing a proposal for a class, a fellowship, a grant, or a project at Humanitarians AI—this template will force you to think clearly before you write a single word.

The Core Failure Mode (And Why Your Draft Probably Has It)

Here’s what most proposals look like:

“I will use [technique] to solve [problem] by building [system]. This is important because [generic claim about impact]. I will evaluate success using [standard metric].”

This isn’t a proposal. It’s a Mad Lib.

The problem? You’re describing what you’ll do without explaining:

  • Why existing solutions fail structurally (not just “nobody’s done this yet”)

  • What specific judgment call or decision your work will inform

  • How you’ll know if you’re wrong before you waste months building

  • What the smallest version is that could still prove something useful

A proposal is not a description of work. It’s an argument that the work is worth doing.

The difference between an AI tourist (someone who uses AI tools) and an AI native (someone who thinks architecturally about human-AI collaboration) is visible in the proposal. Tourists describe features. Natives demonstrate judgment.

What a Proposal Actually Needs to Do

A good proposal does three things:

1. Establishes Consequence

Someone, somewhere, will make a worse decision if this work doesn’t happen. Who? What decision? How much worse?

2. Demonstrates Necessity

Why won’t simpler approaches work? Not “nobody’s tried this”—but “here’s why obvious alternatives fail structurally.”

3. Shows Falsifiability

What’s the smallest version that could prove you wrong? If you can’t name how this could fail, you’re not doing research—you’re doing performance.

Everything else is commentary.

The Template: Seven Questions That Force Real Thinking

Question 1: What Happens If This Fails Silently?

Start here. Not with “the problem” or “the literature gap”—with the consequence.

Weak: “Poor data quality leads to bad model performance”
Strong: “When hospitals deploy sepsis prediction models trained on biased data, they miss 30% of at-risk patients in underrepresented demographics, leading to preventable deaths.”

Your turn:

“If my system produces garbage output, [specific person/organization] will [make what decision?] resulting in [quantified cost/harm].”

Red flags:

  • You can’t name a specific stakeholder

  • The cost is abstract (”inefficiency,” “suboptimal outcomes”)

  • The consequence is academic, not operational (”we won’t understand X as well”)

If you can’t write this sentence concretely, you don’t have a research question yet—you have a topic.

Why this requires advanced data science: A basic regression or simple prompt won’t work because [insert structural reason]. For instance, in wastewater epidemiology, pathogenic signals are sparse in noisy SERS data, environmental variables are confounded, and the stakes are public health decisions—not just prediction accuracy.

Question 2: Why Won’t the Obvious Solution Work?

Most reviewers are thinking: “Why not just [simple thing]?”

You need to preempt this. Not defensively—but by demonstrating structural inadequacy.

Template:

“Why not [obvious approach]? Because [structural reason it fails], evidenced by [example or prior work].”

Example (Boyle System):

“Why not just use GitHub commit logs?
Because commits document what changed in code, not why decisions were made or what failed. Under OPT compliance review, USCIS needs evidence of applied learning, not version control history.”

“Why not weekly status reports?
Because narratives are unfalsifiable. ‘I debugged the auth system’ could mean 2 hours or 20, could be reproducible or tribal knowledge. Reports optimize for sounding productive, not for enabling reproduction.”

Your turn:
List three “obvious” solutions. For each, explain the structural reason it fails (not just “it’s not as good”).

Question 3: Who Does What, and Why?

Every proposal should answer: What judgment requires a human, and what execution can be delegated?

This is Strategic Delegation—the thoughtful distribution of cognitive labor between human judgment and agentic tools.

If everything requires human judgment, you’re not using tools effectively.
If nothing requires human judgment, you’re not doing research—you’re automating.

Create a delegation matrix:

TaskHumanTool/AgentWhy This Division?Define success criteria✓—Requires domain judgment under uncertaintyCheck data completeness—✓Deterministic rule applicationInterpret anomaly significance✓—Context-dependent threshold judgment

Strategic Delegation involves:

  • Task Decomposition: Breaking complex problems into components optimized for AI processing

  • Metacognitive Monitoring: Continuously evaluating and adjusting based on AI performance

  • Platform Awareness: Developing accurate mental models of what AI can and cannot “know”

Red flags:

  • You can’t explain why the human is necessary for specific tasks

  • The tool is doing “magic” that you can’t operationalize

  • Every row says “Human + Tool” without clarification

If you don’t know what you’re delegating to the tool versus reserving for human judgment, you’re going to build something that doesn’t scale or doesn’t work.

Question 4: What’s Your Ground Truth, and How Do You Know It’s Not Contaminated?

Every proposal needs an operational definition of “correct.”

Weak: “We’ll evaluate model accuracy”
Strong: “Ground truth is manual clinical diagnosis from three independent physicians. Contamination risk: physicians may use the same flawed screening tool. Mitigation: audit disagreement cases for systematic bias.”

Template:

“Ground truth: [How do you define correct?]
Contamination risk: [What could make your ground truth wrong?]
Validation: [How will you check?]”

For reproducibility-focused proposals (like documentation systems):

“Ground truth: A documentation log is reproducible if a new contributor can re-execute the work with only the log + artifacts.
Contamination risk: Logs might include implicit knowledge or require access to private systems.
Validation: Blind reproduction test with someone who wasn’t there.”

Key dimensions of data quality:

  • Source Grounding: Every claim mapped to uploaded sources/artifacts

  • I/O Completeness: Inputs, transformations, outputs, lineage all preserved

  • Method Specificity: Steps are parameterized, not vague descriptions

  • Failure Capture: Attempted paths and errors preserved, not just successes

Question 5: What’s the Silent Failure Scenario?

This is the most important question and the one most proposals skip entirely.

Silent failure: Your system produces confident output that’s based on flawed assumptions, and nobody notices until it’s too late.

This is where computational skepticism matters. Your proposal should show you understand that validation is the art of being “rigorously uncertain.”

Template:

“Silent failure: [Which component] produces [confident but wrong output] because [underlying issue]. Detection: [How will you catch it?] Mitigation: [Architectural constraint or validation step].”

Examples:

Healthcare AI:
“Silent failure: Model predicts low risk for a patient who’s actually high-risk because training data underrepresented their demographic. Detection: Stratified performance monitoring by subgroup. Mitigation: Require minimum performance thresholds per demographic before deployment.”

Investment intelligence:
“Silent failure: Model identifies a pattern that exists only due to temporary market anomaly (correlation-not-causation). Detection: Adversarial validation using agents designed to find conditions where model confidence is unjustified. Mitigation: Contrastive inquiry that distinguishes measurement from causation.”

Data pipeline:
“Silent failure: Pipeline reports ‘data quality improved’ because null values decreased, but nulls were replaced with mean imputation—destroying real variance. Detection: Track not just null %, but distribution shifts pre/post-cleaning. Mitigation: Require explicit approval for any imputation strategy.”

Documentation system:
“Silent failure: NotebookLM approves a log that cites ‘personal communication’ as source, making work unreproducible. Detection: Require all sources to be uploaded artifacts with checksums. Mitigation: Reject any log referencing external/unavailable sources.”

Your turn:
What’s the most plausible way your system confidently produces wrong output? If you can’t name it, you’re not ready to build.

Question 6: What’s the Dumbest Version That Could Still Teach You Something?

Most proposals are over-scoped. You’re trying to solve the entire problem when you should be trying to learn the constraint.

The MVP question: What’s the smallest version that could prove you wrong?

Weak MVP: “Build the full pipeline with 3 datasets”
Strong MVP: “Single dataset, single transformation, manual validation. If I can’t reproduce my own work from my documentation, the schema is insufficient.”

Template:

“MVP: [Simplest version]
Falsification criteria: [What would prove this approach is wrong?]
Success: Not ‘it works’ but [what you learned about the constraint]”

Example:

“MVP: One volunteer, one week, one project. Coordinator attempts blind reproduction using only the log.
Falsification: If coordinator can’t reproduce the work, the logging schema is insufficient.
Success: We identify which schema fields are missing vs which are noise.”

Computational realism: Will this run on a standard laptop/Colab, or does it require specialized scaling? For cold chain monitoring using LSTM/GRU networks, you might need edge computing. For wastewater epidemiology, you might need specific sensor access. Name these constraints.

Red flags:

  • Your MVP takes more than 1/3 of your available time

  • You can’t name a falsification condition

  • Your MVP is “version 1.0” of the full system

If your MVP can’t fail, you’re not testing a hypothesis—you’re just building.

Question 7: What Could Go Wrong? (The Pre-Mortem)

Before you propose, do a pre-mortem: Assume the project failed. Why?

Not risks you’ll manage—things that could make the entire approach wrong.

Template:

“Pre-mortem: This could fail because [fundamental assumption] turns out to be false, evidenced by [what we’d observe].”

Examples:

“This could fail because NotebookLM’s outputs aren’t deterministic—different prompts or different runs might give different feedback, making it unreliable as a validator.”

“This could fail because volunteers might game the system—verbose logs that satisfy rubric requirements but still lack reproducible substance.”

“This could fail because the causal DAG I’m assuming is wrong—what looks like causation in observational data might be confounding, and my synthetic data won’t reveal it until production.”

Red flags:

  • All your “risks” are about timeline or resources

  • You don’t name any fundamental assumptions that could be false

  • Your mitigation is always “work harder” or “add more data”

If you can’t imagine how your core approach could be wrong, you’re either not thinking critically or you’re proposing something trivial.

Where Does This Fit? (Critical for Humanitarians AI Proposals)

Before you write a single word of your proposal, reach out to the Project Managers.

Why? Because you need to answer:

  1. Which existing framework does this serve?

  2. Is someone already working on something similar?

  3. What constraint does this remove for that framework?

Your work must map to one of the existing frameworks or justify why it’s novel infrastructure:

Intelligence & Analysis Frameworks

Mycroft (Investment Intelligence): If you’re working on specialized agents that resolve conflicts when data points contradict, conflict resolution in financial/strategic data, or ensemble decision-making under uncertainty.

Popper (Computational Skepticism): If your work prioritizes adversarial validation, breaking models rather than proving them, or systematic testing of where confidence is unjustified.

Madison (Marketing Intelligence): If you’re coordinating multi-layer agent systems, navigating gaps between technical optimization (CTR) and strategic objectives (brand health), or managing five-layer intelligence stacks.

Specialized Applied Research

Dayhoff (Bioinformatics/Public Health): If mistakes in your system have biological consequences, if you’re working with healthcare AI, or if you need different standards for testing and deployment due to patient safety.

RAMAN Effect (Wastewater Epidemiology): If you’re making high-stakes public health calls from noisy, probabilistic data, working with SERS pathogen detection, or dealing with environmental confounding.

Cyber-Physical Systems (Cold Chain/Pharmaceuticals): If you’re predicting failures before they occur (temperature drifts), using LSTM/GRU networks for reliability engineering, or working in domains where physical consequences matter.

Educational & Social Infrastructure

Dewey (Personalized Learning): If you’re exploring how to scale mentorship without losing adaptive expertise, building agentic frameworks for education, or balancing automation with human connection.

Lyrical Literacy (AI + Music for Learning): If you’re working on neuroscience-grounded approaches to memory retention, AI-generated educational content, or cognitive development through multimodal learning.

Botspeak (AI Literacy): If you’re teaching the “interface layer” between human intent and machine capability, prompt engineering, or foundational AI fluency.

Boyle System (Reproducibility Infrastructure): If your work is about documentation rigor, OPT compliance, reproducibility scoring, or making research methods traceable and intelligible.

If It’s Truly Novel

Explain why it’s horizontal infrastructure (serves multiple frameworks) rather than vertical (one-off project). For example, the Boyle System serves all frameworks because reproducibility is a compliance requirement, not a feature of any single framework.

Don’t guess. Ask. The PMs can tell you:

  • Which framework is the natural home for your work

  • Whether someone else is already building something similar

  • What documentation/validation standards apply to that framework

  • Whether your work creates genuine value or is duplicating effort

Common Traps (And How to Avoid Them)

Trap 1: Answering Questions Literally Instead of Holistically

Your proposal reads like a Q&A because you treated the rubric as a checklist.

Fix: Write a narrative first. Then check if it answers the required questions. Don’t start with the questions.

Trap 2: Confusing Sophistication with Quality

You added causal inference, synthetic data, ensemble methods, and adversarial validation because it sounds impressive.

Fix: Every complexity needs a structural justification. “Why is [technique] necessary?” should have a clear answer beyond “it’s more advanced.”

The quantile scoring recognizes this:

  • Bottom 25%: Standard datasets, minimal custom architecture

  • 26-50%: Solid implementation, clear use of AI principles

  • 51-75%: Strong technical logic, real-world application, rigorous validation

  • Top 25%: Innovative causal inference or synthetic data, professional deployment-ready

Moving from 50th to 75th percentile isn’t about adding more techniques—it’s about moving from behavioral labels to identifying specific causal pathways.

Trap 3: Hiding Uncertainty

You’re afraid to say “I don’t know yet” so you pretend you have it all figured out.

Fix: Reviewers trust proposals that name uncertainty explicitly. “This assumes X, which I’ll validate in the MVP” is stronger than pretending X is obviously true.

Remember: documented judgment under uncertainty is the only AI-proof asset. Uncertainty isn’t a weakness—it’s the point.

Trap 4: No Clear Relationship to the Larger Mission

You’re proposing work that could live anywhere. Why does it belong at Humanitarians AI? In your research group? In this fellowship?

Fix: After talking to the PM, name which existing framework/mission/infrastructure your work serves, and what constraint it removes. If it’s truly novel infrastructure, explain why it’s horizontal (serves multiple projects) vs vertical (one-off).

Trap 5: Performance Over Substance

Your proposal reads beautifully but doesn’t actually commit to anything falsifiable.

Fix: If your advisor/reviewer can’t tell after reading whether you succeeded or failed, you didn’t propose research—you proposed vibes.

The Self-Check (Before You Submit)

Answer these honestly:

  1. Can a skeptic understand the consequence?
    Read your opening paragraph to someone unfamiliar with your domain. Do they understand what bad thing happens if this work doesn’t exist?

  2. Did you preempt the obvious objections?
    List three “why not just...” questions. Did you answer them structurally, not just assert yours is better?

  3. Is the delegation clear?
    Can you explain in one sentence what requires human judgment vs what’s algorithmic?

  4. Can this fail?
    If you can’t name a falsification condition or a silent failure mode, revise.

  5. Is the MVP actually minimal?
    If it takes more than 1/3 of your time or can’t prove you wrong, it’s not an MVP—it’s the whole project.

  6. Did you name your assumptions?
    What has to be true for this to work? If you don’t know, you’re not ready.

  7. Did you talk to the PM?
    If you’re proposing for Humanitarians AI and haven’t confirmed which framework this serves or whether others are working on similar problems, stop and do that first.

If you can’t answer all seven, you’re not done thinking.

Your Story: Why Does This Problem Matter to You?

Here’s where most proposals become interchangeable: they solve important problems, but nobody knows why you’re the one solving it.

This isn’t about credentials or resume-padding. It’s about motivation sustainability. Projects driven by genuine care survive setbacks. Projects driven by optimization for grades or portfolio pieces collapse at the first real obstacle.

What “Personal Connection” Actually Means

Personal connection isn’t:

  • A trauma narrative designed to manipulate reviewers

  • A humble-brag about your expertise

  • A generic statement about “passion for helping people”

Personal connection is:

  • Domain insight you carry that shapes how you see the problem

  • A question you can’t stop asking even when no one is grading you

  • A gap you’ve witnessed that makes you angry or confused

  • An experience that taught you where current systems fail

Examples Across the Spectrum

Direct experience: “I watched my mother’s small business fail because she couldn’t get reliable market intelligence without paying for enterprise tools she couldn’t afford. When I learned about ensemble agent systems, I immediately thought: what if small businesses could access the same conflict-resolution intelligence that hedge funds use?”

Witnessed failure: “During my undergrad research assistantship, I saw three graduate students lose months of work because their documentation assumed tribal knowledge. When USCIS started questioning OPT renewals for ‘unverifiable’ research work, I realized the problem wasn’t just inconvenience—it was existential for international students.”

Principled commitment: “I’m not from a public health background, but I grew up in a community where early detection of disease outbreaks could have prevented deaths. When I learned about wastewater epidemiology, I was struck by how the limiting factor wasn’t the science—it was the ability to make confident calls from noisy data. That’s a problem I know how to think about.”

Intellectual curiosity: “I don’t have a personal story about investment fraud, but I’m fascinated by the epistemological problem: how do you know when to trust one data source over another when they contradict? That’s not a finance question—it’s a judgment question. And I want to understand how to architect systems that make those judgment calls explicit.”

What Makes a Connection Credible

Weak: “I’m passionate about healthcare and want to make a difference”
Strong: “I spent a summer coding for a clinic and realized they were making treatment decisions based on incomplete patient records because the EHR system had terrible data quality checks. I kept thinking: this is a validation problem, not a clinical problem.”

Weak: “AI ethics is important in today’s world”
Strong: “I ran an experiment where I asked NotebookLM to validate my code review, and it confidently approved code with a security vulnerability. That moment—where the AI sounded certain but was dangerously wrong—is what made me realize we need architectural constraints, not just better prompts.”

Weak: “I want to help international students”
Strong: “I’m on OPT. I’ve had to document my work for renewal. I know the exact moment you realize your Notion pages and Slack threads won’t survive USCIS scrutiny. That panic is what made me think: what would documentation designed for adversarial review actually look like?”

The “So What?” Test

After you write your personal connection, ask:

Does this story explain:

  • Why you’d keep working on this when it gets hard?

  • Why you’d notice failure modes others might miss?

  • Why you’re positioned to ask questions others aren’t asking?

If your story could be copy-pasted into any proposal in your field, it’s not a real connection—it’s a performance.

Building in Public: The Opportunity

If you can articulate why you care, your work becomes shareable. Not just as a portfolio piece, but as something that resonates with others who’ve witnessed the same failure or asked the same question.

At Humanitarians AI, high-quality work can be featured on the AI Skunkworks Substack or shared with industry networks (like The Erdős Institute). But only if there’s a story behind the work that makes it worth reading about.

The consent question: “Do you give permission for your project to be featured publicly to demonstrate your capability to industry leaders?”

This isn’t about self-promotion. It’s about building in public—showing your thinking, not just your results. The best opportunities don’t come from polished portfolios. They come from people seeing how you think through problems and saying, “I want that person on my team.”

When You Don’t Have a Personal Story

Maybe you stumbled into this problem. Maybe it was assigned. Maybe you don’t have lived experience with the domain.

That’s fine. Say that.

But then explain what happened when you started digging: “I didn’t care about supply chain monitoring until I learned that temperature drift in pharmaceutical transport causes $35B in waste annually. Then I got angry. Not because I have personal stakes, but because this is a solvable engineering problem that’s treated as inevitable.”

“I picked this project because it was available. But three weeks in, I realized the real problem isn’t technical—it’s that everyone optimizes for ‘looking productive’ instead of ‘being reproducible.’ Once I saw that pattern, I couldn’t unsee it.”

Honesty about how you arrived at the problem is the story. What matters is what you noticed once you started paying attention.

What Happens Next

The goal of a proposal isn’t to convince someone you have all the answers.

It’s to convince them you’re asking the right questions and have a falsifiable path to learning.

Good proposals get funded not because they’re safe, but because they’re honest about uncertainty while being rigorous about method.

Bad proposals get rejected not because the idea is wrong, but because the proposer hasn’t thought hard enough to know what could go wrong.

If you’ve worked through this template honestly—including why you personally care enough to see this through—you’re ready.

If you tried to skip questions or game the rubric, start over.

Your future self—three months into a project that isn’t working—will thank you for doing the hard thinking now.
