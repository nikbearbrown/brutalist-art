And every year, students send me a resume and expect me to somehow alchemize that into a narrative that convinces a selection committee you’re one of the 100 most distinguished students at Northeastern.

It doesn’t work like that.

But... those are my accomplishments?

They are! And they matter. But here’s what you need to understand about the Huntington 100 process: the selection committee is explicitly instructed to ignore “additional information and inferences” beyond what’s written in the nomination form itself.

That means if I can’t tell your story compellingly within those seven 500-word criteria sections and the 1,000-word “impressive experience” section, you don’t exist to them. They’re not going to Google you. They’re not going to look at your LinkedIn. They’re not going to give me the benefit of the doubt.

And I can’t write 3,500 words of compelling narrative from “GPA: 3.85, Study Abroad: Copenhagen, Leadership: Robotics Club President.”

Okay but what makes someone actually competitive for this?

The document the university gives nominators literally says the challenge is “the superficiality of the materials provided by students, who frequently submit a standard curriculum vitae in place of a nuanced personal narrative.”

They want narrative nonfiction. Think The New Yorker profiles. They want to know:

  • How your worldview shifted when you did that international co-op

  • The crisis moment when your leadership was actually tested

  • What you gave up to pursue that research project

  • The specific day when you realized you needed to found that organization

  • What it cost you personally to maintain your ideals

That sounds way more personal than a nomination form should be?

It is. But that’s what distinguishes you from the other 20 students with impressive GPAs, international co-ops, and leadership positions.

Let me show you what the seven criteria actually require:

What the Form Actually Asks (And What That Means)

1. Global Engagement (500 words)

Not: “I did a co-op in Berlin.”

Instead: “When I arrived in Berlin, I assumed German engineering culture’s obsession with documentation was bureaucracy. Three months in, I realized it was actually respect for the next person who’d touch the code. That changed how I write software and how I think about legacy.”

The document explicitly says they’re looking for how “the student’s worldview shifted” - passive observation doesn’t cut it.

2. Impact on Campus Community (500 words)

The committee wants a “before-and-after state.” What was broken? What did you fix? What infrastructure did you build that survives after you graduate?

Not: “I was president of the Robotics Club.”

Instead: “When I became president, we had 12 members and no budget. I spent a semester learning grant writing and convinced three departments to co-sponsor us by arguing robotics was interdisciplinary. Dean Martinez pushed back—she didn’t think Engineering should fund something involving the Arts. Here’s how I changed her mind...”

3. Record of Service (500 words)

Service at Northeastern isn’t volunteering - it’s “mission-driven” work that takes a project “from idea to implementation.” What problem were you solving? Why did it matter?

4. Demonstrated Leadership (500 words)

Leadership isn’t about titles. The committee defines it through “development and success of various initiatives.”

They want the crisis moment. The day the chair of your fundraiser got sick and you had to take over. The meeting where your team was falling apart and you had to hold it together. The decision you made that revealed your character.

5. Entrepreneurial or Innovative Spirit (500 words)

This isn’t about starting a company. It’s about “the ability to identify opportunities and mobilize resources in any context.”

What status quo did you challenge? What problem did you solve that everyone else accepted as unsolvable?

6. Academic Record (500 words)

High grades are expected, but they want to know how your “academic work informs your experiential work and vice versa.”

Did your research influence your co-op? Did your co-op change what you studied? Why did your work matter to your field?

7. Commitment to Ideals (500 words)

This is about your moral compass. They want to see you “maintained the highest ethical standards” in a challenging situation.

Ideals are revealed in moments of conflict. When did you advocate for someone? When did you turn down the easier path because it didn’t align with your values?

Okay, I’m dizzy. That’s 3,500 words just on criteria?

Plus the 1,000-word “most impressive experience” section. Plus the relationship description. Plus optional additional comments.

Yeah. It’s a lot.

The Real Problem: You Can’t Write This About Yourself

Student: So why don’t students just... write all this themselves and send it to you?

Because most people can’t narrate their own lives with this kind of specificity and vulnerability. You’re too close to it. You don’t know what details matter. You’re terrified of sounding arrogant or too humble or exposing failure.

That’s why I’m the writer. You’re the source.

But I need raw material that’s actually workable.

What to Send Me Instead of a CV

Answer these questions in paragraphs (not bullets) - like you’re talking to a friend who’s interviewing you for a magazine profile:

For each major experience you want me to potentially write about:

  1. The scene: Where were you when this started? What were you literally doing?

  2. The complication: What went wrong or got messy? What surprised you?

  3. Your decision: What choice did you make? What did you give up to make it?

  4. The conversation: Did someone say something that changed how you thought? What exactly did they say?

  5. What changed: How do you think or work differently now because of this experience?

For character/values:

  1. Competing priorities: When did you have to choose between two things you cared about?

  2. Being challenged: When did someone push back on you? Were they right?

  3. Meaningful failure: What’s something you failed at that actually mattered? Why?

  4. Misunderstanding: What do people get wrong about why you do what you do?

I need enough material that I can write something that makes the committee feel like they know you. CVs don’t do that.

No. The award is great. But the real value is learning to articulate your own experience with specificity. That’s a skill that will serve you long after graduation.

And frankly, too many interesting student stories get compressed into resume bullets and lost. This is a chance to preserve the actual texture of what you did.

Critical Logistics You Need to Know

Since I don’t run this program, here’s what you should know about how it actually works:

  • No self-nominations. You can’t apply directly. You need a nominator (faculty, mentor, co-op employer).

  • No resumes accepted. Seriously. The committee won’t look at them. Everything must be in the nomination form text boxes.

  • Review solely on form content. They won’t Google you. If it’s not in my nomination, it doesn’t exist.

  • One strong nomination beats ten weak ones. Quality over quantity matters here.

  • Selection happens in April. Nominations typically run January through early March.

Yikes. So if you write a bad nomination, I’m just... done?

Pretty much. That’s why I need real material from you.
