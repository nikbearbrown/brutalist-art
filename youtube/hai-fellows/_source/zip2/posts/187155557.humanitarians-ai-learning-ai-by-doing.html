I. The Application That Doesn’t Exist

There is no application form. No admissions committee reviews your GPA, your test scores, or your carefully crafted personal statement about why you’re passionate about artificial intelligence. There are no deadlines, no acceptance letters, no orientation week.

Instead, there’s a conversation. You explain what you want to learn, what you’ve already tried, and why the traditional path didn’t work. Someone explains what Humanitarians AI actually is—which is harder to describe than you’d think, because it doesn’t fit into the usual categories. Not a bootcamp. Not a degree program. Not an internship. Not exactly a research lab, though research happens here.

The best analogy: a working group. A collection of people building things that might matter, learning by making decisions that have consequences.

You either understand why that’s valuable, or you don’t. If you do, you start contributing. If you don’t, that’s fine too. This isn’t for everyone, and that’s by design.

II. What You’re Actually Joining

Humanitarians AI operates as a 501(c)(3) nonprofit that functions like an open-source software collective crossed with a compressed PhD program. There is no tuition. There are no grades. There is no guaranteed timeline. You work on real projects—frameworks, research papers, AI literacy programs, systems that ship to actual users—and you leave when you’re ready or when you get a job.

The evidence of this work is public. Ninety-one repositories on GitHub, all open-source, all MIT-licensed. Some updated hours ago. Some updated last week. Some dormant for months because the project reached completion or the team pivoted. You can inspect every commit, every architectural decision, every iteration. Nothing is hidden behind institutional paywalls or credential gates.

The core thesis is simple: most universities train students for procedural work that AI can now do in seconds. Pattern-matching. Template-following. The execution of known procedures. If that’s what you’re good at, you’re competing with a system that never sleeps, never asks for raises, and improves every quarter.

What remains scarce—what you cannot easily automate—is judgment under uncertainty. The ability to look at conflicting data and make a call. To navigate stakeholder disagreements. To recognize when the stated requirement is wrong. To own the consequences of your decisions.

You cannot learn this from lectures. You cannot learn this from tutorials. You learn it by encountering situations where there is no obvious right answer, making a decision anyway, and seeing what happens.

That’s what happens here.

III. The Three Pools

When you join, you choose your depth. Not based on credentials or years of experience, but based on what you’re actually ready to do.

Foundational learners often arrive having completed expensive master’s programs where they “learned very little.” Maybe the program was optimized for enrollment growth rather than education. Maybe it was online, asynchronous, with minimal feedback. Maybe the same adjunct taught linear regression in all ten courses. The degree is valid. The learning is insufficient.

If this is you, you start by learning properly—not by consuming more content, but by creating it. You might contribute to one of the Courses repositories: GIGO - A Crash Course in Data, Intro to Statistical Learning, Algorithms & Data Structures, Prompt Engineering for Generative AI. You research a topic you need to understand. You build a Jupyter notebook that demonstrates it. You write a tutorial that explains it to someone else who learned it wrong. You create the educational materials you wish had existed.

This is learn-by-teaching as survival strategy. You cannot create a good tutorial on gradient descent without understanding gradient descent deeply enough to explain it to someone else. The act of teaching forces you to operate at a higher cognitive level than the act of following instructions.

Applied contributors work on backend systems, AI/ML implementation, product management, documentation, integration work. You’re not building demos for a grade. You’re building systems that other people will use. When something breaks, there are consequences. When a design decision proves wrong, you have to live with it or refactor it.

You might spend four months on a single project. Look at the Cyber-Physical Systems repository (updated last week) or Job-Agents (also updated last week)—these are active builds where teams are making architectural decisions right now. You’ll make choices that seem reasonable at the time and prove problematic later. You’ll debug production issues. You’ll write code that AI generates in seconds, then spend hours reviewing whether that code is actually what you need. You’ll learn the difference between “technically correct” and “strategically right.”

Research-ready contributors work on publication-grade projects. You co-author papers submitted to peer-reviewed journals. You build frameworks that test ideas under real constraints. You formulate hypotheses, design experiments, analyze results, and defend your reasoning to people who will challenge it.

Look at repositories like Enhancing Trust in LLMs, Causal AI, Research Methods in AI. These aren’t course projects. They’re research infrastructure being built by Fellows who need to publish, who need to contribute to knowledge, who need evidence of thinking at the highest cognitive level.

You choose your pool. You can switch if you chose wrong. The system is designed around honest signaling, not credential theater.

IV. The Frameworks (Or: What “Doing AI” Actually Means)

Here’s what you might actually build:

Mycroft (updated five hours ago) is an investment intelligence framework. It uses specialized AI agents—analytical, portfolio, advisory—that often contradict each other. One agent might flag a stock as undervalued. Another might identify sector risk. A third might note regulatory concerns. Your job: build the system that resolves these conflicts.

There is no textbook answer. Investment decisions involve uncertainty, incomplete information, and tradeoffs. You have to make a call. You have to document why you made that call. Later, when the market moves, you see whether your reasoning was sound.

This is judgment practice. Not judgment theory.

Popper (named for philosopher Karl Popper) is a computational skepticism framework. Most AI systems try to prove they’re correct. Popper does the opposite. It uses adversarial agents to break models, to find edge cases, to expose hidden assumptions.

If you work on Popper, you learn that validation is the art of being rigorously uncertain. You learn to distrust confident-sounding outputs. You learn to ask “what would falsify this claim?” You learn that the goal isn’t to be right—the goal is to be honest about what you don’t know.

Madison (updated two hours ago) is a branding and marketing intelligence system that coordinates five layers: intelligence, content, research, experience, performance. It forces you to navigate the gap between technical optimization (click-through rates, conversion metrics) and strategic objectives (brand health, long-term positioning).

You’ll encounter situations where the AI suggests a tactic that will boost short-term metrics but degrade trust. You have to decide whether to follow the data or override it. You have to defend that decision. You have to explain which risks you’re accepting and why.

Dayhoff (updated January 1st) is a bioinformatics and public health framework. Healthcare AI isn’t standard software. Mistakes have biological consequences. A bug in a recommendation system might annoy users. A bug in a diagnostic system might kill patients.

If you work on Dayhoff, you learn that “good enough for code” isn’t good enough when lives are at stake. You learn to internalize different standards for testing and validation. You learn to recognize when you’re operating in a domain where your normal risk tolerance is inappropriate.

The RAMAN Effect uses Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy to detect pathogens in wastewater. You’re making public health calls from noisy, probabilistic data. You have incomplete information. You have time pressure. You have consequences if you’re wrong in either direction—false positives waste resources, false negatives miss outbreaks.

This is the deep end. You’re practicing judgment in conditions that approximate professional reality.

Beyond these flagship frameworks, there are dozens of active projects: Botspeak (AI literacy and prompt patterns), Lyrical Literacy (AI-generated educational music), Dewey (personalized learning systems), Cancer Textbook AI Tools, Music Medicine with AI, AI for Personalized Learning, Reinforcement Learning for Agentic AI. Some are research. Some are applied. Some are educational infrastructure. All are real.

V. How Teams Actually Work

You don’t work alone. You’re assigned to a project team working on a component of one of the larger frameworks. Maybe you’re building the conflict-resolution system for Mycroft. Maybe you’re implementing the adversarial testing layer for Popper. Maybe you’re designing the dashboard interface for Madison.

Your team has a project manager. You meet twice a week as a group on Discord—debugging together, reviewing each other’s approaches, making collective decisions about architecture and implementation. These aren’t status updates. These are working sessions where you encounter real problems and have to solve them collaboratively.

Discord serves as the coordination layer. You’ll see channels for different projects. You’ll see conversations happening in real-time as someone hits a wall and asks for help. You’ll see code reviews happening asynchronously across time zones. You’ll see architectural debates where team members disagree and have to work toward consensus.

Once a week, your team presents progress, challenges, and decisions to faculty. This isn’t a performance review. It’s a checkpoint: Are you stuck? Are you solving the right problem? Are your architectural choices defensible? Sometimes the answer is yes, keep going. Sometimes the answer is: you’re three weeks down the wrong path, here’s why, pivot.

Between these structured meetings, you work. Sometimes independently—writing code, researching approaches, implementing features. Sometimes you hit a wall and ping your teammate on Discord. Sometimes a teammate hits a wall and pings you.

Sometimes you need to understand something outside your project’s scope, and you reach out to someone on a different team. Sometimes someone from another team needs your expertise. The boundaries between projects are porous. You’re all building parts of interconnected systems.

Here’s what you discover: explaining your work to someone else exposes gaps in your own understanding. When a teammate asks “why did you structure it this way?” you realize your choice was intuitive, not principled. Now you have to articulate it. That process—turning implicit knowledge into explicit reasoning—is where learning happens.

VI. What You Actually Build (Week by Week)

Let’s say you join a team working on Mycroft’s portfolio optimization component. Week one: you’re reading documentation, understanding the existing architecture, identifying where your piece fits into the larger system. You’re not writing production code yet. You’re building mental models.

Week two: your team meets on Discord. The project manager outlines this sprint’s objectives. You’re implementing a risk assessment layer that feeds into the portfolio agent. You start building. You discover the data format from the analytical agent doesn’t match what you expected. You debug. You realize the problem isn’t your code—it’s a misalignment in the API contract between agents.

Week three: team meeting. You present the API mismatch. Someone else on your team has encountered something similar. You collectively decide: do we modify our code to handle the inconsistency, or do we ask the analytical agent team to standardize their output? You discuss tradeoffs. You make a call. You document it.

Week four: presentation to faculty. You explain your decision. Faculty asks: “What happens when the analytical agent scales to handle more data sources? Will your adapter pattern still work?” You realize: it’ll work, but it’ll become a maintenance nightmare. You need to refactor.

Week five: you refactor. You write better abstraction layers. You test against edge cases. You commit your changes to GitHub. Your teammate reviews the pull request and catches something you missed—a scenario where the risk assessment could return null. You fix it. The commit history shows this entire evolution. Anyone can see where you started, where you pivoted, what you learned.

Week six: your component ships. It’s integrated into the larger Mycroft framework. Other teams are now depending on your risk assessment layer working correctly. When it breaks—and eventually it will—they’ll let you know on Discord. This is real accountability.

VII. How You Actually Work: The Boyle System

Every week, you document in The Boyle System repository (updated three days ago):

  • Methods used and decisions made: What approaches did you try? Which did you choose? Why?

  • Failures encountered and iterations attempted: What didn’t work? What did you learn from it?

  • Linkage between time spent and learning outcomes: How did you spend your hours? What capability did that produce?

This is called the Boyle System, named for Robert Boyle’s meticulous experimental logs. It’s not busywork. It’s how you prove to yourself—and to anyone evaluating your work—that you actually learned something rather than just moved information around.

Every four weeks, renewal is based on documented contribution. If you can’t explain what you did, why you did it, what failed, and what changed, you’re not yet ready for professional work. Non-renewal isn’t punishment. It’s an honest signal.

This creates a different kind of pressure than grades. There’s no curving. There’s no partial credit for trying. There’s just: did you make meaningful progress on something real? Can you show your reasoning? Can you defend your decisions?

If yes, you continue. If no, maybe you need to switch pools. Maybe you need to reformulate the problem. Maybe you’re not ready yet. The system is designed to make this visible quickly rather than letting you coast for months.

VIII. What You Won’t Find Here

You won’t find:

  • Lectures. If you want someone to explain a concept in 90-minute blocks, YouTube exists. Faculty here mentor, challenge, and guide. They don’t perform knowledge transmission.

  • Grades. Your work is either advancing a real project or it isn’t. Your reasoning is either defensible or it isn’t. There’s no GPA. There’s no transcript. There’s no dean’s list.

  • Guaranteed outcomes. Some Fellows publish papers. Some ship systems. Some start businesses. Some get jobs after two months. Some stay for a year. The timeline is: when you’re ready, or when something else pulls you away.

  • Credential signaling. There is no Humanitarians AI degree. There is no certificate. What you have when you leave is a portfolio of work that people can inspect: ninety-one public repositories showing exactly what you built, when you built it, how you iterated, and where you made judgment calls. Code repositories, published papers, documented decision-making under uncertainty.

  • Solo hero narratives. You won’t build something entirely alone and take sole credit. You’re part of a team. Your work depends on others’ work. Others’ work depends on yours. You learn to collaborate on Discord, to coordinate through pull requests, to communicate when you’re blocked, and to help when others are stuck.

This team structure is load-bearing. You’re not consuming education. You’re participating in a working group where learning happens as a byproduct of building things that might matter.

IX. A Day in the Life (Hypothetically)

Let’s say you’re working on Mycroft’s conflict-resolution system. You wake up, check Discord, and see a message from your project manager: the analytical agent team pushed an update overnight that changed their confidence scoring format. Your integration broke.

You spend the morning debugging. You realize the issue isn’t your code—their new scoring system uses Bayesian posteriors instead of simple confidence intervals. Your weighted averaging approach no longer makes sense. You post in your team channel: “We have a problem. The analytical agent changed their output format. We need to redesign our conflict resolution layer.”

Your team meets that afternoon (scheduled twice-weekly session on Discord). You walk through the problem. Someone suggests patching it with a normalization layer. Someone else suggests this is actually an opportunity—Bayesian posteriors are better for what you’re trying to do anyway. You debate for thirty minutes. You decide to refactor.

You pair-program with a teammate over Discord screen-share. They understand Bayesian inference better than you do. You understand the existing conflict-resolution architecture better than they do. Together, you design something that neither of you could have built alone.

By evening, you have a working prototype. You test it. It handles the new input format and produces better outputs than the old system did. You commit it to GitHub. You document in your Boyle log: “Analytical agent update broke integration. Initial reaction: frustration. Realized this was actually improvement opportunity. Collaborated with [teammate] to redesign using Bayesian model averaging. New approach is better but computationally expensive. May need optimization before production.”

Next week in your team presentation, you’ll explain this decision. Faculty will probably ask about the computational cost. You’ll need to defend whether that tradeoff is acceptable. You’re prepared for that conversation because you’ve already thought through it.

This is what learning looks like here. Not absorbing facts. Not completing exercises. Making decisions, encountering consequences, adjusting your mental models, collaborating under pressure.

X. The Projects That Actually Ship

Humanitarians AI targets 10-20 peer-reviewed publications per year. Fellows co-author papers on AI ethics, machine learning methodology, educational technology, public health applications. Some get accepted. Some get rejected. When they get rejected, you read the reviewer comments, improve the work, and resubmit. This is how professional research works.

The organization runs active programs visible in the GitHub repositories:

Botspeak (updated November 6th) teaches AI literacy and prompt engineering to schools. There’s also How to Speak Bot: Prompt Patterns and Computational Skepticism and AI Fluency. You’re not building a curriculum in abstract. You’re building something that teachers will actually use with real students. When it doesn’t work, they’ll tell you why. You iterate.

Lyrical Literacy produces AI-generated educational music designed to enhance memory retention and cognitive development. Related work appears in Generative AI for Music Therapy and Music Medicine with AI. This requires understanding neuroscience, music theory, AI generation capabilities, and pedagogical design. You’re synthesizing across domains, not optimizing within one.

The Fellows Program is where you probably are—building frameworks, conducting research, learning by doing.

AI Skunkworks (for Northeastern undergrads) creates a bridge between coursework and real contribution. Students work on “code for a cause” projects that benefit nonprofits while developing skills.

Courses (updated December 24th) maintains open-access learning materials on YouTube and GitHub. The learn-build-teach cycle made public.

These aren’t extracurriculars. These are production systems that real people depend on. When you contribute to them, you’re making decisions that affect outcomes in the world.

XI. What You’re Actually Proving

When you leave Humanitarians AI, you won’t have a degree that says you know things. You’ll have evidence that you can do things.

Maybe that’s the Mycroft repository showing months of architectural decisions on a team project. Maybe it’s a contribution to one of the research repositories like Enhancing Trust in LLMs or Causal AI that turned into a peer-reviewed paper with your name on it. Maybe it’s a system component you built in Cyber-Physical Systems or Job-Agents that other teams integrated into their work. Maybe it’s documentation of your reasoning under uncertainty—visible in commit messages and pull requests—the moment you and your team rejected the simple solution in favor of a harder one that was actually correct.

This is proof, not pedigree. In a world where AI can generate technically correct code in seconds, the only thing that remains scarce is your specific, iterative path to getting there. The decisions you made when multiple options were plausible. The judgment you exercised when data conflicted. The accountability you accepted when requirements were unclear. The collaboration you navigated when teammates disagreed.

All of this is visible in public repositories. An employer who wants to see what you actually did can inspect every commit. They can see where you started, where you struggled, where you made architectural pivots, where you helped teammates, where teammates helped you. They can see the difference between someone who copy-pasted AI-generated code and someone who used AI as a tool while exercising judgment.

Employers who understand this will value it more than a transcript. Employers who don’t understand this won’t, and that’s fine. You’re not optimizing for all employers. You’re optimizing for the ones who recognize that judgment under uncertainty is what separates professional work from procedural execution.

XII. Who This Is For (And Who It Isn’t)

This works if:

  • You’re tired of consuming content and want to build things that might matter

  • You recognize that your degree didn’t teach you what you needed

  • You have a visa clock ticking and twelve months to prove competence

  • You’re intrinsically motivated enough to work without grades

  • You can handle ambiguity and make decisions without clear instructions

  • You’re willing to document your thinking and defend your reasoning on Discord

  • You want honest feedback more than comfortable validation

  • You can collaborate effectively and communicate when you’re stuck

This doesn’t work if:

  • You want a credential that signals you’re smart

  • You need structure provided entirely by others

  • You’re uncomfortable with subjective evaluation

  • You expect guaranteed outcomes for time invested

  • You optimize for the path of least resistance

  • You want someone to tell you exactly what to do

  • You prefer working in isolation without depending on others

  • You’re uncomfortable with your work being public and inspectable

The selection is mutual. You’re evaluating whether this environment matches your needs. The organization is evaluating whether you’re ready to operate at this depth. If either side decides it’s not a fit, that’s valuable information, not failure.

XIII. The Actual Ask

If you’re still reading, here’s what joining looks like:

You reach out. You have a conversation. You explain what you want to learn and why. Someone explains what’s currently being built and where you might contribute. You can look at the ninety-one public repositories and see exactly what’s active, what’s dormant, what’s being updated daily, what’s finished.

If there’s a match, you start contributing. Maybe you join a framework team working on Mycroft or Madison or Dayhoff. Maybe you help with research on one of the AI ethics or causal inference projects. Maybe you create educational materials for the Courses repositories. The work begins before the paperwork. You prove capability through contribution, not through promises.

If it’s working—if you’re making progress, if your reasoning is improving, if you’re shipping meaningful work—you continue. If it’s not working, you find out quickly and can redirect.

The opportunity cost is time. The upside is learning how to make decisions that matter in conditions of uncertainty, while working with others who are doing the same, with all your work visible and verifiable in public repositories. Whether that trade makes sense depends entirely on what you’re optimizing for.

There is no sales pitch here. There is no marketing copy promising transformation. There is only: this is what we do, this is how we do it, this is what you can expect. The GitHub organization has ninety-one public repositories. You can inspect every line of code, every commit message, every architectural decision. The work speaks for itself.

If that’s what you need, you’ll know.

If it’s not, that’s fine too.

The work continues either way.

To learn more or begin a conversation, visit humanitarians.ai or explore the public repositories at github.com/humanitariansai. No application required. Just a willingness to build something real.
