A new fellow at Mycroft is building an AI system to catch revenue lies before they collapse into layoffs. She has public data, and a problem: the most dangerous frauds hide where algorithms can’t see.

You know the story by the time it reaches the headlines. “Company X misses revenue guidance.” Clean. Antiseptic. What you don’t see is the hiring manager who froze twenty positions three months earlier, already sensing the pipeline wobble. Or the product team that stopped experimenting because nobody wanted to explain why conversion rates were softening. Or the VP who watched customer concentration creep from 30% to 47% and kept presenting the growth forecast anyway, because boards prefer a clean number to an honest range.

By the time the miss appears in the earnings release, hundreds of people have already experienced it as a personal catastrophe. The surprise isn’t that the forecast was wrong. It’s that nobody admitted the fragility until it was too late to matter.

Rithanya Chandran, a new fellow in the Mycroft project—an open-source experiment in AI-powered investment intelligence—is trying to build a machine that catches the lie earlier. Not the earnings manipulation that eventually reaches the SEC. The subtler kind: the confident forecast built on foundations everyone knows are crumbling.

It is a revenue-forecasting bullshit detector.

The Fingerprints of Fraud

Start with what can be measured. In 1999, a professor named Messod Beneish developed a mathematical formula that, retrospectively, identified Enron’s accounting manipulations years before the company collapsed. The Beneish M-Score uses eight financial ratios to calculate the probability that a company has inflated its reported health.

Each variable is a stress point. Days’ Sales in Receivables (DSRI) detects channel stuffing—recording revenue before customers actually pay. Asset Quality Index (AQI) catches when companies capitalize costs that should be expensed, making current income look better than it is. Total Accruals to Total Assets (TATA)—the most powerful signal—measures the gap between accounting profits and actual cash. High accruals are the primary engine of earnings manipulation.

In one manufacturing study, the M-Score flagged 284 out of 385 companies as requiring further examination for fraud. It works because fraud leaves mathematical residue. You can dress up the narrative, but the numbers leak.

Rithanya’s first layer needs to be forensic. Feed the system SEC filings. Calculate M-Scores. Track the Sloan Ratio—net income minus operating cash flow divided by total assets—which separates companies whose earnings are backed by actual cash from those running on accounting adjustments. A Sloan Ratio above 15% is a red flag. Above 25%, it’s a siren.

But here’s the problem she’ll hit immediately: by the time these signals appear in public filings, they’re already historical. Quarterly lag means you’re detecting fraud that started six months ago. The hiring freeze happened before the M-Score spiked. The dangerous lies stay internal until they can’t anymore.

The Cognitive Load of Deception

So Rithanya will need to look at language.

Stanford researchers analyzed 30,000 earnings calls and found that deception is cognitively expensive. When executives fabricate a consistent narrative under scrutiny, their speech patterns shift in measurable ways. They use more filler phrases—”you know,” “obviously,” “clearly”—verbal stalling while manufacturing responses. They shift from “I” to “we” when discussing problems, diffusing personal responsibility. They deploy extreme positivity—”incredible,” “unprecedented,” “fantastic”—overcompensating for doubts they can’t quite suppress.

Companies later charged with fraud scored 71 out of 100 on these linguistic markers. Stable blue-chip companies scored 34.

There’s a counterintuitive finding buried in the research: mid-tier AI models often outperform more sophisticated reasoning systems at catching these tells. Deception detection isn’t a high-level logic problem. It’s pattern-matching at the level of “um” and “uh” and pronoun choice. The lies leak in the syntax.

Rithanya will need to build agents that parse earnings call transcripts through FinBERT—a transformer model trained specifically on financial language—tracking not just sentiment but sentiment volatility across consecutive quarters. A sudden spike in “uncertainty tone” or “litigious language” predicts short-term stock drops and guidance misses. When executive sentiment stays “extremely positive” while the Sloan Ratio climbs, you’ve found the gap between performance and reality.

Hypothetically, imagine you’re a CFO. Your renewal pipeline is softening but you haven’t quantified it yet. You know your top customer is 38% of revenue and their procurement budget just got cut, but you tell yourself it’ll work out. During the earnings call, you say “we’re incredibly confident” instead of “we’re confident.” You say “the team” instead of “I.” Your language is giving you away before your spreadsheet does.

The question Rithanya will have to answer: can a machine detect that leak reliably enough to matter?

The Architecture of Disagreement

Here’s where it gets interesting. Traditional forecasting systems compress all analysis into a single model that outputs a single number. Rithanya’s proposing something structurally different: multi-agent systems that preserve disagreement.

Agent One—call it the Analytical Agent—looks at historical revenue patterns, pipeline data, market benchmarks. It projects 20% growth.

Agent Two—the Intelligence Agent—monitors regulatory filings, tracks customer concentration, watches LinkedIn for hiring freezes at major clients. It flags structural fragility: your top-10 customers represent 62% of revenue, procurement cycles have elongated from 107 days to 134 days, and your largest customer just posted a hiring freeze.

Agent Three—the Verification Agent—runs the forecast through stress tests. What happens if your top customer cuts spending 30%? What if CAC payback stretches from 12 months to 18? What if gross retention drops from 95% to 88%?

The agents debate. They surface tensions. When they can’t reach consensus after three rounds, the system doesn’t average their outputs into a false precision. It outputs the disagreement itself: Analytical patterns suggest growth, but structural conditions supporting that growth are deteriorating. Customer concentration risk is high. Forecast should be treated as fragile.

That’s the bullshit detector. Not a number. A warning that the clean number is a lie.

The technical framework is called MADAWSD—Multi-Agent Debate for Adversarial Reasoning—and in mathematical tasks, structured debate increases correct answer convergence by 34% compared to single-model approaches. But Rithanya isn’t solving math problems. She’s trying to detect when humans are lying to themselves about messy, non-linear business reality.

The bet is that preserving disagreement between agents mirrors the internal tensions that already exist in organizations—the sales VP who sees the pipeline wobble, the finance team that watches the accruals climb, the customer success manager who knows renewals are at risk—but that never make it into the confident forecast that travels upward.

What She’ll Hit

Rithanya has one month to make solid progress. Not to solve revenue forecasting. Not to build a production-ready system that replaces Bloomberg terminals. To pick one piece of the architecture described in her 8,000-word technical document and build it well enough that someone else could use it, extend it, or learn from where it breaks.

That constraint is the design. She needs to choose.

Maybe she builds the forensic layer first—agents that calculate Beneish M-Scores and Sloan Ratios from SEC filings, flagging when accruals are climbing faster than cash flow. The math is well-established. The data is public. The question is whether she can make the output useful: not just “M-Score = 2.84” but “This company’s receivables are growing 40% faster than sales, suggesting aggressive revenue recognition. Similar patterns preceded misses in 73% of cases.”

Or maybe she tackles the linguistic analysis—feeding earnings call transcripts through FinBERT to track sentiment volatility, building a classifier that catches cognitive load markers in executive speech. The challenge here is calibration: distinguishing genuine uncertainty from deceptive hedging, measuring false positive rates, figuring out if a shift from “I’m confident” to “we’re confident” actually predicts anything or if it’s just noise.

Or maybe she starts with the multi-agent debate architecture—building a system where one agent makes bullish projections from historical patterns while another flags structural risks from external signals, and they argue until consensus emerges or disagreement forces a fragility warning. The technical framework exists (MADAWSD), but implementing it for financial forecasting is uncharted. Does preserving disagreement make humans wiser or just paralyzed?

She’ll have to choose, and the choice matters. Because whatever she builds becomes a foundation for what comes next.

Building for Others to Build On

Here’s what makes this different from a typical graduate project: it’s public. The code will be open-source. The methodology will be documented on Substack. The failures will be visible.

That means someone reading her technical deep-dive might think: “The forensic layer is interesting, but what about the SaaS metrics layer? She mentions tracking Net Revenue Retention and customer concentration, but doesn’t implement it. I could build that.”

Or: “She’s using FinBERT for sentiment analysis, but what about tracking who’s speaking during earnings calls? When CFOs go silent and CEOs dominate Q&A, that’s often a signal. I could extend her linguistic analysis with speaker dynamics.”

Or: “Her multi-agent system debates revenue projections, but what if we added a ‘scenario simulation’ agent that stress-tests forecasts through extreme but plausible conditions—procurement freezes, platform pricing changes, regulatory shocks? I could build that fourth agent.”

The architecture is modular enough that different people can tackle different layers. Rithanya’s job isn’t to build the complete system. It’s to build one layer well enough that the seams are clean—clear inputs, clear outputs, documented assumptions, visible failures—so the next person knows where to plug in.

This is how Mycroft grows. Not through one person building everything, but through one person building something solid enough that it invites extension.

The Real Learning Starts When Simple Baselines Win

The most humbling moment will come when she compares her sophisticated multi-agent system to a dead-simple heuristic.

Imagine she spends three weeks building the debate architecture. Analytical agents examine pipeline velocity. Intelligence agents track customer concentration. Verification agents run stress tests. The system outputs: “Structural fragility detected. Customer concentration 47%, procurement cycles elongating, CAC payback 16 months. Forecast range: $78M-$112M.”

Then she tests a baseline: “If customer concentration exceeds 40% AND Sloan Ratio exceeds 15% AND sales cycle length increased >20% quarter-over-quarter, flag as fragile.”

If the baseline catches 80% of the cases the complex system catches, she has to be honest about it in her monthly review. That’s not failure. That’s discovery. She’s learned that revenue fragility often has simple mechanical signatures—you don’t need adversarial reasoning to catch a company that’s burning cash faster than it’s booking revenue.

But—and this matters—she’s also learned when the complex system adds value. Maybe the baseline catches obvious cases but misses subtler patterns: companies where metrics look healthy but linguistic analysis reveals executive anxiety, or where quantitative signals are mixed but external proxies (LinkedIn headcount freezes, Glassdoor sentiment crashes) suggest trouble.

The sophisticated system earns its complexity by catching what simple rules miss. If it can’t, then the honest conclusion is: “Multi-agent debate is overkill for revenue forecasting. Use simple heuristics plus human judgment.” That’s a useful finding. It saves the next person weeks of work.

Where Public Data Ends and Proxies Begin

She’ll hit the limit fast. The Beneish M-Score is backward-looking—by the time it spikes in a quarterly filing, the fraud started six months ago. Earnings calls are scripted performances. SEC disclosures are sanitized truths.

The most valuable signals never become public data: the sales Slack channel where reps admit pipeline quality is deteriorating, the customer success meeting where renewals are discussed with nervous hedges, the budget review where R&D spending gets quietly trimmed.

But that’s not a dead end. It’s a forcing function. She’ll have to get creative with proxies:

LinkedIn headcount data shows hiring velocity and function-level changes. If a company forecasts 40% growth but Engineering and Finance headcount are flat or declining, something’s wrong.

Glassdoor sentiment—15 million reviews processed in 8 hours using AI—reveals employee perception of leadership honesty and company direction. Sharp drops precede revenue misses.

Conference appearances and executive visibility. When CEOs suddenly stop doing podcasts or skip major industry events, that’s a signal.

Web scraping of job postings. When “urgent hiring” language disappears or postings get pulled, that’s a leading indicator.

These proxies aren’t perfect. They’re shadows of internal reality. But shadows contain information. The question is whether she can combine them rigorously enough that they predict something useful: not “this company will miss by 8.3%” but “this company’s public confidence appears decoupled from operational reality—investigate further.”

The Honesty Standard

In one month, Rithanya will present her progress. The review won’t ask “Did you solve revenue forecasting?” It’ll ask: “What did you build? What did you learn? What would you do differently?”

That forces a specific kind of honesty. She can’t hide behind jargon. She can’t claim victory by moving goalposts. She has to document:

  • What actually works (even if it’s simpler than expected)

  • Where the system breaks (and why)

  • What assumptions proved wrong

  • What she’d prioritize differently if starting over

That honesty is the real deliverable. Because the next person—the one who reads her Substack and thinks “I could extend this”—needs to know the ground truth. Which signals are reliable? Which are noisy? Where does public data run out? When do complex methods earn their complexity?

If she discovers that forensic accounting ratios predict misses with 70% accuracy but only with a one-quarter lag, that’s useful—the next person knows not to rely on them for early warning.

If she finds that linguistic analysis has a 40% false positive rate because genuinely uncertain executives use the same hedging language as deceptive ones, that’s useful—the next person knows to combine it with other signals rather than treat it as authoritative.

If multi-agent debate surfaces important tensions but takes 10x longer to compute than simple rules-based systems, that’s useful—the next person can decide if the tradeoff is worth it for their use case.

Building Something of Value

Here’s what makes this more than an academic exercise: AI companies are actually hard to forecast. The problem is real. People are actually getting blindsided by “surprise” cuts that weren’t surprises to anyone paying attention.

Even an imperfect detector has value if it:

  • Makes one class of fragility more visible (high accruals + low cash flow)

  • Demonstrates one methodology rigorously (conformal prediction for uncertainty quantification)

  • Creates one reusable component (FinBERT pipeline for earnings call analysis)

  • Documents one important failure mode (when simple baselines outperform complex systems)

The goal isn’t perfection. It’s building something solid enough that it invites the next question: “What if we added...?” or “What if we tried...?” or “What if we combined this with...?”

That’s how open-source projects grow. Not through one person building the complete vision, but through one person building something real enough that others can see where to extend it.

Rithanya’s month isn’t about solving the problem. It’s about making solid progress on one piece of it—progress that’s documented well enough, tested honestly enough, and built cleanly enough that someone else can pick up where she leaves off.

The struggle is part of the value. When she hits the wall of public data limitations, that’s not failure—it’s mapping the boundary of what’s detectable. When simple baselines outperform complex systems, that’s not embarrassment—it’s discovery.

And when someone reads her work and thinks “I could build the piece she didn’t have time for,” that’s when the project succeeds. Not because one person built everything, but because one person built something solid enough to be built upon.
