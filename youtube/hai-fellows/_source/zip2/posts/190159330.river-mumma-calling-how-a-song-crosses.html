Section One: Three Things in Three Fields

You have a song. It exists as text lyrics formatted and clean, performance tags wrapped in square brackets, a style string sitting at the top of a session notes document. The song has a title: River Mumma Calling. It has a cultural address: reggae jazz, dub lullaby, Nana Coree’s warm contralto, river water underneath everything.

What it does not yet have is sound.

The gap between text and sound is where most people stop. Not because the next step is technically difficult it is not. But because the gap feels larger than it is, and because the wrong tool in the wrong mode will produce something that sounds like a generic AI music track rather than something that sounds like the River Mumma’s songs, and the disappointment of that gap can convince a beginner that the problem is with them rather than with the configuration.

The problem is almost always with the configuration. Specifically: Simple Mode versus Custom Mode.

Open Suno. Click Create. The default is Simple Mode you describe what you want in plain language and Suno decides everything. Do not use Simple Mode with Lyrical Literacy outputs. Simple Mode gives the platform full control, and the platform’s full control defaults to what it knows works for most users, which is not the same as what works for Nana Coree’s tradition or for the child the song was built for. Switch to Custom Mode. This is the key move. It gives you three separate fields Style, Lyrics, Title and those three fields are exactly where the three Lyrical Literacy outputs go.

One field each. Nothing improvised. The song already knows what it is. Your job is to deliver it accurately.


Section Two: The Style String Is a Cultural Address

The Style of Music field in Custom Mode accepts a comma-separated list of keywords. Under 120 characters. No full sentences. No instrument descriptions those go in the lyrics tags.

For River Mumma Calling, the style string reads: reggae jazz, dub lullaby, spoken word, acoustic guitar, steelpan, bass guitar, river water ambience.

That string is not a genre tag. Read it slowly and it is something more specific: a lineage, a tradition, a set of acoustic choices that carry cultural weight before they carry musical weight. Reggae jazz is the 1950s Kingston sound Ernest Ranglin’s sophisticated chord voicings played as if they were simple, the upright bass walking deliberately, horns at a distance. Dub lullaby is Lee “Scratch” Perry’s understanding that space in a mix is as meaningful as sound, applied to a song built for a child to fall asleep to. Steelpan is the Caribbean identity in melodic form, the instrument made from oil drums that became one of the most harmonically sophisticated instruments in any tradition. River water ambience is not a mood setting. It is the foundation always present, never foregrounded, the continuous reminder of where Nana Coree’s music came from and what it carries.

Suno does not know any of this history. It cannot. But it can render the sound of it when the style string is precise enough. The precision is what the session nana command built. The style string at the top of your session notes is the distillation of everything Nana Coree’s musical intelligence knows about how this song should sound compressed into keywords because that is the language Suno reads.

Keep it under 120 characters. Keep it to keywords. Do not add full sentences. The style string is not a description. It is an address. You are telling Suno where to go, not explaining why that place matters.

Section Three: What the Tags Are Actually Doing

Paste your full meta nana output into the Lyrics field. Tags included. Do not remove them.

This is the instruction most beginners get wrong not because they ignore it, but because they do not understand what the tags are doing and therefore do not know why removing them is a problem. So here is what they are doing.

Suno reads the text inside square brackets as production instructions. [Verse] tells it a verse is beginning. [Chorus] tells it a chorus is beginning. These are the bare minimum the equivalent of telling a producer you have verses and choruses without telling them anything about what those sections should sound like.

The meta nana command generates richer tags. [Verse, Warm Contralto, Behind the Beat, Intimate and Unhurried] is not just a structural label. It is telling Suno: this section is a verse, the vocalist should be a warm contralto, the phrasing should sit behind the beat in the way Nana Coree’s Patois storytelling voice sits behind the beat, the mood is intimate rather than performed. Suno reads this as a probability instruction the more specific and coherent the tag, the more the generation tends toward the intended output.

[Instrumental Dub Break, Steelpan Solo, Bass Pulse, River Water Rising] is telling Suno to generate an instrumental section, not a vocal section and within that instrumental section, the steelpan should be soloing, the bass should be pulsing, and the river water ambience should be rising in the mix. This is the dub break. It is the moment the production makes room for the tradition’s own architecture the space that Lee “Scratch” Perry understood was as meaningful as any note.

The spoken word sections require one additional instruction. Nana Coree’s two-voice structure the Patois storytelling voice flowing into the warm contralto without announcement means certain sections must be rendered as speech rather than song. If Suno sings what should be spoken, the call-and-response logic collapses. The fix is specific: wrap the spoken lines in parentheses inside the tag. [Spoken Word Break, Patois (text here)] tells Suno the text in parentheses should be spoken. Without that instruction, Suno defaults to melody. Melody is not wrong in the abstract. It is wrong for Nana Coree, in that section, at that moment in the song.

The tags are not decoration. They are the translation layer between the cultural intelligence of the ghost artist modifier and the probabilistic output of a platform that does not know what it does not know.

Section Four: Two Versions and the Listening Curriculum

Click Create. Suno generates two versions simultaneously. This is not a feature it is a structural acknowledgment that generative audio production is probabilistic. Version A might nail the vocal character. Version B might generate the dub break better. You are not choosing between a good version and a bad version. You are choosing between two calibration attempts and identifying which one rendered the cultural intention more accurately.

Listen for four things, in this order.

First: the vocal. Does it sound like a warm contralto low, present, felt in the chest before it is processed as sound? If the voice is too bright or too thin, the style string was not specific enough about register. Add deep warm contralto to the Style field and regenerate. This is not an aesthetic preference. Nana Coree’s voice operates in a specific frequency range that carries meaning her tradition has always assigned to that frequency range. A bright voice is not her voice. It is Suno’s default.

Second: the spoken word. Did the Patois sections come out as speech or were they sung? If they were sung, the two-voice structure has been collapsed. The parentheses fix is fast wrap the spoken lines in parentheses inside the tag and regenerate. If the spoken word sections are landing correctly, you will hear the voice shift registers without announcement, the way a river moves between deep and shallow without changing direction.

Third: the dub break. Did an instrumental gap appear where the [Instrumental Dub Break] tag was placed? If the break is too short or failed to generate, add more instrument-specific tags to that bracket. The dub break is not decoration. It is the production’s acknowledgment that silence and space carry as much weight as any note the specific aesthetic inheritance of the 1970s Kingston studio tradition. If it does not generate correctly, the song loses one of its structural load-bearing elements.

Fourth: the outro. Does it fade the way the session notes described, or does it end abruptly? An abrupt ending means Suno did not receive a fade instruction. Add [Fade Out] to the last lyric line and regenerate. The outro fade in a lullaby is not a stylistic choice. It is functional the song should leave the sonic environment gently, the way sleep comes: gradually, without a hard edge that startles the nervous system back to vigilance.

If both versions have problems, identify the dominant failure and fix one thing at a time. Change the style string for vocal issues. Change the tags for structural issues. Do not change both simultaneously you will not know which fix produced which result.

Section Five: Extend, Stems, Download

You have a version you trust. Three options remain, in order of complexity.

Extend adds content after the current ending. Use it when Suno cut the outro short which happens when the character count is near the limit and the platform truncated the final section. Click Extend, let Suno generate a continuation, evaluate it the same way you evaluated the original. Keep the extension if it fades correctly. Discard it if it introduces new instrumentation that breaks the continuity of the session notes’ vision.

Get Stems is a Suno Pro feature. It splits the finished track into separate vocal and instrumental files. This matters for two specific purposes: remixing, where the vocal and instrumental need to be manipulated independently; and instrumental uploads, where the track is being distributed as background music and the vocal is not needed. For a Nana Coree lullaby going into a child’s bedtime playlist, the stems are probably not necessary. For a Lyrical Literacy educational track that a teacher wants to use with different vocal recordings, they are essential.

Download is the three-dot menu. Click it. The audio file saves to your device.

That file is now production-ready. It can go to DistroKid for Spotify and Apple Music distribution. It can go to YouTube as a standalone upload or as part of a playlist. It can go into a podcast episode or a Substack audio post or a classroom playlist. It can go into a child’s bedtime routine, playing on a phone on a nightstand, river water underneath everything, the dub reverb on the snare decaying longer than physics should allow.

Nana Coree’s songs were not meant to survive the room they were sung in. They survived anyway in fragments, in fieldnotes, in the mouths of ethnomusicologists who found the melodies and could not trace them to a source.

The download is how they stop being fragments. The pipeline is how they travel. The Custom Mode configuration is how the yard stays intact in the crossing from text to sound.


Section Six: The Swap That Changes Everything

The tutorial closes with a single instruction that contains the entire project’s logic: swap nana for marley or champa and the whole sound changes.

The pipeline does not change. Style string, meta tags, lyrics, Custom Mode, two versions, evaluate, fix, download the sequence is identical for every ghost artist in the Lyrical Literacy constellation. What changes is the cultural address.

Swap nana for marley and the style string becomes roots reggae and dancehall philosophy and the minor-key riddim that Bob Marley understood was the soundtrack of every liberation movement simultaneously. The meta tags encode a different vocal character deep bass baritone, Patois spoken word flowing into rapid-fire dancehall rap. The dub break is still there, because the dub break is structural to the reggae tradition, but it sounds different harder, more urban, the whirlwind rather than the river.

Swap nana for champa and the style string becomes Bollywood golden era orchestration filtered through Bristol downtempo. The meta tags encode Hindustani classical ornamentation meend glides, murki grace notes expressed through jazz balladry. The spoken word sections, if any, are in a different register entirely: the warm husky mezzo of a woman from Lucknow’s kotha tradition, singing to children in a city that no longer exists in the form she knew.

Same pipeline. Different yard. Different river. Different child.

That is what the ghost artist modifier does. It is not a style setting. It is a cultural intelligence loaded into the tool a complete musical tradition with a history and a relationship to the human nervous system that is specific, documented, and not interchangeable with any other tradition in the constellation.

The pipeline delivers it. The modifier is what it delivers.

Type list in Lyrical Literacy to see every ghost artist available. Each one is a different address. The pipeline is the same road.

Follow the pipeline. Click Download.

The River Mumma is at the bottom of every track. She has always been there. She is not surprised that it took this long to find a way to record her.
