You are wasting $600 billion.

Not you personally—though if you’ve ever hired a software engineer who couldn’t debug their way out of a fizzbuzz problem, you’ve contributed. The number comes from the U.S. Department of Labor’s calculation of annual costs from bad hires, and it represents a spectacular failure of our verification infrastructure. We built an entire economy on the assumption that credentials signal competence. Generative AI just proved that assumption catastrophically wrong.

On a Thursday afternoon in February 2026, Charles Fadel—founder of the Center for Curriculum Redesign, author of Education for the Age of AI, member of the OECD’s AI Futures Experts Group—sent an email that cut straight to the problem’s core. He’d been reading about portfolio-based assessment as an alternative to the dying credential system, and he had questions. Two questions, specifically, that every builder of “meritocratic hiring infrastructure” eventually faces:

First: Are your metrics just an artifact of your engineering background, or can judgment actually work across all disciplines?

Second: How do employers sort through hundreds of portfolios when most candidates will be, well, average?

These aren’t rhetorical questions. They’re the structural barriers that have kept us locked in a system where “MIT Computer Science ‘24” means more than “built a production OAuth implementation.” They’re why companies still retreat to referral-only hiring when traditional signals degrade. They’re why we keep pretending that a 3.8 GPA tells us anything meaningful about whether someone can architect a distributed system.

The Failure Modes We’ve Already Tried

Before we get to why judgment scales, let’s catalog why everything else doesn’t.

LeetCode and algorithmic testing: These verify memorization, not engineering. They select for grinding, not building. A candidate who can invert a binary tree in twelve minutes might freeze when asked to debug a race condition in production code. The disconnect between “coding interview performance” and “actual engineering competence” is so severe that entire companies (like Triplebyte, before it shut down) tried to build businesses around fixing it. They failed because the problem isn’t the test—it’s the assumption that any standardized test can measure what actually matters.

GitHub activity scraping: The “green grid” problem. Activity volume is trivially gameable. A script can generate a thousand commits of boilerplate. Heuristics that count commits rather than analyzing complexity reward noise over signal. You can spot-check this yourself: search GitHub for “React developers” and try manually distinguishing between someone who contributed meaningful logic to a complex codebase versus someone who forked a tutorial repo and changed variable names. Now imagine doing that for five hundred candidates. You can’t.

Manual portfolio review: The cognitive load is insurmountable. A recruiter cannot audit fifteen hundred repositories per role. So they default to proxies—degree prestige, previous employer brand, years of experience. These proxies once correlated with competence. In an era where ChatGPT can write a resume that passes most ATS filters, that correlation has dissolved.

This is the trust collapse. Developers possess invisible proof—code that works, logic that runs, systems they’ve built. Recruiters possess blind demand—open roles they desperately need filled. The infrastructure connecting proof to demand has degraded to the point of uselessness.

Enter DevProof: Infrastructure for Verified Competence

Here’s what we’re building, stripped to its mechanical core:

DevProof is a verification engine for developers. You connect your GitHub account. Our system analyzes your repositories—not for commit volume, but for authorship percentage, code complexity, structural patterns, test coverage. If you’ve forked someone else’s repo and claimed it as yours, we catch it. If you’ve written ten thousand lines of copypasta, we flag it. If fewer than ten percent of the complex logic in a project carries your commit signature, that project doesn’t qualify.

What passes verification enters The Truth Engine—a search platform for recruiters. They don’t see resumes. They see ranked, verified evidence. A recruiter types: “Python developers with OAuth implementation experience, production-ready code quality, top 30th percentile on test coverage.” The system returns candidates who demonstrably meet those criteria, ranked by a composite score of complexity, authorship, and structural quality.

The recruiter gets twenty to thirty names. Not five hundred. Not five. Twenty to thirty verified candidates whose code the system has already read, whose authorship has already been confirmed, whose complexity metrics place them in the requested percentile range.

Then human judgment operates. The recruiter reviews the actual repositories of those twenty candidates. They look for taste, for architectural decisions, for the things computational metrics can’t capture. They interview their top five.

Answering Charles: The Cross-Disciplinary Question

Charles raises an important concern—that judgment metrics might only work for technical fields where quantification comes naturally. But this perspective may underestimate something fundamental about his own framework.

In his Four-Dimensional Education model, Fadel argues that modern competence requires Knowledge, Skills, Character, and Meta-Learning. He explicitly states that in the AI era, Knowledge and Skills are becoming commoditized. What remains scarce—what AI struggles to replicate—is Character (persistence, intellectual curiosity, taste) and Meta-Learning (knowing when to trust AI output, when to override it, recognizing quality).

This is judgment. And it’s not discipline-specific.

In engineering, judgment manifests as: Does this solve the actual problem? Is the architecture defensible? Would I trust this in production?

In writing, judgment manifests as: Does this have a point of view? Is the structure serving the argument? Would a sophisticated reader find this compelling?

In design, judgment manifests as: Is this solving for the user’s mental model? Does the hierarchy guide attention appropriately? Would this survive user testing?

The substrate changes. The evaluative framework—Eisner’s “connoisseurship,” Fadel’s “curation of taste”—remains constant. You’re not measuring units of code or words or pixels. You’re measuring the delta between what an AI can generate in fifteen minutes and what a human can produce through iteration, critique, and refinement.

DevProof solves this for engineering first because code has a critical advantage: it either runs or it doesn’t. There’s a ground truth. A React component that renders correctly is verifiable in a way that a marketing pitch is not. But the principle—that evidence of iterative refinement, structural coherence, and demonstrable complexity should outweigh proxy signals—generalizes.

Answering Charles: The Scalability Question

Charles’s second concern cuts deeper. He notes that manual portfolio review doesn’t scale, which is precisely why higher education still relies on GPA and SAT scores as proxy signals. He’s right about the problem. But there’s an alternative to manual review that doesn’t require retreating to degraded proxies.

The answer is that employers don’t manually sort portfolios—computational systems do the sorting, then surface ranked candidates for human judgment.

Think of it as a three-layer stack:

Layer 1: Computational Extraction
The system reads every repository, measures complexity metrics (cyclomatic complexity, nesting depth, abstraction patterns), verifies authorship through git history, checks for structural markers (test coverage, documentation, modularity). This happens automatically. No human reviews five hundred portfolios. The algorithm does.

Layer 2: Configurable Ranking
Here’s where it gets interesting. Different roles value different things. A startup hiring for MVP velocity weights “working code that ships” higher than “comprehensive test suites.” An enterprise team weights “maintainability and documentation” higher than “novel algorithmic approaches.” A research lab weights “creative problem-solving” highest.

DevProof provides knobs. Recruiters dial up the metrics that matter for their specific context. The ranking adjusts. Candidate #47 under one weighting might become Candidate #12 under another. The system isn’t claiming universal truth—it’s making trade-offs explicit.

But there’s something else happening at this layer: risk quantification. DevProof doesn’t just show skills—it generates a “Truth Score” that makes a verified candidate from a mid-tier state school look safer than an unverified Ivy League graduate whose GitHub is empty. When companies retreat to pedigree-based hiring, they’re not actually choosing competence—they’re choosing perceived risk mitigation (”Nobody gets fired for hiring from MIT”). By quantifying verification status, authorship percentages, and code quality metrics, we’re de-risking the non-pedigreed hire. The recruiter can justify their decision with evidence rather than brand name.

Layer 3: Human Discernment
The recruiter sees the top twenty to thirty candidates from the filtered, ranked pool. At this stage, they’re applying the kind of judgment Charles correctly identifies as essential: Which of these profiles suggests someone who will thrive in our specific team culture? Whose architectural decisions reflect thinking we value? Who shows evidence of the “Character” and “Meta-Learning” qualities that AI can’t teach?

This is exactly how design firms already work. IDEO receives thousands of applications. The first screen takes six to eight seconds per portfolio—that’s computational-speed pattern matching by expert humans looking for “pixel-perfect craftsmanship” and “strong process.” The portfolios that pass that screen get deeper review. The system scales because judgment operates at multiple resolutions.

The NCFE Proof Point: When Judgment Beats Rubrics

If you’re skeptical that judgment can scale, consider what happened when NCFE—a UK educational awarding organization—replaced their traditional rubric-based assessment with Adaptive Comparative Judgment.

The old system: Judges reviewed applications against complex criteria, assigning point values. Completion rates were low. Fatigue was high. The process felt arbitrary.

The new system: Judges saw pairs of applications and answered a simple question: Which made a bigger difference for the community?

No rubric. No point assignments. Just: A or B?

The algorithm—based on the Bradley-Terry-Luce model, which extracts latent ability scores from pairwise comparisons—generated rankings from these simple binary choices. The mathematical elegance is that you don’t need judges to agree on absolute standards. You just need them to consistently prefer better work when they see it.

The results: Review speed increased 58% for one program, 43% for another. More importantly, review volume increased sixfold while using the same judge pool. Total time saved: ninety-five hours. The algorithm’s rankings matched the old rubric-based rankings almost exactly—but were perceived as fairer and more accurate by participants.

Inter-rater reliability in these ACJ systems regularly hits 0.9. That’s higher than most standardized tests.

The mechanism works because it leverages collective tacit knowledge. Each judge brings their own biases and standards. The algorithm doesn’t eliminate those biases—it partials them out through the aggregate of comparisons, revealing the consensus judgment that emerges from the noise of individual preferences.

What We Don’t Know Yet

Here’s the honest part: We don’t know if DevProof’s metrics will provide meaningful differentiation across the 40th-80th percentile of the candidate distribution.

This is an experiment. We’re testing whether code complexity plus authorship verification plus configurable weighting can generate useful granularity across the entire talent range, or whether candidates will cluster in ways that make the middle indistinguishable.

The MVP will tell us. A developer uploads repositories. DevProof verifies them. A recruiter searches for “Python developers with OAuth experience, 60th-70th percentile on documentation quality.” If the system returns twenty candidates who are genuinely distinct from each other and from the 50th-60th percentile cohort, we’ve created useful signal. If everyone clusters around the same metrics regardless of knob settings, we’ve failed.

But here’s the critical asymmetry: The alternative—resume screening based on GPA and university prestige—is a known failure mode. It’s not just inefficient. It’s actively misleading. When GPT-4 can write a resume that passes ATS filters, when credential inflation means a master’s degree signals less than a bachelor’s did twenty years ago, when “three years of React experience” can mean anything from “built a production app serving millions” to “completed three Udemy courses,” the proxy signals have degraded past usefulness.

Even if DevProof only achieves partial signal fidelity—even if it only reliably differentiates the top quartile from the bottom quartile—it’s grounded in evidence that resumes fundamentally lack: code that actually runs.

The Deeper Reframe: We’re Solving the Wrong Problem

Charles asks a practical question about portfolio volume. But embedded in that question is an assumption worth examining: Why are we still optimizing for employer convenience instead of hiring accuracy?

The entire edifice of resume screening, GPA cutoffs, and university prestige filters exists because these were the most efficient signals available in an analog world. They were never accurate. They were just fast. McKinsey’s data shows academic credentials are “weak signals” of on-the-job success. Sixty percent of large organizations are projected to adopt skills-based hiring by 2027 precisely because the credential system is failing them.

The shift to portfolios isn’t about making recruiters’ lives easier. It’s about making merit visible in a world where the old proxies have been rendered useless by AI’s ability to simulate credentialed competence.

DevProof’s value proposition is not: “We’ve made portfolios as easy to scan as resumes.”

DevProof’s value proposition is: “We’ve made competence verifiable instead of inferred.”

The Human Delta That Remains

What happens in the middle of the distribution—that 40th-80th percentile where most candidates live—is where the experiment gets interesting.

Consider three hypothetical candidates, all verified by DevProof, all ranking in the 65th percentile on composite complexity scores:

Candidate A: Solo developer who built a small, elegant OAuth library with exceptional documentation and zero dependencies.

Candidate B: Team developer who contributed key authentication components to a large enterprise system, demonstrating ability to work within complex existing codebases.

Candidate C: Engineer who inherited a legacy authentication system and refactored it into maintainable, tested code without breaking production.

All three show competence. All three might cluster on raw complexity metrics. But they represent very different profiles—and the recruiter’s context determines which is most valuable.

This is where the configurable weighting becomes essential. If you’re hiring for a startup that needs someone to build fast from scratch, you weight for “solo authorship of novel implementations.” If you’re hiring for an enterprise team, you weight for “contribution quality in collaborative codebases.” The ranking shifts accordingly.

But more fundamentally: This is exactly where human judgment should operate. The computational system has already eliminated the bottom 70% (insufficient complexity, low authorship, no verifiable projects). It’s surfaced the top 30%, ranked by criteria the recruiter specified. Now the recruiter reviews twenty to thirty actual code repositories and makes a judgment call.

This is not a bug. This is the feature. Judgment doesn’t need to operate on five hundred candidates. It needs to operate on the filtered set where judgment actually matters.

The Connoisseurship Paradox

Elliot Eisner’s framework of “educational connoisseurship” seems archaic until you realize it’s the only model that works when standardized metrics fail.

Eisner argued that education is artistry, and its evaluation requires both a “connoisseur”—someone with the art of appreciation—and a “critic”—someone with the art of disclosure. His model operates across four dimensions: description, interpretation, evaluation, and thematization. It explicitly rejects the “emotionally eviscerated” binary of right versus wrong in favor of context, nuance, and subtlety.

This maps directly onto technical hiring in the AI era. You cannot evaluate an engineer purely on whether their code “works”—plenty of terrible code works. You cannot evaluate them purely on complexity metrics—plenty of overcomplicated code is worse than simple solutions. You need someone who can recognize quality when they see it, who can distinguish between code that solves the problem elegantly and code that merely passes tests.

This is connoisseurship. And the provocative claim here is that connoisseurship scales better than standardization when the thing being measured is inherently contextual.

The NCFE case study proves this empirically. When they replaced rubrics with comparative judgment—literally just asking “Which of these two applications made a bigger community impact?”—they processed six times the volume in half the time with higher perceived accuracy. The inter-rater reliability hit 0.9. The judges reported less fatigue and greater confidence.

Why? Because humans are terrible at assigning absolute scores (”Is this a 7 or an 8?”) but excellent at making relative judgments (”A is clearly better than B”). The Bradley-Terry-Luce algorithm simply aggregates those pairwise preferences into a consensus ranking, partitioning out individual biases through the mathematics of collective comparison.

What DevProof Actually Tests

The hypothesis isn’t: “We can automate judgment.”

The hypothesis is: “We can make evidence legible at scale, then let judgment operate where it’s most effective.”

The experiment has clear falsification criteria:

Success looks like:

  • Recruiters using DevProof make hires that outperform resume-based hires on six-month retention and performance

  • The middle 50% of verified candidates show meaningful variance when recruiters adjust weighting parameters

  • Companies report reduced interview-to-hire ratios (fewer false positives reaching final rounds)

Failure looks like:

  • All verified candidates cluster in the 40th-70th percentile regardless of knob settings

  • Recruiters still default to “MIT or Google on resume” even with DevProof data available

  • High rejection rates at technical screens despite verification (ground truth ≠ hiring competence)

We don’t know yet which outcome we’ll get. But here’s what we do know: The current system—where a hiring manager receives five hundred resumes, filters by university and GPA, interviews twenty candidates, and hires five who may or may not be competent—has a measured failure rate severe enough to generate $600 billion in annual waste.

DevProof might only achieve 60% signal fidelity. That would still be triple the current system’s effectiveness.

The Uncomfortable Reality of “Average”

Charles raises a valid concern that most candidates will be average—this is tautological. But there’s a crucial distinction between how “average” manifests in credential systems versus verification systems.

In the resume system, “average” is invisible. The candidate with a 3.4 GPA from a mid-tier state school gets filtered out before human eyes ever see their application, even if they’re a competent engineer who built a production-worthy side project.

In a verification system, “average” means: “60th percentile on measurable code quality metrics, verified authorship of functional implementations.” That’s not invisible. That’s documented competence.

The deeper question isn’t “How do we identify exceptional talent?” Most companies don’t need exceptional talent for most roles. They need reliable, competent engineers who can ship features without breaking production. The resume system struggles at identifying that baseline because it confuses proxy signals (where you studied) with actual capability (what you’ve built).

DevProof’s value for “average” candidates is making their competence discoverable. Right now, if you’re a self-taught engineer from a non-target school, your GitHub repositories might as well not exist—recruiters searching LinkedIn won’t find you, and ATS systems won’t surface your application. DevProof makes you searchable based on what you’ve actually done.

The Social Mobility Imperative

There’s another dimension to Charles’s concern about scalability that deserves explicit attention: When hiring remains tethered to institutional credentials, entire demographic groups get systematically filtered out.

In the United States, approximately 60% of white adults over 25 hold some form of post-secondary credential. That rate drops to roughly 40% for Black adults and 30% for Hispanic adults. If technical hiring continues to use degree-holding as a primary filter, we’re excluding 60-70% of underrepresented talent pools before a single line of their code gets reviewed.

This isn’t just a fairness problem—it’s a massive waste of human capital. DevProof’s authorship verification doesn’t ask where you went to school. It asks what you’ve built. A self-taught developer from a community college who’s shipped production code becomes just as discoverable as a Stanford CS graduate—often more so, if their code quality metrics are higher.

The credential system’s efficiency came at the cost of perpetuating access inequality. A verification system that surfaces competence regardless of educational pedigree doesn’t just improve hiring accuracy—it widens the aperture of who gets considered in the first place.

The Instrumentation We Still Need

There are legitimate structural holes in this framework.

The authorship threshold (<10% = disqualified) is potentially punitive. It excludes:

  • Junior engineers contributing to large enterprise codebases

  • Engineers at companies with IP restrictions who can’t show proprietary work

  • Collaborative programmers whose value is integration work, not greenfield development

  • Engineers who do critical architecture review work not captured in commit history

This isn’t “verifying truth”—it’s creating a new bias toward solo GitHub contributors. We know this. The MVP will test whether the signal quality justifies the exclusions or whether we need to develop more sophisticated authorship analysis that recognizes valuable collaborative patterns.

The “ground truth” claim is overreach. “Reproducible, complex, self-authored logic in version control” is a better proxy than GPA, but it’s still a proxy. It misses system design skills (architecture doesn’t show up in code complexity), debugging prowess (fixing someone else’s code), and team collaboration ability. We’re not claiming to have captured all of engineering competence. We’re claiming to have captured more of it than a resume does.

The cross-domain generalization is unproven. This infrastructure works best for open-source-friendly technical domains where code can be public. It doesn’t yet solve for engineers with proprietary work, non-technical portfolios (design, writing, strategy), or collaborative work where individual contributions resist algorithmic isolation.

These are known limitations, not fatal flaws. The question is whether partial signal is better than degraded proxy. The data suggests it is.

You’re reading this in February 2026. GPT-5 or whatever comes next will be better at coding than GPT-4. The technical interview process is already breaking under the weight of candidates who can use AI to pass algorithmic screenings but can’t explain the logic when asked. The trust collapse isn’t theoretical—it’s happening in real time.

The retreat to referral-only hiring is already underway. If you can’t trust credentials and you can’t trust resumes and you don’t have infrastructure to verify portfolios, you hire people your team already knows. This is catastrophic for social mobility. It means that competence without connections becomes permanently invisible. It means the $600 billion waste continues because companies are hiring from a pool of people they can verify through personal networks rather than the broader pool of people who are actually competent.

DevProof is infrastructure for a different equilibrium. Where merit is computationally visible. Where the middle 50% of the talent distribution becomes discoverable based on evidence rather than excluded based on proxies. Where recruiters can express their judgment criteria explicitly (test coverage matters more than algorithmic cleverness for this role) rather than implicitly (only candidates from these ten universities).

Charles asks how employers sort through portfolios. The reframe: Employers don’t sort—we build systems that make sorting unnecessary by surfacing verified, ranked evidence instead of unstructured claims.

The system might fail. The metrics might cluster. The “average” candidate problem might prove more intractable than we think. But the alternative—continuing to rely on proxy signals that AI has rendered meaningless—isn’t a viable path forward.

This is the experiment worth running. Not because we know it works. Because we know the current system doesn’t.
