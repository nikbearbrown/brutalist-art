Bear Growls: The Chart Everyone’s Reading Wrong

A conversation with Professor Bear about the most dangerous graph in software engineering

You’ve seen the chart. Perhaps you shared it. The METR graph showing AI task completion times going vertical—what once took humans 7 hours now takes Claude Opus 4.5 a few minutes. The caption declared it “the most important chart in AI.”

So here’s my question: What is that chart actually measuring?

Take a moment. Don’t jump to the obvious answer. What does “time to complete 50% of tasks” actually tell us about the future of software engineering?

Most people I talk to give me one of two responses:

Response A (from technologists): “It shows AI can do junior engineer work now. Exciting times!”

Response B (from junior engineers): “It shows I’m about to be automated out of a job. Time to panic.”

Both responses share the same assumption: that the chart measures replaceability. As if software engineering were just a race to type code faster, and whoever types fastest wins.

But here’s the data that breaks that assumption. In 2024-2025, companies that aggressively adopted AI coding assistants saw something unexpected:

  • 35% of senior engineer time now spent verifying AI output

  • 91% increase in code review time

  • 154% increase in pull request size

  • 59% deployment error rate from AI-generated code

  • And most tellingly: experienced developers were 19.4% slower when required to use AI assistance

Wait—slower? How can that be? The chart shows tasks completing in minutes instead of hours!

Let me pose this differently: What’s the difference between completing a task and owning a task?

The Question Nobody’s Asking

Here’s what the METR chart doesn’t show: the code review. The debugging session at 2am. The architectural decision about which of three approaches to use. The moment when you realize the “correct” implementation will fail catastrophically in production under load.

The chart measures generation speed. But generation was never the bottleneck.

Think about it this way: If I give you a flight simulator that can land a plane perfectly every time under ideal conditions, have I made pilots obsolete? Or have I just eliminated the easy part of flying?

The hard part isn’t landing when everything works. The hard part is landing when engine three fails over the Atlantic. And the only way to develop that judgment is through experience with failures.

So now we arrive at a more uncomfortable question: If AI automates away the 10,000 hours of “grunt work” that traditionally built that judgment, how do you create senior engineers?

You’re probably thinking: “Can’t we just hire experienced engineers and skip the junior positions entirely?”

Let’s examine that answer with actual numbers.

The Math That Executives Are Missing

Employment data from 2024-2025 shows:

  • Software engineers aged 22-25: -13% relative to other cohorts

  • Software engineers aged 35-49: +9%

  • Entry-level postings (≤3 years experience): collapsed from 43% to 28%

  • Junior hiring overall: -73%

Simple algebra, right? If AI can do junior work, cut junior positions. Three fewer juniors at $90K each = $270K saved. The board nods. The decision takes fifteen minutes.

But here’s my question: What happens in 2030 when those senior engineers retire?

Let me make this concrete. If you have:

  • 100 senior engineers today (average age 42)

  • Normal attrition of 10% annually

  • Zero junior hiring from 2024-2026

  • And a 5-year timeline to develop senior judgment

How many senior engineers do you have in 2031?

Even assuming you lose no one early and hire no one new, you’re down 40-50% just from natural attrition. But that’s not the real problem.

The real problem is this: Can you create a 10-year-aged whiskey by throwing money at a distillery today?

The Hollow Organization

You’re beginning to see it now. The structure emerging across the industry:

At the top: Expensive seniors drowning in verification work At the bottom: AI systems generating massive volumes of code
In the middle: Nothing. No one climbing the ladder.

What do we call this?

I call it the Hollow Organization. And here’s what makes it terrifying: by the time you notice the problem, it’s too late to fix it.

Because expertise can’t be instant-manufactured. You can’t hire your way out of a judgment shortage when the judgment doesn’t exist in the market.

Let me show you what’s already happening:

Salary inflation for what’s left:

  • Platform engineers: $182K-$251K (up 15% YoY)

  • Senior architects: $183K-$298K (up 12% YoY)

  • AI/ML specialists: +12% premium over generalists

  • Design verification engineers: $126K-$380K

That last one is fascinating. Verification engineers—the people who check whether code is correct—now command nearly the same salaries as the people who wrote the code in the first place.

What does that tell you about where the value is shifting?

The False Choice

So now you’re facing a decision. Most companies think they have two options:

Option A: Cut junior positions for immediate savings, accept future talent shortage Option B: Keep hiring juniors to write code that AI now writes 10x faster

Both options destroy value. Option A mortgages the future. Option B maintains an obsolete training program—teaching juniors to manually implement code they’ll never write professionally while AI handles those tasks faster.

But what if I told you there’s a third option? One that actually accelerates the development of senior judgment instead of eliminating the pathway to it?

To see it, we need to ask a different question: What were those 10,000 hours of grunt work actually for?

You might say: “To learn syntax. To understand data structures. To build coding skill.”

But that’s not quite right, is it? Here’s how I know: Ask any senior engineer how often they reference their CS textbook when making architectural decisions.

The 10,000 hours weren’t about memorizing syntax. They were about building pattern recognition. The intuition that tells you: “This code looks correct but will fail in production.” The experience that whispers: “This architecture will work for 10,000 users but break at 10 million.”

You developed that intuition by encountering failures. By debugging that race condition at 2am. By watching your “elegant solution” collapse under load.

So here’s the real question: Can you compress 10,000 hours of pattern recognition into a shorter period without sacrificing the depth of judgment?

The Flight Simulator Insight

Consider how pilots develop judgment.

In 1950, a commercial pilot needed 10,000 hours of flight time to encounter the 100 emergency scenarios that build the intuition to land a plane when three engines fail. They developed expertise slowly, through rare organic encounters with danger.

How long does it take today?

Modern pilot training uses flight simulators. A trainee experiences 10,000 emergency scenarios in their first 1,000 hours—engine failures, severe weather, hydraulic malfunctions—all compressed into systematic exposure without risk.

The result: faster expertise development through wisdom compression.

That’s a 1000x acceleration in expertise development.

Now apply this to software engineering:

Traditional path:

  • Write 100,000 lines of code over 3 years

  • Encounter ~100 subtle edge cases organically

  • Wisdom accrual rate: 0.01 insights/hour

AI-augmented path:

  • Review 50-100 AI implementations per week

  • See 10,000+ failure modes systematically in 12 months

  • Wisdom accrual rate: 10 insights/hour

But—and this is critical—only if you deliberately design the training program.

The Judgment Ladder

What if the job isn’t “write code” but “verify correctness”?

Think about what that changes. A junior engineer in 2020 spent their first year writing CRUD operations, debugging CSS, implementing basic APIs. They wrote maybe 20,000-30,000 lines of code and encountered perhaps 50-75 edge cases.

A Verification Specialist in 2026 reviews 50 AI-generated implementations per week. That’s 2,500+ implementations in Year 1. Each implementation represents a different approach to solving a problem—different architectural choices, different failure modes, different security vulnerabilities.

By end of Year 1, they’ve seen more code patterns than a traditional junior saw in three years. But are they ready to be a senior engineer?

No. Of course not. And that’s where people get confused.

The Judgment Ladder isn’t one role—it’s five progressive levels of decision-making authority:

Rung 1: Verification Specialist (Year 0-1)

  • Responsibility: Ensure AI-generated code meets production standards

  • Judgment scope: Bounded—comparing against Gold Standard Library

  • Question they’re answering: “Is this code correct according to our patterns?”

Rung 2: Component Architect (Year 1-2)

  • Responsibility: Design features where AI implements, human evaluates

  • Judgment scope: Architectural—choosing between multiple approaches

  • Question they’re answering: “Which of these three approaches is right for our context?”

Rung 3: System Architect (Year 3-4)

  • Responsibility: Own architectural integrity across multi-team systems

  • Judgment scope: Strategic—second-order effects, scaling, technical debt

  • Question they’re answering: “What breaks when we 10x our scale?”

Rung 4-5: Technical Leader (Year 5+)

  • Responsibility: Organizational strategy and technical culture

  • Judgment scope: Organizational—what to build with AI vs. what requires humans

  • Question they’re answering: “What should we build and why?”

Each rung represents a qualitative shift in the type of judgment required, not just more experience doing the same thing.

Here’s the economic reality:

A Principal Engineer hired externally in 2030 will command $600K-$800K in a constrained market. A Principal Engineer developed through your Judgment Ladder costs $1.2M total from Rung 1 to Rung 5 (5 years × $240K average fully-loaded cost), arrived 3-5 years faster than traditional progression, and has zero poaching risk.

But you can’t start that process in 2029 when the crisis becomes obvious. The lag time is 3-5 years.

The Objection You’re Forming

I can see your objection forming: “Juniors can’t audit code. They don’t know what ‘right’ looks like.”

You’re absolutely correct. Which is precisely why this doesn’t work without infrastructure.

Let me ask you: How does a pilot in their first month know whether they landed the plane correctly? Because the simulator has objective measures of a safe landing—descent rate, runway alignment, brake temperature.

What’s the software equivalent?

The Gold Standard Library: a curated set of reference implementations representing approved patterns for every common operation in your stack.

  • Authentication: OAuth 2.0 + PKCE with refresh token rotation

  • Database queries: Parameterized statements with connection pooling

  • API design: RESTful endpoints with rate limiting and idempotency

  • Error handling: Structured logging with trace IDs

  • Security: Input validation, output encoding, CSP headers

When a Verification Specialist reviews AI-generated authentication code, they’re not evaluating from first principles. They’re asking: “Does this match our OAuth pattern? If it deviates, is there documented justification?”

This transforms verification from “subjective architectural judgment” (requires years of experience) to “pattern matching against known-good examples” (trainable in months).

But here’s the key insight: They’re not just pattern matching. They’re asking why the AI chose this approach. They’re hypothesizing edge cases. They’re reading production logs to see what broke.

By Month 6, they’re not blindly comparing anymore. They’re reasoning about correctness. By Month 12, they’re catching bugs that seniors miss because they’ve seen 10,000 implementations while the senior has seen 500.

That’s the wisdom compression.

The Prisoner’s Dilemma

So if the economic case is this compelling, why are only 6% of companies doing this?

Because of a game theory problem. Watch what happens:

Company A’s decision matrix:

Option 1: Invest $330K developing a junior to Rung 3 over 3 years

  • 40% chance they leave at Year 2 (industry-average attrition)

  • Risk-adjusted cost per retained engineer: $550K

Option 2: Don’t train, poach Rung 3 engineers from companies that do train

  • Market rate: $200K/year base

  • 3-year total: $662K (with 10% annual increases)

The math says training is cheaper. But Company A knows Company B will poach their trained engineers. So the dominant strategy becomes: don’t train, free-ride on others’ investment.

Nash Equilibrium: No one trains. Senior pool depletes. Prices spike.

And that’s exactly what’s happening:

2026 reality: Senior salaries up 8-15% annually
2030 projection: Senior shortage leads to $600K+ salaries for roles that paid $250K in 2025

But here’s what breaks the prisoner’s dilemma: retention mechanisms that reduce attrition from 40% to 15%.

Now internal development is $1.4M cheaper than external hiring. And that calculation doesn’t account for institutional knowledge, cultural fit, or the option value of having bench depth when competitors face crises.

The 6% who figured this out aren’t smarter. They just solved retention through:

  • Stock vesting over 4 years (golden handcuffs)

  • Clear progression paths (internal promotion trumps lateral moves)

  • Learning opportunities (people stay for skill development)

  • Culture of ownership (psychological investment beyond compensation)

The Manufacturing of Struggle

But there’s a deeper problem we haven’t addressed. One that makes this genuinely difficult.

Traditional juniors developed judgment through struggle. Debugging that race condition at 2am. Wrestling with CSS for hours. Discovering why the “obvious” approach fails.

AI removes the struggle. A junior can now prompt “implement authentication with JWT tokens” and receive working code in 30 seconds. No struggle. No debugging. No learning.

This is the “Easy Button Problem”: When the path from problem to solution is frictionless, the brain doesn’t encode the pattern.

You need cognitive load—not too much (overwhelm) or too little (boredom), but optimal difficulty that forces active problem-solving. Learning science calls this “desirable difficulties.”

So how do you manufacture struggle when AI makes everything easy?

Medical residency solved this decades ago. Doctors don’t learn from textbooks—they treat patients under supervision. The “struggle” is real (patient care) but bounded (attending physician oversight).

For software engineering:

Sandbox Simulations (Controlled Failure)

Month 1: Verification Specialists work exclusively in sandbox environments with deliberately flawed AI implementations—code that compiles but contains security vulnerabilities, performance issues, subtle logic errors.

Their task: identify all flaws before “deploying to production” (actually staging).

Success metric: 90% flaw detection rate before graduation to production review.

Socratic AI Mentors (Guided Discovery)

When they miss a bug, the system doesn’t tell them the answer. It asks:

“You approved this authentication implementation. What happens if the JWT token expires during a long-running request?”

“This database query works with 100 users. Walk through what happens with 1 million users.”

“The code handles the happy path. What are three failure modes you should test?”

This forces retrieval practice—being forced to reason through problems creates 50% stronger memory encoding than passive review.

Evaluation-Driven Development

Before reviewing code, they must define success criteria:

“This authentication implementation should:

  • Prevent SQL injection (test: input validation with malicious strings)

  • Handle token expiration gracefully (test: artificial timeout scenarios)

  • Rate-limit login attempts (test: automated brute force simulation)

  • Log authentication events (test: verify structured logs exist)”

Then they evaluate whether AI-generated code meets these criteria.

The cognitive task shifts from “Does this look right?” (pattern matching) to “Does this meet explicit requirements?” (systematic verification).

By Month 12, this becomes intuitive. That’s expertise.

What This Means for Monday Morning

You’re convinced. Or at least intellectually persuaded. But what do you actually do?

The answer depends on which side of 2030 you want to be on.

If you cut juniors now, by 2030 you’ll be:

  • Bidding $600K-$800K for external hires

  • Competing for talent that doesn’t exist in sufficient quantities

  • Suffering succession crises as seniors retire with no replacements

  • Drowning in technical debt as overloaded seniors cut corners

If you build the Judgment Ladder now, by 2030 you’ll have:

  • Internal pipeline producing 15-20 Rung 3-4 engineers annually

  • Average cost: $220K (vs. $600K+ external)

  • Zero poaching risk (promoted from within, culturally invested)

  • Institutional knowledge preserved across generations

The companies that start in 2026 will have Rung 3-4 engineers in 2029-2031 precisely when competitors face catastrophic shortages.

But here’s what you need to understand: This isn’t a technology problem. It’s a 10-20-70 problem.

Research from BCG and McKinsey shows AI transformation success depends on:

  • 10%: AI algorithms and model selection

  • 20%: Data infrastructure and technical integration

  • 70%: People, processes, and organizational transformation

The companies achieving 5%+ EBIT impact from AI honor this allocation religiously. The 61% stuck in pilot purgatory invert it—spending 70% on tool evaluation, 20% on technical implementation, 10% on preparing humans to work differently.

Which means the hard part isn’t choosing between GitHub Copilot and Cursor. The hard part is:

  • Redesigning job descriptions for Rung 1-5 roles

  • Training Rung 3+ engineers to mentor verification skills (not coding skills)

  • Building the Gold Standard Library

  • Creating feedback loops so juniors learn from mistakes

  • Establishing retention mechanisms

  • Cultural transformation from “AI replaces humans” to “AI accelerates human judgment”

Time investment: 12-24 months of continuous iteration.

The companies that fail spend 6 months evaluating which AI tool to buy, implement it in 2 weeks, and wonder why adoption stalls.

The companies that succeed spend 2 weeks choosing a tool, then 18 months building organizational capacity to use it effectively.

The Chart We’re Not Graphing

Let me return to where we started: that METR chart showing AI capability going vertical.

What does the chart actually measure? It measures how quickly AI can complete tasks that we can clearly specify and objectively evaluate.

What doesn’t it measure? The judgment to know:

  • When AI is confidently wrong

  • When code that compiles will fail in production

  • When the third architectural option is actually the right one

  • When to accrue technical debt deliberately vs. refactor

  • Who is responsible when the system fails at 2am

That judgment cannot be automated. Not because the technology isn’t powerful enough, but because accountability requires a human signature.

Someone must own what happens when it fails. That someone is not the AI.

The vertical line on the METR chart shows capability increasing. The hidden line—the one not graphed—shows judgment capacity decreasing as pipelines collapse.

Here’s my final question for you: Five years from now, when that senior engineer you’re counting on decides to retire, who replaces them?

If your answer is “we’ll hire someone,” I have bad news. By 2030, you’ll be competing with every other company that gave the same answer. And the price will reflect a scarcity you helped create.

If your answer is “the Rung 3 engineer we’re developing now,” then you understand something that 94% of companies are missing:

The most important chart in AI went vertical. The most important decision in talent strategy is happening right now.

The ladder is waiting.

What step are you on?
