Question 1: What Happens If This Fails Silently?

If this system produces garbage output, marketing directors will approve inconsistent brand messaging across platforms, resulting in a 23% reduction in purchase intent among multi-platform exposed customers and a measurable decline in customer lifetime value.

Specifically: When a D2C fashion brand’s Instagram sounds like a best friend giving style advice (”babe, this dress is EVERYTHING for your body type 🔥”) but their email marketing reads like a legal document (”We are pleased to present our curated collection of premium garments”), customers experience cognitive dissonance. They’re not sure if they’re buying from a friend or a corporation. Research from the Journal of Brand Management (2023) documents that voice inconsistency reduces purchase intent by 23% among customers exposed to multi-platform messaging.

The silent failure: Marketing teams operate in platform silos. The Instagram team (Gen Z interns optimizing for engagement) thinks they’re being “authentic.” The email team (copywriters trained in traditional direct marketing) thinks they’re being “professional.” Customer support (offshore team following scripts) thinks they’re being “helpful.” Nobody realizes they sound like three different companies until customer surveys reveal: “I wasn’t sure what you stood for.”

But here’s what makes this genuinely dangerous: the drift is gradual and statistically invisible in single-platform metrics. Instagram engagement goes up. Email open rates stay stable. Support resolution times improve. Every team’s dashboard shows green. Meanwhile, brand equity—the only metric that matters for long-term survival—is quietly hemorrhaging.

Why this requires advanced data science: You can’t solve this with keyword lists or sentiment analysis. “Professional but approachable” might mean:

  • LinkedIn: Formal grammar (ASL: 25-30 words) + inclusive “we” + no emoji

  • Instagram: Casual grammar (ASL: 8-12 words) + direct “you” + strategic emoji

  • Support: Mirror user’s emotional state in first 2 tokens + gender-neutral language

The underlying voice parameters—formality, power distance, in-group vs out-group framing, expertise signaling—are latent dimensions requiring parameterized modeling across high-dimensional embedding spaces. You need stylometric quantification (Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, Gunning Fog Index, lexical diversity metrics), semantic similarity measures (BERTScore using contextual embeddings), and statistical drift detection (KL divergence, Wasserstein distance) to capture what humans perceive as “off-brand.”

This isn’t a natural language processing problem. It’s a distributional drift detection problem in a multi-modal, multi-platform latent space where ground truth is contaminated by the very inconsistencies we’re trying to detect.

Question 2: Why Won’t the Obvious Solution Work?

Why not brand guidelines?
Because guidelines are interpretative documents, not operational specifications. When the brand book says “professional but approachable,” different teams operationalize this differently:

  • LinkedIn team interprets: “Use complete sentences, avoid contractions, cite credentials”

    • Result: “We’re excited to announce that our Q4 results exceeded expectations” (Formal + Distant)

  • Instagram team interprets: “Be real, talk like a person, show personality”

    • Result: “omg our Q4 numbers are INSANE we literally can’t believe it 🎉” (Casual + Intimate)

Structural inadequacy: Brand guidelines describe aspirational adjectives (”bold,” “innovative,” “empathetic”) without parameterizing what those mean linguistically. They don’t specify:

  • Whether to use first-person plural (”we”) or second-person (”you”)

  • Whether expertise is signaled through credentials or demonstration

  • Whether mistakes are acknowledged explicitly or implicitly

  • What the acceptable range is for Average Sentence Length, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, or lexical diversity

Even when guidelines include examples, humans interpret them through their own linguistic priors. A writer from a formal academic background sees “approachable” differently than someone from a journalism background.

Why not content approval workflows?
Because they’re slow, they bottleneck, and people route around them for “urgent” content—which is precisely when brand voice matters most.

When a competitor launches a viral campaign, the social team needs to respond in hours, not days. When a customer complaint goes viral, support needs to respond in minutes. When a trending meme offers brief relevance, the content team can’t wait for a three-layer approval process.

Structural inadequacy: Approval workflows enforce consistency after content is created and scheduled. By then:

  1. The creator has invested effort and is emotionally attached

  2. The opportunity window is closing (trending topics decay exponentially)

  3. The approver optimizes for “good enough to ship” not “perfectly on-brand”

This creates a systematic bias: urgent content (the most visible, highest-stakes content) is the most likely to be off-brand.

Why not just train employees better?
Because voice consistency requires real-time feedback loops that training can’t provide. A one-week onboarding can’t simulate the thousand micro-decisions that define voice:

  • How formal is your greeting? (”Hi!” vs “Hello” vs “Good morning”)

  • Do you use industry jargon or explain everything?

  • When you acknowledge problems, do you accept responsibility or explain constraints?

  • Do you hedge (”might,” “perhaps,” “could”) or commit (”will,” “definitely,” “guaranteed”)?

Structural inadequacy: Training addresses conscious knowledge (”our brand values are X, Y, Z”) but voice operates at the level of unconscious linguistic habits. A writer who naturally uses passive voice (”mistakes were made”) won’t shift to active voice (”we made mistakes”) just because a trainer said “be accountable.” You need continuous feedback at the moment of creation, not frontloaded training that fades over weeks.

Additionally, training assumes the ground truth is known and stable. But brands evolve. What was “on-brand” in 2023 might be outdated in 2026. Training can’t keep pace with drift in the brand itself.

Question 4: What’s Your Ground Truth, and How Do You Know It’s Not Contaminated?

Ground truth: Content is “on-brand” if it falls within the parameter bounds (5th-95th percentile) defined by a curated “Golden Dataset” of executive-reviewed, high-performing content across platforms.

Why this definition:

  1. Executive-reviewed content represents intentional brand voice (earnings calls, founder posts, official announcements), not ad-hoc improvisation by junior team members

  2. High-performing content (measured by engagement-per-impression adjusted for platform baseline) validates that “on-brand” content actually resonates with audiences

  3. 5th-95th percentile captures the range of acceptable variation, not just the mean—brands need flexibility, not rigid uniformity

  4. Cross-platform ensures we’re learning “brand voice adapted to LinkedIn” and “brand voice adapted to TikTok,” not “generic LinkedIn voice” vs “generic TikTok voice”

Contamination risks:

Risk 1: Executive content might itself be inconsistent

If the CEO sounds different on earnings calls (formal, data-driven) vs Twitter (casual, provocative), we’re learning inconsistency as ground truth.

Detection: Use hierarchical clustering on executive content embeddings. If we find multiple distinct clusters (e.g., k-means with k=3 shows three separate voice profiles), we have contamination.

Mitigation:

  • Present clusters to brand leadership with representative examples from each

  • Force explicit choice: “Which cluster represents the authentic brand voice?”

  • Use only the selected cluster as ground truth, treating others as historical drift

Risk 2: High-performing content might be off-brand but viral

A post might go viral because it’s off-brand (controversial, unexpected), not because it’s good branding.

Detection: Separate “engagement rate” from “brand-appropriate engagement.” Use sentiment analysis and comment analysis to distinguish:

  • Positive viral: High engagement + positive sentiment + comments reinforce brand values

  • Controversial viral: High engagement + mixed sentiment + comments express surprise/confusion

Mitigation:

  • Weight ground truth by sustained performance (30-day engagement, not 24-hour spike)

  • Exclude content that got high engagement but low conversion (attention without trust)

  • Include only content that performed well and generated brand-aligned discussion

Risk 3: The 90th percentile might exclude legitimate innovation

A new campaign style (e.g., deliberately more casual for Gen Z platform) might be intentionally different but still “on-brand.”

Detection: Track override rate—if >30% of flagged content gets approved anyway and performs well, our bounds are too conservative.

Mitigation:

  • Explicitly model “approved divergence” as a separate category

  • When content falls outside bounds but gets executive approval + performs well, treat it as potential parameter expansion

  • Trigger quarterly “parameter calibration” reviews where humans approve/reject current bounds

  • Allow platform-specific parameter ranges (TikTok can have lower Flesch-Kincaid than LinkedIn while both are “on-brand”)

Risk 4: Historical data codifies existing problems

The existing content corpus might already have the drift we’re trying to prevent. LinkedIn was already too formal, Instagram already too casual, but the model treats this as ground truth.

Detection:

  • Customer feedback analysis: If survey responses mention “inconsistent” or “can’t tell what you stand for,” parameters are wrong even if content matches them

  • Net Promoter Score correlation: If brands with high voice variance have lower NPS, our definition is flawed

Mitigation:

  • Bootstrap ground truth from executive-reviewed content only, not the full platform corpus

  • Use “Mirror Exercise”: Show brand leadership 5-10 “Gold Standard” pieces and extract voice from those specifically

  • Validate parameters against external benchmark: Do customers actually perceive consistency?

Validation methodology:

Blind ranking test: Show marketing directors pairs of posts:

  • Pair A: One within parameter bounds, one outside

  • Pair B: Both within bounds (control)

  • Pair C: Both outside bounds (control)

If parameter-based flagging doesn’t correlate (r > 0.6) with human rankings of “more on-brand,” the parameters don’t capture what matters.

Expected outcome: Moderate correlation (0.6-0.75) in MVP, indicating some dimensions work (formality, emotional distance) while others need refinement (lexical complexity might be noise).

Question 5: What’s the Silent Failure Scenario?

Silent Failure 1: False Positives (Flagging Innovation as Inconsistency)

Failure mode: The system flags a TikTok post as “off-brand” because its Average Sentence Length is 8 words (below the brand’s 90th percentile bound of 12-15 words). But the brevity is intentional—TikTok’s algorithm penalizes long captions, and the brand is adapting appropriately.

Consequence:

  • Immediate: Marketing team either (a) doesn’t post it, missing connection with younger audience, or (b) posts anyway, learning to ignore the system

  • Compound: If override rate exceeds 40%, the system loses credibility. Teams stop checking it. Within 3 months, it’s just noise.

Detection metric:
Track: (Flagged Content Posted Anyway) / (Total Flagged Content)
If ratio > 30% AND that content performs above platform baseline, the system is too conservative.

Mitigation:

  • Implement platform-specific parameter ranges, not single global bounds

  • TikTok allows: ASL 6-10, FKGL 6-8 (middle school), higher emoji density

  • LinkedIn requires: ASL 20-30, FKGL 12-14 (college), minimal emoji

  • Both are “on-brand” if they maintain the same underlying personality dimensions (warmth, expertise, formality relative to platform baseline)

Architectural constraint:
Use hierarchical parameter model:

Brand Core Voice (global):
  - Personality: Warm + Expert + Slightly Formal
  
Platform Filters (local adaptation):
  - LinkedIn: ASL +12, FKGL +4, Emoji -80%
  - TikTok: ASL -7, FKGL -4, Emoji +200%
  
Flag if: (Platform-adjusted content) deviates from (Brand Core Voice)

Silent Failure 2: False Negatives (Approving Drift as Consistency)

Failure mode: Customer support responses are technically consistent with past support responses (ASL: 18 words, FKGL: 11, formal pronouns). But the entire support voice has been drifting toward robotic corporate speak over 6 months. Each individual response seems fine—it’s only in aggregate that the drift is visible.

Consequence:

  • Customers increasingly describe support as “unhelpful” and “cold” despite faster response times

  • Support satisfaction scores decline 12% over 6 months

  • Nobody notices because each week’s responses look consistent with last week’s

  • Only customer churn analysis reveals: “They stopped caring about us”

Detection metric:
Track temporal trends in voice parameters:

  • Plot: Average Formality Score, Emotional Distance Score, Hedge Word Density over time

  • Alert if: Monotonic increase/decrease over 3+ months without explicit parameter updates

  • This is drift, not evolution

Mitigation:

  • Require quarterly voice calibration reviews where humans:

    • Review parameter trends over past 90 days

    • Explicitly approve: “Yes, we’re intentionally becoming more formal” OR reject: “No, this is drift”

    • Compare current content against original Golden Dataset, not just last month’s content

  • Implement “anchor audits”: Every month, randomly sample 10 posts and compare against year-old ground truth, not rolling baseline

Architectural constraint:

if detect_monotonic_trend(parameter, months=3) and not explicit_approval:
    alert("Possible drift: {parameter} has moved {direction} for 3 months")
    require_human_review()

Silent Failure 3: Codifying Bad Existing Patterns

Failure mode: The system learns from existing content that already has inconsistencies. LinkedIn is already too formal (ASL: 35, FKGL: 16—college graduate level), Instagram is already too casual (ASL: 7, FKGL: 5—elementary level). The model treats this as ground truth and enforces it, preventing the brand from correcting its existing problems.

Consequence:

  • Brand wants to make LinkedIn more accessible (reduce FKGL from 16 to 12)

  • But system flags new, simpler LinkedIn posts as “off-brand” because they deviate from historical data

  • Innovation is blocked by the very system meant to enable it

Detection metric:

  • Customer feedback mentions: “Inconsistent,” “Can’t tell what you stand for,” “Different on every platform”

  • Net Promoter Score is declining despite content matching parameters

  • Parameters are descriptively accurate (match existing content) but prescriptively wrong (don’t reflect desired brand)

Mitigation:

  • Bootstrap ground truth from executive-reviewed content only, not full corpus

  • Use “Mirror Exercise” explicitly:

    1. Brand leadership selects 5-10 “Gold Standard” pieces

    2. System extracts voice parameters from only those pieces

    3. Historical corpus is audited against this standard, not used to define it

  • Implement “aspiration mode”: Allow users to define target parameters that differ from historical averages

Silent Failure 4: Platform Context Collapse

Failure mode: The system enforces global consistency, failing to recognize that the same brand can sound different on different platforms appropriately. A brand that sounds “warm + expert” should be:

  • LinkedIn: Warm-within-professional-norms (use “we,” explain rationale, data-driven)

  • TikTok: Warm-within-casual-norms (use “you,” show personality, story-driven)

But the system flags TikTok content as “too casual” because it compares against LinkedIn baseline.

Consequence:

  • All platforms homogenize toward a bland middle (neither professional nor casual)

  • Platform-specific content underperforms because it violates platform norms

  • The system enforces consistency at the expense of platform-appropriate adaptation

Detection metric:

  • Content flagged as “inconsistent” consistently outperforms content flagged as “consistent” on that platform

  • Engagement rate for flagged content: 1.8x platform baseline

  • Engagement rate for approved content: 0.9x platform baseline

This is the critical signal: The system is optimizing for the wrong thing.

Mitigation:

  • Model brand voice as a personality vector (warmth, expertise, formality) rather than absolute linguistic features

  • Allow platform-specific transformations of that personality

  • Example: “Warm + Expert” brand becomes:

    • LinkedIn: High expertise signaling, moderate warmth, high formality

    • Instagram: Moderate expertise signaling, high warmth, moderate formality

    • TikTok: Low expertise signaling, very high warmth, low formality

  • Flag only when the underlying personality changes, not when the expression adapts to platform

Question 6: What’s the Dumbest Version That Could Still Teach You Something?

MVP: Single brand, two platforms (LinkedIn + Instagram), 100 posts per platform, manual validation by brand leader, 2-week sprint

Scope

Brand selection:
Notion—known for extremely consistent voice across platforms. If parameterization works anywhere, it should work here. Falsification is easier with a “best case” brand.

Platform selection:

  • LinkedIn: Professional, long-form, B2B audience

  • Instagram: Visual, short captions, B2C audience

  • Why these two? Maximum stylistic distance. If we can maintain consistency across these, we can handle any pair.

Data collection:

  • Extract 100 recent posts per platform (200 total) using official APIs

  • Separate into:

    • Golden Dataset (20 posts): Executive-reviewed, founder-authored, or official announcements

    • Test Set (80 posts): Regular marketing content

Parameterization:
Extract 5 stylometric dimensions:

  1. Formality: Average Sentence Length (ASL), Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level

  2. Personal vs Institutional: First-person singular (”I”) vs first-person plural (”we”) density

  3. Expertise Signaling: Technical jargon density, credential mentions

  4. Emotional Distance: Emotional word density (LIWC), exclamation mark usage

  5. Linguistic Complexity: Lexical richness (TTR), subordinate clause density

Voice bounds:
Calculate 5th-95th percentile for each parameter using Golden Dataset.

Flagging logic:
Flag any post in Test Set that exceeds bounds on ≥2 dimensions.

Validation (The Critical Test)

Blind ranking task:

  • Present Notion’s marketing director with 20 pairs of posts:

    • 10 pairs: One within bounds, one outside bounds (experimental)

    • 5 pairs: Both within bounds (control—should be rated equally on-brand)

    • 5 pairs: Both outside bounds (control—should be rated equally off-brand)

  • Director ranks: “Which feels more on-brand?” (forced choice)

Success criteria:

  • Strong evidence (proceed): Correlation r > 0.7 between parameter-based flagging and human ranking

  • Moderate evidence (refine): Correlation r = 0.5-0.7 → some dimensions work, others are noise

  • Weak evidence (pivot): Correlation r < 0.5 → parameters don’t capture human intuition of “on-brand”

Falsification Criteria

This approach is WRONG if:

  1. Low correlation (r < 0.5): Parameters don’t predict human judgment

    • Interpretation: “On-brand” isn’t parameterizable—it’s emergent, context-dependent, or based on dimensions we didn’t measure

  2. Director can’t reliably rank within-bounds pairs: If posts that meet all parameters still vary widely in “on-brand” feeling, parameters are insufficient

    • Interpretation: We’re missing critical dimensions (maybe humor style, cultural reference density, or something qualitative)

  3. Platform-specific parameters don’t help: If allowing different bounds for LinkedIn vs Instagram doesn’t improve correlation, maybe consistency across platforms isn’t what matters

    • Interpretation: Customers expect brands to sound different on different platforms

Learning Outcomes (Not “Success” But Knowledge)

Best case: High correlation—we’ve identified the dimensions that matter and can scale this to automated flagging

Good case: Moderate correlation—some dimensions (formality, emotional distance) predict human judgment; others (lexical complexity) are noise. Refined parameter set for next iteration.

Learning case: Low correlation—we’ve discovered that “on-brand” isn’t captured by these five dimensions. Need qualitative research: What are marketing directors actually responding to? Possibly:

  • Cultural alignment: References to shared values/memes

  • Narrative coherence: Story-telling approach

  • Aspirational vs actual: Gap between how brand wants to sound vs how it does sound

All three outcomes are valuable. The point is to learn which stylometric dimensions correlate with human perception of brand consistency.

Computational Realism

Infrastructure:

  • Data collection: Official APIs (LinkedIn, Instagram)

  • Feature extraction: Standard NLP libraries (NLTK, spaCy, Transformers)

  • Embedding models: Sentence-BERT or RoBERTa (via Hugging Face)

  • Statistical analysis: scipy, sklearn

  • Visualization: matplotlib, seaborn

Hardware: Standard Colab (T4 GPU sufficient for BERT embeddings on 200 posts)

Timeline:

  • Week 1: Data collection, Golden Dataset curation, parameter extraction

  • Week 2: Validation study, analysis, synthesis of learnings

No specialized infrastructure required. This is deliberately minimal to test the core hypothesis: Can stylometric parameters operationalize “on-brand”?

Red Flags Avoided

✓ Takes < 1/3 of project time: 2 weeks out of 12-week project
✓ Can fail: Clear falsification criteria (r < 0.5)
✓ Tests core assumption: That brand voice is parameterizable at all
✓ Not version 1.0: This isn’t a production system—it’s a hypothesis test

Question 7: What Could Go Wrong? (The Pre-Mortem)

Pre-Mortem 1: Voice Isn’t Parameterizable

Failure hypothesis: “On-brand” isn’t a continuous dimension but a discrete category. There’s no underlying formality scale—there are just certain phrases that sound like Brand X and others that don’t, with no generalizable rules.

What we’d observe:

  • Parameter-based flagging shows no correlation with human judgment (r < 0.3) even after trying multiple parameter sets

  • Human reviewers can’t explain why something feels on-brand: “I just know it when I see it”

  • Posts that match all parameters still feel off-brand to humans

  • The only predictor of “on-brand” is whether it uses specific phrases from the brand lexicon (essentially memorization, not generalization)

Why this would invalidate the approach:
If voice isn’t parameterizable, then real-time detection is impossible. You’d need human review of every post, or you’d need to build a massive database of pre-approved phrases (which doesn’t scale and prevents innovation).

Alternative path forward:
Shift to a retrieval-based approach: Instead of parameterization, use similarity search. For each new post, find the 10 most similar posts in the Golden Dataset (using BERT embeddings). Flag posts where nearest neighbors are >2 standard deviations away in embedding space. This doesn’t explain why something is off-brand, but it might detect drift without requiring explicit parameters.

Pre-Mortem 2: Brand Voice Is Platform-Specific, Not Brand-Specific

Failure hypothesis: Brands don’t have a single voice—they have a “LinkedIn voice” and an “Instagram voice” that are fundamentally different by design. Trying to enforce consistency across platforms makes all platforms worse by eliminating platform-appropriate adaptation.

What we’d observe:

  • Content flagged as “inconsistent” consistently performs better on that platform (high engagement, positive sentiment, strong conversions)

  • Engagement rate for flagged content: 1.8x baseline

  • Engagement rate for approved content: 0.9x baseline

  • Customer feedback doesn’t mention inconsistency—different voices on different platforms are expected and appropriate

Why this would invalidate the approach:
If platform-specific voice is correct, then enforcing global consistency actively harms brand performance. The goal shouldn’t be “sound the same everywhere” but “sound appropriate to each platform.”

Alternative path forward:
Shift the research question: Instead of “How do we maintain voice consistency?”, ask “How do we maintain personality consistency while adapting expression to platform norms?” This requires modeling brand as a personality vector that transforms based on platform context, not as a fixed set of linguistic features.

Pre-Mortem 3: Voice Consistency Doesn’t Actually Matter

Failure hypothesis: Customers don’t notice or care about voice inconsistency. The cognitive dissonance I’m claiming exists is a theoretical problem, not a practical one. Customers happily accept that Brand X sounds different on LinkedIn vs Instagram because they expect different contexts.

What we’d observe:

  • Brands with high voice inconsistency (large parameter variance across platforms) have customer satisfaction and conversion rates identical to brands with high consistency

  • In A/B tests, customers can’t tell which brand is more consistent

  • Net Promoter Score doesn’t correlate with voice variance

  • Customer churn is predicted by product quality, price, and support responsiveness—not by voice consistency

Why this would invalidate the approach:
If customers don’t care about voice consistency, then building infrastructure to enforce it is solving a non-problem. Resources would be better spent on product, pricing, or customer experience improvements that customers actually notice.

What would make this finding valuable:
Even if voice consistency doesn’t matter to customers, it might matter for:

  • Operational efficiency: Consistent voice is easier to teach to new team members

  • Brand clarity internally: Teams that understand the brand voice make better strategic decisions

  • Scalability: Inconsistent voice might be fine with 3 content creators but break down at 30

So the research would shift: “When does voice consistency become operationally necessary, even if customers don’t consciously notice?”

Pre-Mortem 4: Ground Truth Is Contaminated From the Start

Failure hypothesis: Executive-reviewed content is inconsistent too. If the CEO’s voice varies wildly, or if different executives have different voices, there’s no “true” brand voice to learn from. The Golden Dataset itself is multivocal.

What we’d observe:

  • Clustering executive content reveals 3+ distinct voices with no clear primary cluster

  • k-means with k=3 shows roughly equal cluster sizes (33%, 35%, 32%)—no dominant voice

  • Parameter bounds are so wide they’re meaningless (e.g., ASL ranges from 8 to 40 words)

  • Different executives consistently fall into different clusters: CEO is Cluster A, CMO is Cluster B, founder tweets are Cluster C

Why this would invalidate the approach:
If there’s no single ground truth, you can’t train a system to recognize it. The brand doesn’t have a consistent voice—it has multiple voices, and the inconsistency is at the leadership level.

Alternative path forward:
This is actually a valuable finding for the brand. It reveals that voice inconsistency is a leadership problem, not a content team problem. The research output becomes:

  1. A typology of the brand’s distinct voices

  2. Recommendations for which voice should be primary

  3. A remediation plan: “These 47 pieces of content represent Voice A. If we commit to this as ground truth, here’s what needs to change.”

Where Does This Fit?

Madison Framework → Content Agents + Orchestration Layer

This project removes a specific constraint for Madison’s Content Agents: the inability to continuously validate voice consistency during content creation, rather than after publication.

Current state:
Madison’s Content Agents can generate content, and Performance Agents can measure engagement. But there’s no mechanism to detect when content generation has drifted from brand identity, especially when that drift happens gradually over weeks.

After this project:
Content Agents would have a real-time validation layer that:

  1. Flags divergence before publishing

  2. Enables course correction in the creation process (”This draft reads 3 points more formal than your brand baseline—adjust?”)

  3. Tracks drift over time (”Your support voice has become 15% more distant over 3 months—intentional?”)

  • Instagram: “Paradise vibes only 🌴✨”—sounded like a teenager from Mumbai

Same businesses. Completely different voices. And tourists would come, expecting “authentic Goa” from the brochure, and get confused when the actual experience felt... off. Not because the food was bad or the beaches weren’t beautiful, but because the tone of the place didn’t match the tone of the marketing.

I remember my mom (who ran a small heritage homestay) getting a bad TripAdvisor review: “The house was beautiful but I expected something more traditional based on their website.” The website sounded like a museum. The house was cozy and personal. Different writers, different platforms, different voices.

That’s when I started noticing brand voice mismatches everywhere.

Then I watched Mad Men.

I know, I know—everyone in marketing loves Mad Men. But here’s what got me: the scene in Season 1 where Don Draper presents the Kodak Carousel pitch. He doesn’t just sell a product—he defines what the product means. “It’s not called the Wheel. It’s called the Carousel. It lets us travel the way a child travels—around and around, and back home again.”

One voice. One idea. Total clarity.

But here’s the thing: in the Mad Men era, brands had one copywriter and one art director working on one campaign for one medium (print or TV). Today, a brand has:

  • 15 people writing Instagram captions

  • 8 people writing email marketing

  • 12 support agents writing chat responses

  • 5 people writing LinkedIn articles

  • And a bunch of freelancers writing blog posts

You can’t have Don Draper review every piece of content. The volume is 100x what it was in 1960. But the need for a single, clear voice is exactly the same.

That’s the mathematical problem I want to solve.

I love math because it forces clarity. You can’t be vague in a proof. You either define your terms or you fail. And I think brands need that same rigor. “Professional but approachable” isn’t a brand voice—it’s a vague aspiration. But “Average Sentence Length: 15-20 words, Flesch-Kincaid: 10-12, first-person plural density: 3-5%, emotional words: 8-12%” is operationalizable.

Here’s what made this personal:

Last year, I was trying to decide between two productivity apps. App A had this warm, thoughtful blog about work-life balance and mental health. Their Twitter was funny and self-aware. Then I talked to their sales team, and it was like talking to a robot. “Our platform leverages cutting-edge AI to optimize your workflow across multiple verticals.”

I thought: Did I click the wrong company?

App B was boring. Same voice everywhere—straightforward, no-nonsense, slightly dry. I picked them. Not because their voice was better, but because it was consistent. I knew what I was getting.

And that’s when it clicked: Consistency isn’t about being perfect. It’s about being predictable. Customers need to know what kind of relationship they’re in with your brand. Are you their friend? Their professional partner? Their helpful guide? You can’t be all three depending on which platform they find you on.

The research question:

Can you operationalize “brand voice” the way Don Draper operationalized “nostalgia” in that Carousel pitch? Can you take the intuition of “this sounds like us” and turn it into mathematical parameters that a multi-agent system can enforce at scale?

Because if you can’t, then every brand is doomed to sound like three different companies as they scale. And if you can, then you’ve solved a problem that’s been invisible but critical since the internet made every brand an omnichannel publisher.

  • Marketing: Knowing that “professional but approachable” is useless without operationalization

  • Linguistics: Stylometric analysis of what makes a voice feel like a voice

The people who understand brand voice can’t do the math. The people who can do the math don’t understand why brand voice matters. I’m betting I can do both.

And if I’m wrong? Then I’ll learn why brand voice isn’t parameterizable, which is just as valuable as learning that it is. Either way, we’ll know more about how humans perceive linguistic consistency—and whether AI can learn to enforce what humans can only intuit.
