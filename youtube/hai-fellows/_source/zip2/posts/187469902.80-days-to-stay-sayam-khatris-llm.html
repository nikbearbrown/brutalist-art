The Stakes

I’ve been staring at this number for weeks: 28,800. That’s how many funded startups in our dataset have raised money but never sponsored an H-1B visa. Not because they can’t—because they’ve never been asked.

[Professor’s Note: Good framing. You immediately establish the delta between “no sponsorship history” and “won’t sponsor.” Most students would miss this distinction. The real insight is that absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence. But let’s talk about what breaks this assumption: What if these 28,800 companies specifically avoid H-1B sponsorship due to legal complexity, cost aversion, or hiring philosophy? Your dataset doesn’t tell you why they haven’t sponsored. Have you considered surveying even a small sample to validate that “haven’t been asked” is actually the blocking variable?]

Our project goal is concrete: help at least one person get a job that saves their visa status within 80 days. We’ve merged SEC Form D filings, DOL Labor Condition Applications, and USCIS H-1B statistics into a single dataset of 30,370 companies. The data engineering worked. The entity resolution—fuzzy matching company names across three government databases—was brutal, but we got it done.

Now we hit the hard part: turning “ACME TECHNOLOGIES INC” into a real website humans can actually use.

[Professor’s Note: Entity resolution across government datasets is genuinely hard—good work there. But you’re about to learn that the last mile of data enrichment is often harder than the first ninety-nine. Here’s why: the SEC filing gives you a legal entity name. The website belongs to a brand. These are often different. “ACME TECHNOLOGIES INC” might do business as “TechForward” and have acmetech.io as their domain. Your agent needs to handle legal name → DBA → domain mapping, which is a three-layer problem, not a two-layer one.]

Why This Can’t Fail Quietly

If my extraction agent returns wrong links, Nidhi’s team—doing manual validation—wastes time contacting the wrong companies. Users lose trust before we even launch. This isn’t a Kaggle competition where 80% accuracy gets you an A. Wrong website data breaks the entire chain of trust.

**[Professor’s Note: Correct framing of production ML vs. academic ML. But you need to get more specific about failure modes. What does “wrong” mean? There’s a taxonomy here:

  • Type 1: Confident wrong match (worst case—passes validation, pollutes dataset)

  • Type 2: No match found (annoying but safe—human can research manually)

  • Type 3: Multiple plausible matches returned (honest uncertainty—human reviews)

  • Type 4: Correct company, outdated/defunct website (tricky—looks right but isn’t useful)

Your current plan treats all errors equally. They’re not. Build your evaluation framework around this taxonomy.]**

The “Just Use X” Graveyard

“Use the scraping pipeline you already built.”

We did. It generates URLs from company names and checks if they’re live. Problem: a live website isn’t the right website. Too many false positives.

[Professor’s Note: This is where most students would stop and say “good enough.” You correctly identified that HTTP 200 ≠ correctness. But your current solution—LLM-based extraction—introduces a different problem: confident hallucination. The scraper gives you false positives. The LLM gives you plausible-sounding wrong answers. Neither tells you when it’s guessing. Have you considered a hybrid approach where the LLM outputs a confidence score AND you validate against structured data sources like DNS WHOIS records to check entity alignment?]

“Use Crunchbase/LinkedIn.”

Coverage gaps for small startups and staffing firms—which happen to be major H-1B sponsors. Also expensive at 30K+ companies.

[Professor’s Note: LinkedIn Company Pages actually have decent coverage for staffing firms specifically because recruiters live there. Have you tested whether LinkedIn’s free search API (limited but available) could serve as a validation layer rather than primary source? You don’t need to pay for Crunchbase if you’re using it to validate, not discover. Run your LLM results through LinkedIn search and flag mismatches. That’s a cheap way to catch hallucinations.]

“Manual lookup.”

Doesn’t scale. We need automated extraction first, human review for edge cases.

Task                    Human    Agent    Why
─────────────────────────────────────────────────

**[Professor’s Note: Your division of labor assumes the LLM is good at “matching.” This is the core gamble. LLMs are great at fuzzy pattern matching but terrible at deterministic verification. Here’s what I’d add to this table:

Verify legal entity alignment    ✓        Human spot-checks samples
Generate audit trail                   ✓  Agent logs reasoning + sources
Flag confidence boundaries             ✓  Agent knows what it doesn't know

Without these, you’re building a black box that generates plausible garbage at scale.]**

Ground Truth and the Validation Trap

Correct = website belongs to the same legal entity that filed the SEC/DOL paperwork.

What could go wrong:

  • Name collisions (20 companies called “Delta Systems”)

  • Subsidiary vs. parent confusion

  • Defunct companies with parked domains

Validation plan: manually check 200 results—mix of known sponsors (easy) and unknown companies (hard).

**[Professor’s Note: Your validation plan has a critical flaw: selection bias. You’re sampling results the agent successfully returned. What about the companies where it returned nothing? Or returned something with low confidence? Those might be your most important cases—the staffing firms and small startups that actually sponsor but have minimal web presence.

Here’s a better validation design:

  1. Stratified sampling: Sample proportionally across company size, industry, and whether they have existing H-1B history

  2. Include failures: Manually research 50 companies where the agent returned null or error_flag=true

  3. Adversarial validation: Deliberately test edge cases (common names, regional variations, acquired companies)

Also: 200 samples out of 30K is 0.66%. That’s a 95% confidence interval of roughly ±7% on your accuracy estimate. If you’re targeting 70% accuracy, you need to know whether you’re actually at 65% or 75%. Bump your sample to 384 for ±5% margin of error.]**

Silent Failure Scenarios I’m Watching For

Confident wrong match: LLM finds a legit-looking “ABC Technologies” website but it’s a different company in a different state. Looks correct, passes validation, pollutes the dataset.

Staffing agency blind spot: Many H-1B sponsors are IT staffing firms with minimal web presence. Agent might consistently return LinkedIn/Glassdoor instead of official sites, or nothing at all.

**[Professor’s Note: You’ve identified the two failure modes I’d be most worried about. Here’s what you’re missing:

Acquisition amnesia: Company was acquired. Old website redirects to parent company. Your agent returns the parent. But the legal entity that filed the Form D is the subsidiary. Is that correct or not? (Answer: depends on whether the parent is now the sponsoring entity.)

Domain squatting: Company went under. Domain was bought by SEO spam site. Looks professional, passes HTTP check, but it’s not the company.

Regional franchises: “ABC Staffing Inc” in California is different from “ABC Staffing Inc” in Texas. Both have websites. Your SEC data has one, DOL has the other. Which do you return?

Add these to your test cases.]**

How I’m catching these:

  • LLM outputs reasoning for each match (auditable)

  • Stores alternative URLs when uncertain

  • Retries with cleaned company names if first search fails

**[Professor’s Note: Auditable reasoning is good but insufficient. Reasoning can sound plausible and still be wrong. Add:

  • Cross-validation: If you find a company website, scrape their “About” page and check if it mentions the executives listed in the SEC filing

  • Temporal alignment: Check domain registration date against SEC filing date (if domain was registered 5 years after the funding round, something’s off)

  • Negative signals: Flag if top search results include “lawsuit,” “bankruptcy,” “acquired,” or other status changes]**

The MVP Test

500 companies:

  • 100 with existing H-1B history (baseline)

  • 400 without (actual use case)

Fail condition: <70% accuracy on manual review of 50 samples.

**[Professor’s Note: This MVP is well-designed. You’re testing on the hard cases (the 400 unknowns) which is correct. But your pass/fail threshold is backwards. Here’s what I’d do:

Success criteria:

  • ≥90% accuracy on known sponsors (proves the approach works)

  • ≥70% accuracy on unknown companies (proves it generalizes)

  • <5% confident wrong matches across both (proves it fails safely)

If known sponsors hit 90% but unknowns drop to 50%, that doesn’t mean “approach works for easy cases but fails where it matters.” It means your ground truth for “unknown” companies is probably wrong—you don’t actually know what the correct answer is for companies with no H-1B history.

Better test: Take 100 unknown companies, have THREE humans independently research them, and use majority vote as ground truth. If your agent matches human consensus, call it correct. If humans disagree, flag the company as ambiguous and exclude from accuracy calculation.]**

Pre-Mortem: Ways This Completely Fails

  • API costs/limits make 30K companies impractical

  • Gemini gives different results on different runs (already seeing 503 errors under load)

  • 40% of target companies simply don’t have discoverable websites

  • Website links are accurate but users don’t actually use them—wrong enrichment

**[Professor’s Note: Your pre-mortem misses the biggest risk: You solve the technical problem but not the human problem.

Let’s say you achieve 85% accuracy at scale. You deliver a CSV with 25,000 high-confidence website matches. Then what? Does Nidhi’s team manually validate all 25K? Does the user-facing product just display them raw? What happens when a student emails the wrong “ABC Technologies” because your agent matched the wrong one?

The failure mode isn’t “dataset is 70% accurate.” It’s “dataset is 85% accurate but we present it as if it’s 100% certain, so 15% of users waste their time on dead ends and blame us.”

Mitigations:

  1. Surface confidence scores in the UI (let users self-select high-confidence leads)

  2. Add user feedback loop (if student confirms wrong match, propagate that back to training data)

  3. Provide alternative contact methods (LinkedIn profiles for executives even if website is uncertain)

  4. Under-promise: “Here are companies we think match your criteria” not “Here are companies that match”]**

**[Professor’s Note: Personal motivation is clear and valid. But be careful about projecting your experience onto your users. You’re technical enough to know when data is garbage and work around it. Many international students aren’t. They’ll trust whatever the tool gives them because they’re desperate. That’s why accuracy isn’t just a technical metric—it’s an ethical obligation.

Make sure your project documentation sets realistic expectations about what this solves.]**

Next Steps (Professor’s Recommendations)

Immediate (Before Scaling to 30K)

  1. Run the 500-company MVP but fix the validation:

    • Stratify sample across company size, industry, H-1B history

    • Include failures (companies where agent returned null/low confidence)

    • Use 3-human consensus for ground truth on ambiguous cases

    • Track error taxonomy (Type 1-4 from earlier), not just overall accuracy

  2. Add confidence scoring to your output:

json

   {
     "official_url": "...",
     "confidence": 0.87,  // NEW
     "reasoning": "...",
     "sources_checked": ["google.com", "crunchbase.com"],  // NEW
     "domain_registration_date": "2019-03-15",  // NEW
     "executive_name_match": true  // NEW - scraped About page
   }

  1. Stress-test Gemini API:

    • Simulate 10K requests with rate limiting

    • Measure cost per company (target: <$0.05/company = $1,500 total)

    • Build retry logic with exponential backoff

    • Consider fallback to cheaper model (GPT-4 Mini) for bulk work, Gemini for quality checks

Short-term (This Month)

  1. Build the audit trail:

    • Log every LLM call with input/output/timestamp

    • Create dashboard showing error rates by industry/company size

    • Flag patterns (e.g., “All staffing firms return LinkedIn instead of websites”)

  2. Add cross-validation layers:

    • WHOIS lookup to check domain registration against SEC filing date

    • Scrape “About” pages and check for executive name matches

    • Compare against LinkedIn Company Page for entity alignment

    • Flag temporal misalignments (domain registered after funding round)

  3. Test adversarial cases:

    • Companies with common names (”Delta Systems,” “ABC Technologies”)

    • Acquired companies (does domain redirect to parent?)

    • Defunct companies (is domain parked/squatted?)

    • Regional franchises (same name, different states)

Medium-term (Next Month)

  1. Build the user feedback loop:

    • Add “Is this correct?” button in UI next to each company

    • Store corrections and use them to retrain/improve prompts

    • Surface confidence scores so users can prioritize high-confidence leads

  2. Create fallback enrichment strategies:

    • If no official website found, return LinkedIn Company Page

    • If no LinkedIn, return Glassdoor profile

    • Always provide executive names from SEC data even if website uncertain

  3. Document the known unknowns:

    • What % of companies have no discoverable web presence?

    • What % are acquired/defunct?

    • What % have ambiguous matches?

    • Be transparent with users about these limitations

Long-term (If This Works)

  1. Scale intelligently:

    • Don’t run all 30K at once

    • Process in batches of 5K, validate each batch

    • Use early batches to refine prompts and improve accuracy

    • Stop if accuracy drops below threshold

  2. Consider the contact enrichment problem:

    • Website ≠ recruiter contact info

    • Can you scrape careers pages for email/application links?

    • Can you extract executive LinkedIn profiles from SEC data?

    • The full solution is website + contact + application method

  3. Measure what matters:

    • Track: Did a student use this data to get an interview?

    • Track: How many wrong matches got reported?

    • Track: Did we hit the 80-day goal of helping someone save their visa?

    • These metrics matter more than accuracy scores

Final Professor’s Note

Sayam, this is a strong proposal because you’ve thought through failure modes before building. Most students don’t. But you’re about to learn that data enrichment at scale is a systems problem, not just an LLM problem.

Your agent is one component. The real system includes:

  • Data quality monitoring (are results getting worse over time?)

  • User trust management (how do you recover from wrong matches?)

  • Cost optimization (can you afford to run this on 30K companies?)

  • Feedback loops (how do corrections flow back to improve the model?)

Build the simplest version that exposes these system-level questions. A perfect agent that costs $10/company is useless. A 70% accurate agent that costs $0.02/company and surfaces its uncertainty might change someone’s life.

Your goal isn’t to build a perfect dataset. It’s to build a dataset good enough that one person gets a job in 80 days.

Keep that concrete. Measure that ruthlessly. Everything else is engineering details.
