Chapter-by-Chapter Summaries

Introduction: Why I Hated Calculus But Love Statistics

Wheelan opens with confession—a near-failure in high school calculus salvaged by his teacher’s accidental distribution of the wrong exam. The story isn’t nostalgia; it’s contract. Calculus felt “tedious and mechanistic” because no one explained its purpose. Statistics is different. Every concept answers: What’s the point? The promise: statistics explains DNA testing, lottery idiocy, game show strategy. It’s math with stakes. Wheelan positions himself as reformed math-phobe, though his résumé (economics PhD, decades teaching stats) suggests someone who made peace with numbers long ago. Still, the performative skepticism serves its purpose—comfort for the intimidated. He ends with Swedish mathematician Andreas Dunkels: “It’s easy to lie with statistics, but it’s hard to tell the truth without them.” That tension—statistics as both clarifying and obscuring—runs through everything that follows.

What’s the Point?

Descriptive statistics collapse complexity into comprehensibility. The NFL passer rating distills quarterback performance into one number. The Gini Index measures income inequality on a scale from zero to one. Both have limitations—the passer rating can’t capture clutch performance, the Gini Index obscures causes—but both make complex phenomena discussable. Wheelan’s move is subtle: descriptive statistics democratize knowledge while also enabling deception. His mutual fund example (opening twenty funds, advertising only the three that beat the S&P 500) shows how statistics legitimate lies. The tension never resolves. Perhaps it can’t. But the chapter establishes that statistics matter because they simplify without (usually) distorting, and because the ease of calculation (any six-year-old with Excel) means they’re easily misused.

Descriptive Statistics

The chapter examines how we collapse thousands of data points into single figures. Wheelan walks through mean, median, standard deviation—but what makes it work is attention to when each tool succeeds and fails. Mean salary obscures inequality; median reveals it. Standard deviation tells us variation; lack of it in mutual fund returns might signal fraud. His discussion of percentiles is sharp: when your child scores in the third percentile on a reading test, you don’t need to know anything about the test itself—you know 97% of children performed better. The relative statistic matters more than absolute score. Yet this can mislead: US News college rankings assign false precision to subjective judgments, and families make life-altering decisions based on whether a school ranks 14th or 17th. The chapter builds toward indices—composite statistics like the Human Development Index or passer rating—which it treats with appropriate ambivalence. They’re useful shorthand, but construction is arbitrary. Change the weighting, change the ranking.

Deceptive Description: “He’s Got a Great Personality”

True statements can be grotesquely misleading. Joseph McCarthy’s claim about “205” communists gained credibility from specificity, though the list didn’t exist. Wall Street’s Value at Risk models predicted with mathematical precision outcomes that were simply wrong. Wheelan distinguishes precision from accuracy: a golf rangefinder set to meters gives exact but useless distances. Similarly, the “health” of US manufacturing depends on whether you measure output (rising) or employment (falling). Both statistics are accurate; neither tells the complete story alone. His treatment of unit of analysis is instructive: “60% of schools had lower test scores” and “80% of students had higher test scores” can both be true if students showing improvement attend very large schools. The entity being measured matters as much as the measurement. Even brilliant statistics can obscure when construction serves agenda rather than truth.

Correlation: How Does Netflix Know What Movies I Like?

Netflix doesn’t know you, but it knows what you’ve liked, and from that it predicts with eerie accuracy what you’ll enjoy next. The correlation coefficient collapses complex relationships into a single number between -1 and 1, making comparisons possible across wildly different domains. Wheelan’s explanation of how it works—converting measurements to standard deviations from the mean, then calculating relationships across observations—demonstrates the book’s pedagogical approach. Never hide the math, but make it comprehensible by building intuition first. The SAT material is effective: correlation between SAT scores and first-year college GPA is 0.56, same as correlation between high school GPA and college performance, suggesting the SAT adds value primarily when combined with grades. Yet the chapter’s most important move is negative: establishing what correlation cannot do. Students whose families own many cars score better on the SAT, but buying five extra vehicles won’t help. The cars are a proxy for wealth and parental education.

Basic Probability: Don’t Buy the Extended Warranty

Schlitz beer spent $1.7 million on live television taste tests during NFL playoffs, pitting their beer against competitors. It looked risky—what if Schlitz lost? But probability made it safe. If people genuinely can’t distinguish beers, the blind taste test is a coin flip, and roughly half will choose Schlitz. Having Budweiser drinkers pick Schlitz 50% of the time makes Schlitz look great, even though it merely confirms the beers taste identical. Wheelan walks through binomial experiments, expected value, law of large numbers, illustrated through dice, coins, lottery tickets. His Illinois lottery ticket has expected payout of 56 cents, making it “an absolutely miserable way to spend a dollar.” The chapter builds to insurance: any car might crash, but aggregate outcomes are predictable. Law of large numbers means casinos always profit eventually. Probability won’t eliminate uncertainty, but it makes uncertainty navigable.

The Monty Hall Problem

This brief interlude focuses on a single probability puzzle that “has delighted statisticians ever since and perplexed everyone else.” On Let’s Make a Deal, contestants choose one of three doors, Monty reveals a goat behind a door the contestant didn’t pick, then offers a chance to switch. Should you switch? Yes—switching doubles your probability of winning from 1/3 to 2/3. Wheelan provides three different explanations, recognizing different readers need different entry points. The empirical approach: play it 100 times and see. The intuitive approach: switching is equivalent to choosing two doors instead of one. The extreme case: with 100 doors, if Monty opens 98 goat doors, clearly you should switch. The chapter serves as reminder that probability problems often violate intuition. Your gut says switching doesn’t matter—both remaining doors have equal probability. Your gut is wrong.

Problems with Probability: Overconfident Math Geeks Nearly Destroyed the Global Financial System

Wall Street’s pre-2008 Value at Risk models assigned precise probabilities to financial outcomes and catastrophically failed. The models assumed future market movements would resemble past patterns—they didn’t. They offered 99% confidence that losses wouldn’t exceed certain thresholds, but the 1% tail risk contained civilization-threatening outcomes. “This is like an airbag that works all the time,” hedge fund manager David Einhorn observed, “except when you have a car accident.” Wheelan catalogs probability mistakes: confusing independent and dependent events (two jet engines failing isn’t truly independent if birds strike both), gambler’s fallacy (coin has no memory), seeing patterns in randomness (cancer clusters, hot hands in basketball). The section on statistical discrimination is uncomfortable: If data shows Hispanic men aged 18-30 driving red trucks are statistically more likely to be methamphetamine smugglers, should police use that information? The vast majority fitting that profile are innocent, yet the pattern has predictive value. Wheelan doesn’t resolve this tension—he can’t—but makes clear that our growing ability to identify patterns creates ethical challenges statistics alone cannot address.

The Importance of Data: Garbage In, Garbage Out

In spring 2012, researchers published a striking finding: when male fruit flies are spurned repeatedly by female fruit flies, they drown their sorrows in alcohol. The New York Times covered it front page. This research advances understanding of the brain’s reward system, potentially helping with drug and alcohol dependence. But what makes it remarkable isn’t statistics—it’s data. The genius was figuring out how to create sexually-satiated and sexually-frustrated fruit flies, then finding a way to compare their drinking habits. Once researchers did that, the number crunching wasn’t more complicated than a typical high school science fair project. Data are to statistics what a good offensive line is to a star quarterback. Before every star quarterback is a good group of blockers. No amount of fancy analysis can make up for fundamentally flawed data. The chapter catalogs garbage-in-garbage-out: selection bias (Literary Digest poll of 1936), publication bias (vitamin supplements), recall bias (breast cancer patients “remember” high-fat diets they never consumed), survivorship bias (mutual funds closing poorly performing funds), healthy user bias (people who take vitamins are healthy because they’re the kind of people who take vitamins).

The Central Limit Theorem: The LeBron James of Statistics

Suppose you’re searching for a missing marathon bus. You board a broken-down bus near the race. The passengers are, well, very large—average weight over 220 pounds. Is this the marathon bus? No way. If blind taste test between beers is coin flip, roughly half of Mikalob drinkers will pick Schlitz, making Schlitz look good. That’s basic intuition behind central limit theorem. When we add statistical bells and whistles, we can quantify likelihood you’ll be right or wrong. For marathon field of 10,000 runners with mean weight of 155 pounds, there’s less than one in 100 chance that random sample of 60 runners would have mean weight of 220 pounds or more. The central limit theorem tells us that sample means for any population will be distributed roughly as normal distribution around population mean. Most sample means will be very close to population mean. Some will be higher, some lower. But very few will be significantly higher or lower. This enables us to make striking inferences about populations from properly drawn samples.

Inference: Why My Statistics Professor Thought I Might Have Cheated

In spring of senior year, Wheelan took statistics class. He wasn’t devoted during term—also writing senior honors thesis. Regular quizzes: ignored or failed. Midterm: studied a little, did passively well. Then two things happened: finished thesis (copious free time), realized statistics wasn’t difficult. Earned A on final exam. Professor called him in: “You really did much better on the final than the midterm.” Implicit accusation. Wheelan explained, professor seemed content. This anecdote embodies statistical inference: observing pattern or outcome, using probability to determine most likely explanation. Statistics cannot prove anything with certainty. Power derives from observing patterns and using probability to determine most likely explanation. If students in treatment group show marked improvement compared with control group, we determine whether pattern is probably meaningful or probably chance. We never achieve certainty; we quantify likelihood. Statistical inference is marriage of two concepts: data and probability, with help from central limit theorem.

Polling: How We Know 64% of Americans Support the Death Penalty

Late 2011, New York Times ran front page story: a deep sense of anxiety and doubt about future hangs over nation. 89% of Americans distrust government. Two-thirds said wealth should be more evenly distributed. These fascinating figures provided meaningful insight into American opinions one year before presidential race. How do we know all this? We conduct polls. New York Times and CBS do polls. Fact that two competing news organizations collaborate is first clue that conducting methodologically sound national poll isn’t cheap. Poll or survey is inference about opinions of some population based on views expressed by some sample drawn from that population. Power stems from central limit theorem: if we take large representative sample of American voters, we can reasonably assume our sample will look like population from which it’s drawn. If exactly half of American adults disapprove of gay marriage, our best guess about attitudes of representative sample of 1,000 Americans is about half will disapprove. Margin of error of plus or minus 3% is really just 95% confidence interval.

Regression Analysis: The Miracle Elixir

Can stress on job kill you? Yes. There’s compelling evidence that rigor on job can lead to premature death, particularly of heart disease. But it’s not stress you’re imagining. CEOs who routinely make massively important decisions are at significantly less risk than their secretaries. Most dangerous kind of job stress stems from having low control over responsibilities. Several studies of thousands of British civil servants, Whitehall studies, found workers with little control over jobs have significantly higher mortality rate than other workers with more decision-making authority. This isn’t chapter about job stress. Relevant question: how can researchers possibly come to such conclusion? Clearly can’t be randomized experiment. Can’t arbitrarily assign humans to different jobs, force them to work for years, measure who dies at highest rate. Regression analysis is statistical tool that helps deal with this challenge. Specifically, regression allows us to quantify relationship between particular variable and outcome we care about while controlling for other factors. We can isolate effect of one variable while holding effects of other variables constant.

Common Regression Mistakes: The Mandatory Warning Label

Here’s one of most important things to remember when doing research involving regression analysis: Try not to kill anyone. You can put post-it note on computer monitor: do not kill people with your research. Some very smart people have inadvertently violated that rule. Beginning in 1990s, medical establishment coalesced around idea that older women should take estrogen supplements to protect against heart disease, osteoporosis, other conditions associated with menopause. By 2001, some 15 million women were prescribed estrogen believing it would make them healthier. Why? Because research at time suggested this was sensible medical strategy. Longitudinal study of 122,000 women, nurse’s health study, found negative association between estrogen supplements and heart attacks. Then estrogen was subjected to most rigorous form of scientific scrutiny: clinical trials. Clinical trials showed women taking estrogen had higher incidence of heart disease, stroke, blood clots, breast cancer. Starting 2002, doctors advised not to prescribe estrogen. How many women died prematurely or suffered because they took pills doctors prescribed to keep them healthy? Tens of thousands.

Program Evaluation: Will Going to Harvard Change Your Life?

Brilliant researchers in social sciences are brilliant not because they can do complex calculations in heads or win more money on Jeopardy. Brilliant researchers are often individuals or teams who find creative ways to do controlled experiments. To measure effect of any treatment or intervention, we need something to measure it against. How would going to Harvard affect your life? We have to know what happens after you go to Harvard and what happens after you don’t. Obviously can’t have data on both. Yet clever researchers find ways to compare some treatment with counterfactual—what would have happened in absence of that treatment. Does putting more police officers on street deter crime? Challenge: we cannot answer simply by examining whether jurisdictions with high number of police officers per capita have lower rates of crime. Even multiple regression analysis won’t save us. We’ll have serious reverse causality problem. Jonathan Klick and Alexander Tabarrok studied how putting more police on street affects crime rate. Their research strategy made use of terrorism alert system. Washington DC responds to high alert days by putting more officers in certain areas. Number of crimes committed when terrorism threat was orange was roughly 7% lower than when threat level was yellow.

Conclusion: Five Questions That Statistics Can Help Answer

Not long ago, information was much harder to gather and far more expensive to analyze. During Great Depression, there were no official statistics to gauge depth of economic problems. Government didn’t collect official information on GDP or unemployment. Herbert Hoover declared Great Depression was over in 1930 based on inaccurate, outdated data. He told country 2.5 million Americans were out of work. In fact, five million were jobless, and unemployment was climbing by 100,000 every week. Washington was making policy in dark. We’re now awash in data. For most part, that’s good thing. These statistical tools can be used to address significant social challenges. Five important questions whose socially significant answers will involve many tools introduced in this book: What is future of football? What’s causing dramatic rise in autism? How can we identify and reward good teachers and schools? What are best tools for fighting global poverty? Who gets to know what about you?

Bridge

What emerges from Wheelan’s survey isn’t triumphalism about statistics democratizing knowledge—though he believes that—but something more complicated: an argument that numerical literacy has become prerequisite for citizenship itself. You can’t evaluate claims about climate change, vaccine safety, or economic policy without some grasp of probability and inference. You can’t recognize when you’re being manipulated by cherry-picked data without understanding selection bias. The promise that statistics lets us “see” patterns invisible to intuition comes bundled with warning that those same tools enable deception. What follows is less book review than attempt to sit with that tension—to think about what it means to write statistics primer for democracy when democracy itself seems increasingly innumerate.

The Arithmetic of Democracy

You’re standing in a voting booth in November, trying to parse poll numbers that show your candidate ahead by three points, margin of error plus or minus four. You’ve read that hormone replacement therapy might prevent heart disease—or cause it. Your teenager’s ACT score puts him in the 83rd percentile, but you’re not sure if that’s cause for celebration or concern. The numbers surround you, precise and authoritative, but you’re navigating blind.

Charles Wheelan wrote Naked Statistics: Stripping the Dread from the Data for this exact moment of democratic peril. The book arrives as an act of civic rescue, a 400-page intervention against a peculiar form of modern illiteracy: the inability to interrogate the numbers that increasingly govern our lives. When Wheelan announces that “statistics is like a high-caliber weapon—helpful when used correctly and potentially disastrous in the wrong hands,” he’s not being hyperbolic. He’s describing the 15 million women prescribed estrogen supplements in 2001 based on observational studies, only to discover through randomized trials that the treatment increased heart disease, stroke, and breast cancer. The New York Times would later ask how many women died because they took pills their doctors prescribed to keep them healthy. The answer: tens of thousands.

This is Wheelan’s opening gambit—establish that ignorance kills—and he sustains the urgency throughout. His methodology combines the accessibility of Malcolm Gladwell with the rigor of an econometrics textbook, a high-wire act that mostly succeeds. The book progresses systematically from descriptive statistics through probability, inference, and regression analysis, using an arsenal of real examples: Schlitz beer’s statistically sophisticated (and ethically dubious) 1981 Super Bowl taste test, Netflix’s $1 million competition to improve its recommendation algorithm by 10%, the Monty Hall Problem that has “delighted statisticians ever since and perplexed everyone else.”

Whelan’s signal achievement is making the central limit theorem—”the LeBron James of statistics,” he declares—feel both comprehensible and consequential. By Chapter 8, readers who began the book intimidated by mathematical notation can understand how pollsters extrapolate the opinions of 330 million Americans from conversations with 1,000 randomly selected adults. The margin of error of plus or minus 3% isn’t mysticism; it’s a 95% confidence interval derived from calculable standard errors. When you learn that Joseph McCarthy’s 1950 claim about having “a list of 205” communist infiltrators exploited the false precision of specific numbers to lend credibility to lies, you’re learning both statistics and how democracies fail.

The book’s structural logic mirrors good pedagogy: establish intuition before introducing formulas, use real stakes to motivate abstract concepts, acknowledge complexity without drowning in it. Whelan spends pages explaining why you should switch doors on Let’s Make a Deal (the answer involves conditional probability), then leverages that intuition to explain why screening entire populations for rare diseases generates more false positives than true cases. He’s building an architecture of statistical literacy, one insight stacked on another, each example doing double duty as illustration and proof of concept.

Yet the work carries contradictions. Whelan positions statistics as democracy’s essential tool—”it’s easy to lie with statistics, but it’s hard to tell the truth without them,” he quotes Andreas Dunkels—while documenting its systematic abuse. Regression analysis, he demonstrates, enabled researchers to establish that low-control jobs literally kill workers (the Whitehall studies), but the same tool generated the erroneous conclusions about estrogen that killed patients. Value-at-risk models were supposed to quantify and manage Wall Street’s exposure; instead, their false precision nearly destroyed the global financial system in 2008. The book catalogues these failures meticulously—omitted variables, reverse causality, data mining, the confusion of correlation and causation—creating a catalog of statistical sins that reads like a prosecutor’s brief.

This tension between statistics as liberation and statistics as weapon never quite resolves, and perhaps that’s appropriate. Whelan’s chapter on polling explains how CBS and the New York Times spent days and significant resources conducting telephone interviews with 1,650 adults to produce the finding that 9% of Americans approve of Congress’s performance—a finding both technically precise and potentially meaningless if the sampling methodology contained hidden biases. The book teaches you to ask: Were cell phone users included? How were non-responders handled? What was the actual response rate? These are democracy’s questions, the barrier between informed citizenship and manipulation.

Consider Whelan’s treatment of the autism epidemic—or diagnosis epidemic, the distinction matters. He presents the statistics: one in 88 American children diagnosed with autism spectrum disorders in 2012, up from one in 150 in 2002. He documents the cluster analysis identifying autism hotspots in affluent, highly-educated California neighborhoods. He refutes the vaccine-autism connection that inspired parents to refuse immunizations, putting children at risk of preventable diseases. But he also admits the honest limit: “Thomas Insel, director of the National Institute of Mental Health, has said, ‘Is it cell phones, ultrasound, diet sodas? Every parent has a theory. At this point, we just don’t know.’” The book models intellectual humility even as it demonstrates statistical power.

Whelan closes with five questions statistics can help answer: the future of football (chronic traumatic encephalopathy studies show players receiving blows equivalent to hitting a windshield at 25 mph), the autism puzzle, teacher evaluation controversies, global poverty interventions, and the boundaries of data privacy. That Target’s predictive analytics can identify pregnant women before their fathers do—and did, in one documented Minneapolis case—represents both statistical triumph and surveillance state nightmare. The book ends not with solutions but with stakes made explicit: “Math cannot supplant judgment.”

Naked Statistics functions as both textbook and manifesto, teaching regression analysis while arguing for its democratic necessity. Whelan’s prose occasionally strains under the burden of making probability distributions entertaining—there are limits to how much levity the T-distribution can sustain—but the project succeeds on its own terms. You finish the book able to parse poll margins, skeptical of single-study headlines, alert to the difference between statistical significance and practical importance.

More crucially, you finish aware of what statistics can and cannot do. It can help establish that exercise correlates with lower cardiovascular disease rates while acknowledging it cannot prove causation without controlled experiments. It can quantify discrimination by isolating wage gaps unexplained by education, experience, and occupation—but cannot definitively separate discrimination from unobservable worker characteristics. It can demonstrate that students randomly assigned to smaller classes perform better—but only if society invests $12 million in the experiment, as Tennessee did with Project STAR.

The book’s most radical claim arrives quietly in Chapter 11: brilliant researchers “are often individuals or teams who find creative ways to do controlled experiments.” Whelan profiles economists who measured police effectiveness by studying crime rates on terrorism high-alert days (when D.C. deployed extra officers for reasons unrelated to street crime), who evaluated education’s health benefits by exploiting changes in compulsory schooling laws across states and decades, who tested whether strangers’ prayers improve surgical outcomes through double-blind trials ($2.4 million spent, no effect found). These examples share a common architecture: ingenuity applied to causality’s intractable problem. We can observe correlation everywhere; causation requires cleverness.

This is statistics as craft, as art, as essential civic infrastructure. Whelan makes the case not through abstraction but accumulation: the Framingham Heart Study’s 2,000+ papers elucidating cardiovascular disease, the longitudinal research establishing that one additional year of schooling extends life expectancy by 1.5 years, the autism studies that simultaneously advance knowledge and debunk dangerous falsehoods. The prose occasionally betrays its pedagogical origins—”Let’s stick with weight for a while” doesn’t scan like The New Yorker—but Whelan’s commitment to accessibility serves the larger democratic project.

You close the book with tools: an understanding of p-values, confidence intervals, standard errors, regression coefficients, the perpetual confusion between causation and correlation. But you also close it with questions, chief among them: In an era of unprecedented data abundance and analytical power, why does statistical literacy remain so rare? Whelan spent 400 pages demonstrating that statistics can be taught, that the concepts aren’t impossibly complex, that the stakes couldn’t be higher. The persistence of innumeracy becomes not a cognitive failure but a political one, a collective decision to navigate democracy half-blind.

The book ends with a warning about Target, Facebook, facial recognition technology, DNA databases, and the surveillance architecture statistics enables. “Math cannot supplant judgment,” Whelan writes, and leaves you there—statistically literate, ethically unmoored, better equipped to interrogate the numbers but no clearer on when the interrogation should stop. It’s an honest ending, acknowledging that statistical tools amplify both human wisdom and human folly. You wanted a weapon for democracy; Whelan delivered that, along with the reminder that every weapon cuts both ways.
