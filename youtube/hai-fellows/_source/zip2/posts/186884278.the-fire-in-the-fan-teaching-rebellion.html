The song is two minutes and thirty-seven seconds long. It opens with a simple observation: “Small and still / Yet fiercely / Wrote / What the world / Dared only / Think.” No introduction. No context. Just the immediate claim that someone small wrote dangerous things. Then it names her: Jane Austen. The woman your high school English teacher probably made you read before you understood why it mattered.

This is Lyrical Literacy, a project from Humanitarians AI that uses AI-enhanced music to teach youth—and anyone else listening—to look deeper at why certain people get taught in classrooms and commemorated in history. The methodology is specific: take a historical figure, distill them into poetry that captures their essence, layer it over music designed to engage multiple brain regions simultaneously, then deploy it on YouTube where it becomes searchable, archivable, replayable. The goal is not just to teach facts about Jane Austen. The goal is to rewire how people understand what rebellion looks like.

Because if you can learn to see Jane Austen as a revolutionary hiding her fire in a fan, you can learn to see resistance in unexpected places everywhere.

The Problem Jane Austen Solves

Here is what most students learn about Jane Austen: she wrote books about marriage in Regency England. Pride and Prejudice. Emma. Sense and Sensibility. Stories where women worry about finding husbands and everyone drinks tea and nothing violent happens. If you are a teenage reader trying to understand why this matters, the standard curriculum offers: “She wrote about society” or “She was important for women’s literature” or “Her prose style influenced the novel form.”

None of this explains why Jane Austen is taught.

Here is what Lyrical Literacy’s version says: “She stitched with ink a / Wild rebellion / Beneath lace gloves.” The metaphor is surgical. Austen was writing under conditions where women could not own property independently, could not vote, could not attend university, were legally subordinate to fathers and husbands, and were expected to perform compliance with a social system that treated them as decorative objects to be traded between families. She could not write a manifesto. She could not march in the streets. She could not even sign her books with her own name—her first novel was published “By A Lady.”

So she hid her fire in a fan. She wrote novels that looked like romantic comedies on the surface but systematically dismantled every assumption of the Regency social order underneath. She made fools of the aristocracy (”every dashing duke she made / Became / A / Fool / By the end”). She exposed how marriage was an economic trap for women with no inheritance. She showed that the “accomplished” women praised by society were often vapid while the sharp-tongued, difficult women were the ones worth listening to. She did all of this in prose so polite that you could read it aloud in a parlor without scandal.

“Dear Jane they said sweet auntie plain / But books don’t bow nor blush nor bend”

This is the pedagogical move. The song teaches you to see that “sweet auntie plain” was a disguise. The books—not Jane herself, but the books—don’t bow or blush or bend. They stand. They refuse. They do the work of rebellion while looking like drawing-room entertainment. Once you understand this about Jane Austen, you can start seeing it everywhere: rebellion that looks soft, resistance that uses the master’s tools against him, people who burn with grace instead of screaming in the streets.

Why Music, Why AI, Why Now

Lyrical Literacy is anchored in a specific claim about neuroscience: music engages more brain regions simultaneously than almost any other human activity. When you listen to music with lyrics, your brain processes rhythm, melody, harmony, linguistic meaning, emotional content, and narrative structure all at once. This creates what researchers call “multimodal learning”—information encoded through multiple pathways, which makes it easier to recall and harder to forget.

A textbook can tell you Jane Austen was important. A song makes you feel it, remember it, repeat it. The melody lodges in your head. The phrases become mantras. “Austen is a verb you see / It means / To burn with grace / And hide your fire / In a fan.” You can recite this after one listen. You cannot recite a textbook paragraph about the development of the domestic novel in early 19th century England.

The AI component serves two functions. First, it allows for rapid production at scale. Nik Bear Brown can write poems about Jane Austen, Harriet Tubman, Rumi, James Baldwin, whoever needs to be understood more deeply, and convert them into fully produced songs without needing a recording studio, session musicians, or a traditional music label. This is the democratization of educational content creation—one person with access to AI tools can produce what would have previously required an entire production team.

Second, AI allows for voice enhancement and cloning that creates emotional resonance beyond what text alone can achieve. The Jane Austen song uses a human vocalist whose voice is then enhanced through AI to add layers of texture, harmony, emotional depth. The result sounds richer than a straightforward reading would, more memorable than a lecture, more engaging than a documentary voiceover. It creates the cognitive conditions for retention.

This is not about replacing teachers or traditional education. This is about supplementing it with tools that work the way brains actually work—through pattern, repetition, emotion, and multimodal encoding.

The Verb Problem

“Austen is a verb you see”

This is the line that rewrites everything. Not “Jane Austen was a novelist” but “Austen is a verb”—a thing you do, an action you take, a strategy you deploy. When you Austen something, you are hiding your fire in a fan. You are burning with grace. You are writing rebellion beneath lace gloves. You are making the powerful look foolish while appearing to comply with their rules.

This transforms a dead historical figure into a transferable skill.

Students who listen to this song are not just learning about Jane Austen. They are learning a grammar of resistance that applies to any situation where direct confrontation is impossible or counterproductive. If you are a teenager in a school system that does not respect you, you can Austen your way through—comply with the surface requirements while building something subversive underneath. If you are an employee in a corporation that exploits you, you can Austen your way through—maintain professional courtesy while documenting what is actually happening. If you are a citizen under a government that kills protesters, you can Austen your way through—make songs that look like laments but function as historical records.

This is why the Jane Austen song and the Iran song are part of the same project. Both teach people to recognize that effective resistance does not always look like open confrontation. Sometimes it looks soft. Sometimes it hides. Sometimes it burns with grace instead of setting fires in the street. But it is still resistance. It is still refusal. It is still the insistence that things should be otherwise.

The Architecture of Hidden Fire

The song’s structure mirrors its content. It appears simple—short lines, straightforward language, a gentle melody. But the line breaks create emphasis that transforms meaning. Look at how “Wild rebellion” is isolated on its own line, capitalized, demanding attention before being immediately followed by “Beneath lace gloves.” The rebellion is wild, but it is also hidden. Both things are true simultaneously.

“Never married / Always wed / To / Truth / And irony”

The enjambment—breaking the line after “To”—forces you to pause before the payoff. You expect “wed to” to be followed by a person, but instead it is “Truth / And irony.” Austen’s marriage was to ideas, not to a man. Her commitment was to exposing the gap between what society claimed to be true and what was actually true. The irony becomes her life partner, her constant companion, her method of operation.

“A quiet knife / Behind the smile”

This is the image that teenagers remember. Not “Austen wrote satirical novels” but “a quiet knife behind the smile.” You can see it. You can feel the threat. You understand immediately that something dangerous is happening even though it looks polite. This is what multimodal learning creates—visceral understanding that bypasses the need for lengthy explanation.

“She knew the price of being good / Too good / And gently crashed the parlor doors / With commas sharp and women / Loud / In whispers”

Here is the technique made explicit. Being good—too good—is a trap. Perfect compliance is a form of erasure. But Austen found the exploit in the system: she could crash the parlor doors (break the rules, violate the norms, say the unsayable) as long as she did it gently, with grammatical precision (”commas sharp”), creating women characters who were loud but in whispers. Loud enough to be heard. Quiet enough not to be thrown out of the parlor.

This is the lesson for any oppressed population: find the frequency at which you can broadcast without getting shut down. Make noise that looks like silence. Be loud in whispers.

The Pages Soft As Thunder

“So now we sit / And sip / And turn / Her / Pages / Soft / As thunder”

The closing image is a paradox. Soft pages. Thunder. Things that should be opposites existing simultaneously. You sit and sip tea—the picture of gentility, the performance of compliance with Regency propriety—while turning pages that contain thunder. The content does not match the form. The delivery system (a novel about marriage) conceals the payload (systematic critique of patriarchy and class hierarchy).

This is what Lyrical Literacy is teaching: how to read. Not just how to decode words on a page, but how to see what is hidden beneath the surface. How to recognize that “pages soft as thunder” means something is happening in the gap between appearance and reality. How to understand that the people who change the world are not always the ones shouting in the streets. Sometimes they are the ones writing novels. Sometimes they are the ones making songs about massacres under internet blackout. Sometimes they are the ones running onto battlefields without guns.

All of them are burning with grace. All of them are hiding fire in fans. All of them are betting that soft thunder is more durable than loud silence.

The Transferable Skill Set

When a student listens to the Jane Austen song, they are learning:

  1. Recognition of hidden resistance: that compliance can be a disguise for rebellion

  2. Strategic deployment of tone: that being polite does not mean surrendering your critique

  3. The long game: that books outlast their authors, that ideas are more durable than individuals

  4. Grammar as weapon: that how you say something can be as powerful as what you say

  5. The value of looking deeper: that the people dismissed as “sweet” or “plain” may be the most dangerous

These are not abstract concepts. These are operational principles for navigating systems of power. When you teach a teenager to see Jane Austen as a revolutionary, you are teaching them to see revolutionary potential in unexpected places—including in themselves.

This is why Lyrical Literacy uses AI-enhanced music to deliver this content. The goal is not information transfer. The goal is cognitive restructuring. You want people to internalize “Austen is a verb” so deeply that when they encounter their own version of the Regency parlor—the school that dehumanizes them, the workplace that exploits them, the government that shoots them—they instinctively ask: where can I hide my fire? How can I burn with grace? What does it look like to crash the doors gently, with commas sharp and voice loud in whispers?

The answer will be different for everyone. But the grammar is the same. Learn from Jane Austen. Learn from the medic running onto battlefields without a gun. Learn from the dried cloud that keeps gathering even after being shot full of holes. Learn that resistance does not always announce itself. Sometimes it stitches with ink. Sometimes it carries the wounded. Sometimes it makes songs that document massacres no one wants to remember.

All of it is fire. All of it burns. Some of it just hides better than the rest.
