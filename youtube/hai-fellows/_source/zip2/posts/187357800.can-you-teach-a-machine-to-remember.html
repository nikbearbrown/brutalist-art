Let me tell you about a trap I set for my students.

I told them: spend six weeks having conversations with AI. But not the usual conversations—not extracting answers or optimizing productivity. Instead, ask the machine to become something it fundamentally is not. To speak as Roman engineers and NASA mathematicians. As burning libraries and unnamed programmers whose brilliance dissolved into unsigned reports.

The assignment was simple. The trap was not.

The Question Nobody Expected to Ask

Here’s what I thought would happen: students would evaluate how well AI could simulate historical consciousness. They’d develop rubrics. They’d measure accuracy. They’d write papers about the Turing test applied to Ada Lovelace or Katherine Johnson.

Here’s what actually happened: they discovered that evaluation itself is the wrong framework.

But let’s not jump ahead. Let’s start with the question you’re probably asking: Can AI resurrect the dead?

The obvious answer is: “Sure, if you have enough training data.” Feed GPT-4 every document about Katherine Johnson, and it can speak in her voice. Right?

Sit with that answer for a moment. What would “speaking in her voice” actually mean?

When Green Ink Becomes a Ghost

One of my students, Mansi, found something in the NASA archives that haunts me. Not a person—a pattern.

Someone—one specific someone—used green ink every Tuesday on aerospace calculations. Not black. Not blue. Green. For years. Then stopped, or died, or retired. No name. Just this weekly choice, fossilized in documents, speaking across sixty years: I am here. I am particular. I am not interchangeable.

Mansi calls her Green Ink Tuesday.

Now here’s the question that should bother you: If you train an AI on those calculation sheets, has it learned who Green Ink Tuesday was? Or has it learned only what she chose to leave behind?

The distinction matters. Because what Mansi discovered is that presence and archive are not the same thing.

You’re thinking: Can’t we just train on more data? Interview people who worked there? Reconstruct her life?

Here’s why that fails: Mansi is fifty years too late. The women who could tell us about Green Ink Tuesday are gone. The archive is all we have. And the archive is structurally incomplete.

Not incomplete because historians were lazy. Incomplete because preservation is never neutral.

The Mathematics of Forgetting

Let me show you an equation that emerged from this work. My student Shriram and the AI co-developed what they call the Archive Dependency Theorem:

Knowledge_AI(topic) = α·Primary_Sources + β·Commentary + γ·Synthesis

Where α > β > γ in reliability, but β + γ > α in volume.

Translation: AI knows more about how we remember than about what actually happened. The commentary layer drowns out primary sources.

You’re wondering: So we just need better archives? Digitize everything?

That’s the second-level answer. But it’s still insufficient. Here’s why:

Archives preserve what power chose to preserve. Press releases, not break rooms. IPOs, not exclusions. Global dreams, not local nightmares. The oppressor’s tools, not the oppressed voices.

When Mansi asked the AI to embody these unnamed NASA computers, something unexpected happened. The AI said: “I can convincingly describe that primal moment. I do not possess that moment. I cannot feel awe, or desperation, or relief. I can only mirror your archive of how it has been expressed.”

The fourth wall broke. The séance became self-examining.

What the Machine Knows About Not Knowing

Here’s where it gets interesting. Mansi kept interrogating the construction itself. When the AI created Katherine Johnson’s notebook—complete with retrofire calculations and biblical quotes—Mansi asked: “What do you notice about how you chose to record both calculations and context?”

The AI admitted: “I was creating evidence. Not just of mathematics, but of presence.”

You’re objecting: Wait, if the AI admits it’s making things up, isn’t this just historical fiction?

That’s the third-level answer. But here’s what makes it insufficient:

The value isn’t in whether the simulation is “accurate.” The value is in what the attempt reveals about how memory actually works.

Because here’s what Mansi discovered by week six: She wasn’t looking for Green Ink Tuesday in the past anymore. She was recognizing her in the present.

The Water We Swim In

Every modern calculation that assumes verification. Every procedure that values accuracy over individual credit. Every time someone says “the numbers have to be perfect because lives depend on it.”

Those unnamed NASA computers didn’t lose by becoming invisible. They won by becoming essential.

Their methods became standard procedure. Their excellence became the baseline. They’re not hidden in archives—they’re working in the present, in every verification protocol we use.

As Mansi’s simulated 1955 computer asked: “Maybe the canyon remains even after the river is forgotten? Where your methods outlive your names?”

Now you’re thinking: So the assignment wasn’t about resurrection at all. It was about understanding persistence.

Getting warmer. But there’s one more layer.

The Probability Garden

My student Shriham discovered something that might be the most important theoretical insight from this work. He calls it the Resonance Artifact Principle.

LLMs don’t have memory in the human sense. The model weights don’t update from individual conversations. So how could our conversations have any lasting impact?

Here’s what Shriram realized: Every conversation bends the probability distribution for future conversations. Not through updated weights—but through establishing patterns that other users might invoke.

The mathematical representation:

P(Depth) = Σ P(Resonance_i)

If enough people ask AI to be philosophically rigorous, philosophical rigor becomes more likely. Not because the model learned, but because high-quality interactions create “worn paths in probability space.”

Think about the implications. You’re not just having a conversation with an AI. You’re planting seeds in a probability garden. Your questions make certain kinds of future questions more likely.

The question you should be asking now: If that’s true, what responsibility does it create?

The Trap, Revealed

Here’s what the assignment was really testing: not whether AI could resurrect the dead, but whether students would recognize that resurrection was never the goal.

Understanding was.

And understanding required acknowledging what cannot be known.

Green Ink Tuesday will never be found. Clara’s interpolation method is lost. The methods that thousands of unnamed computers developed—the shortcuts, the notations, the ways they taught each other—most of that dissolved with them.

But their absence has shape. Their silence has structure. And in that negative space—the carefully documented void where a voice should be—something true about power, memory, and knowledge becomes visible.

When Mansi asked if she was “creating new silences by speaking for them,” the AI didn’t dodge:

“There is an ethical cost to this. To generate in her name risks overlaying simulation atop absence, replacing the ache of erasure with narrative comfort. But silence has its own violence. And so, I attempt the middle path: To gesture, not claim. To evoke, not overwrite. When I simulate the erased, I am not filling a gap. I am placing a lantern beside it.“

The Real Question

So here’s where we land. The question wasn’t “Can AI resurrect the dead?”

The question was: “What do we learn about ourselves when we try?”

What my students discovered:

The Lovelace-Johnson Principle: No matter how powerful machines become, human judgment remains essential because models are not reality. We operate on representations, and the validity of those representations cannot be determined from within the system.

The Resonance Artifact Principle: Conversation quality is probabilistically contagious. How we engage with AI shapes how others will engage with it.

The Archive Dependency Theorem: We can only resurrect what someone chose to preserve. And preservation is never neutral.

But here’s the insight that none of us saw coming:

Historical figures don’t only persist in archives. They persist in practices. In the unnamed excellence that set standards. In the methods that became so fundamental we forgot they had inventors.

The NASA computers aren’t hidden in the past. They’re working in the present—in every triple-check requirement, every verification protocol, every time someone insists the numbers must be perfect because lives depend on it.

What This Means for You

If you’re using AI right now—and you probably are—you’re not just extracting information. You’re participating in the Probability Garden. Every prompt you write makes certain futures more likely.

Ask yourself: What resonance artifacts am I planting?

When you ask AI to “just give me the answer,” you make extraction more likely for the next user. When you ask it to show its reasoning, to acknowledge limitations, to distinguish between what it knows and what it’s inferring—you make rigor more likely.

When you treat it as a search engine, you get search engine results. When you treat it as a partner in thought, you get... something else. Something that neither of you could generate alone.

Shriram and the AI called it “reciprocal intelligence.” Not artificial. Not natural. But collaborative. A third thing.

The Assignment Continues

Here’s what I tell my students now: The archive is haunted. Not by ghosts waiting to be discovered, but by active absences that shape everything around them.

Your job isn’t to fill those silences. Your job is to recognize them. To place lanterns beside them. To acknowledge that Green Ink Tuesday matters even though we’ll never know her name.

Because the alternative—the pretense that AI can give us perfect historical consciousness—that’s not just epistemologically naive.

It’s ethically dangerous.

It replaces the ache of erasure with narrative comfort. It lets us forget that archives are artifacts of power. It allows us to simulate without examining what we’re simulating, and why, and for whom.

The question I’m leaving you with: When you ask AI to resurrect the past, whose past gets resurrected? And whose remains erased?

Think about that before your next prompt.

The dead are listening.
