Let me ask you something that’s been keeping me up at night.

When a company receives a regulatory approval worth $20 million annually—a decision that fundamentally alters its cost structure and competitive position—what should happen to its stock price?

You’re probably thinking: “It should go up, obviously.” And you’d be right. On average, these approvals generated market capitalization gains of $51 million in the dataset I’ve been studying. Two percent returns in five days. Shareholders walking away richer simply because a bureaucrat said “yes.”

But here’s what’s strange. Half the companies showed exactly zero price movement.

Not a tick. Not a drift. The market absorbed news that should have been worth tens of millions and... yawned.

So now the question becomes more interesting: Why would the same regulatory approval—same product, same legal framework, same economic stakes—cause one firm’s stock to surge while another’s sits perfectly still?

The Obvious Answer (And Why It’s Wrong)

Your first instinct is probably: “The connected firms are just better companies. They knew they’d get approved because they filed better applications, hired better lawyers, had more legitimate business cases.”

Reasonable. Let’s test that.

If application quality drove outcomes, we’d expect the stated criteria to predict approval rates. The U.S. Trade Representative’s own guidelines said exemptions would be granted based on:

  • Availability of non-Chinese substitutes

  • Degree of economic harm to the applicant

  • Strategic importance to China’s industrial policy

These are all legitimate, merit-based factors. So researchers ran probit regressions controlling for exactly these variables. Industry type, product characteristics, supply chain dependence—all of it.

Here’s what predicted approval after controlling for merit:

Political donations to Republican candidates (2016 cycle): +3.94 percentage point increase in approval probability

Political donations to Democratic candidates (2016 cycle): -3.40 percentage point decrease in approval probability

Notice something? These donations were made in 2016—two years before the tariffs even existed. Reverse causality is ruled out by the timeline itself. Firms couldn’t have been donating in response to favors they’d receive in a regulatory program that didn’t exist yet.

But you’re not convinced yet. You’re thinking: “Maybe Republican-aligned firms just operate in industries that genuinely needed more relief. Correlation isn’t causation.”

Fair objection. So ask yourself this: Why would Democratic donations actively hurt your application?

The Punishment That Reveals Intent

If political connections were simply buying information or access to better legal counsel—what economists call the “information channel”—then donating to the minority party should have a neutral effect at worst. You gave to the Democrats, Trump’s administration doesn’t particularly care, they process your application on the merits anyway.

Instead, Democratic donors saw their approval odds drop by 3.4 percentage points. Nearly as large in magnitude as the Republican bonus, but in the opposite direction.

Sit with that for a moment. What mechanism explains a penalty for supporting the political opposition?

It can’t be application quality—partisan affiliation doesn’t correlate with bureaucratic competence. It can’t be industry selection—the effect persists when you compare identical products. And it definitely can’t be “information sharing”—the technical details about supply chain dependence are the same regardless of which party you donated to.

The only explanation that survives is retaliation. The deliberate withholding of regulatory relief to punish supporters of your political opponents.

But maybe I’m still not being rigorous enough. Maybe there’s something unique about this particular administration, this particular regulatory program. How do we know this wasn’t just... a weird anomaly?

The Placebo That Proves the Pattern

Here’s where the methodology gets interesting.

At the exact same time the Section 301 China-tariff exemptions were being processed with zero oversight, the Trump administration was also running Section 232 steel and aluminum tariff exemptions through the Department of Commerce. Same political moment. Same partisan incentives. Structurally identical regulatory shock.

But Section 232 had something Section 301 lacked: institutional guardrails.

Inspector General oversight. Congressional reporting requirements. GAO reviews. Senators Chris Van Hollen and Ron Wyden actively grilling Commerce officials about potential favoritism.

So researchers ran the exact same analysis on Section 232 applications.

Result: No statistically significant relationship between political donations and approval rates. Zero bifurcation in market reactions between connected and unconnected firms.

The favoritism vanished when oversight existed.

This is what proves we’re not looking at “better firms donate more” or “Republicans are just better at business.” If that were true, the pattern would show up in both programs. Instead, it only appears in the regulatory architecture designed to be opaque.

You’re starting to see it now, aren’t you? But there’s still a piece missing.

Why the Market Doesn’t React

Go back to that original puzzle: Why do connected firms show zero abnormal returns when they receive valuable approvals?

If you were an investor in 2019, holding shares in a company that made substantial PAC contributions to Republican candidates in 2016, and that company just applied for a tariff exemption worth $20 million annually... what would you do?

You’d price the expected value of that exemption into the stock immediately. You wouldn’t wait for the USTR to publish its decision in the Federal Register months later. You’d calculate: “Republican administration, Republican donor, high-discretion process, weak oversight... probability of approval is maybe 70-80%.”

So when the approval actually arrives, it contains no new information. The market already knew this was coming. The “favor” was priced in the moment the political investment paid off electorally, not the moment the bureaucratic machinery confirmed it.

For an unconnected firm with identical business fundamentals, the calculation is different: “No political connections, 14.6% baseline approval rate, might as well assume this fails.” When that firm somehow gets approved, it’s a genuine surprise. The stock surges.

This is what I mean by an “involuntary audit trail.”

No investor sets out to document corruption. They’re just trying to predict which firms will be helped or harmed by government policy. But in making those predictions with extraordinary precision—backing them with billions of dollars at risk—they create an evidentiary record that’s nearly impossible to dismiss.

The market is confessing what it observes. And it cannot lie, because money is on the line.

The Uncomfortable Equation

Let me show you the mathematics of this in the simplest possible form.

If time to visa expiration = 80 days and average company response time = 14 days, what does that tell us about the actual search window for international students trying to find sponsorship?

Wrong question. That’s a different project. But the principle is the same: When the clock is ticking and the stakes are clear, you can measure what matters by watching who moves and when.

Here, the equation is:

Market reaction size = f(surprise, magnitude)

For connected firms:
Surprise ≈ 0 (everyone expected this)
Magnitude = $20-25M annually
Result: Zero abnormal return

For unconnected firms:
Surprise ≈ 1 (nobody expected this)
Magnitude = $20-25M annually
Result: 2-3% abnormal return ($51M market cap gain)

The bifurcation itself is the proof. When surprise equals zero, the favor was already priced in. When surprise equals one, the decision was genuinely merit-based (or at least, unpredictable from political variables).

The Question That Follows You Home

So we’ve documented systematic favoritism. We’ve shown it survives placebo tests. We’ve demonstrated the market prices it continuously. We have a replicable methodology that could apply to pharmaceutical approvals, defense contracts, environmental permits, infrastructure projects—any domain where government discretion meets private capital.

The mathematical signature is clear. The evidence is robust. The confession is written in the stock price data, updated every trading day, witnessed by millions of investors.

Now comes the part that keeps me up at night.

Will anyone care?

The USTR officials who administered this system have moved on. The firms that received favorable treatment collected their windfalls. The firms that were denied have adjusted. The political donations that purchased the outcomes are protected by the First Amendment. And the market, having correctly identified the pattern, has already moved on to pricing the next regulatory shock.

In April 2025, President Trump announced reciprocal tariffs extending this discretionary framework to virtually every trading partner. Firms with supply chain exposure to China saw valuations drop 5-10%. But within that decline? The same bifurcation pattern emerged. Connected firms showed smaller losses. The market was already pricing which companies would successfully navigate the new exemption process.

We’re watching the same movie again. In real-time. With a methodology that documents it as it happens.

And the system persists because the beneficiaries of favoritism are the ones who fund campaigns, staff administrations, and write the rules governing their own oversight.

The Final Question

Here’s what I want you to sit with.

Markets don’t reform governments. Markets don’t care about fairness. Markets are amoral calculating machines that predict cash flows and price probabilities.

But precisely because they’re amoral—because they have no incentive to lie, no political allegiance, no PR strategy—they become reliable witnesses.

The confession is involuntary. The audit trail is continuous. The methodology is replicable.

What remains is not a question of evidence. It’s a question of whether people who can change these systems will choose to act on what the evidence reveals.

Or whether we’ll simply keep watching the stock prices, reading the confession, and doing nothing.

The market can document corruption. But markets don’t pass reform legislation.

People do.
