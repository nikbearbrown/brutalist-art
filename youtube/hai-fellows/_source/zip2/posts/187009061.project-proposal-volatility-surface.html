Phase 1: The Problem & The “Why”

You’re standing in front of your finance class at Northeastern, and a student asks the question you’ve been wrestling with yourself: “Professor, where do you invest when everything’s already expensive?”

It’s February 2026. Your tech portfolio—the systematic positions you built over the past year—has doubled. NVIDIA, AMD, the major names. Your approach worked: check fundamentals first, buy quality companies when they’re down, hold through volatility. It’s the same discipline that generates 17% returns in six weeks during StockTrak competitions while other teams struggle past 2%.

But now you face the investor’s paradox: success creates scarcity. The stocks you’d buy aren’t on sale anymore. The entire sector seems priced for perfection.

Then Qualcomm starts bleeding. Down 12% through January. Analysts downgrade. Technical indicators break. Earnings loom on February 4th, and the Street is nervous.

You could buy on the dip. You could wait. Or you could test something you’ve been studying: whether options markets contain readable signals about what institutional money actually thinks will happen.

Wait—options? Isn’t that just gambling?

Not the way professionals use them. When a pension fund managing $50 billion wants to protect against downside, they buy puts. When those puts get expensive—really expensive—it creates a pattern in something called the “volatility surface.” A three-dimensional landscape of prices across strike levels and time horizons that encodes collective institutional anxiety.

The question: Can a retail investor read that landscape? Or is it only useful if you have a Bloomberg Terminal and a PhD in financial engineering?

You’re about to find out.

You pull up Qualcomm’s options chain. At $151 per share, with earnings in one week, here’s what institutions are paying:

  • $155 call (bet on upside): $3.70

  • $155 put (bet on downside): $6.50

The put costs 76% more. Put/call ratio: 1.76x.

In the literature, anything above 1.3x qualifies as “elevated fear.” This isn’t subtle—institutions are paying a significant premium for downside protection.

But here’s the critical question you’ve been researching: Are they worried about next week, or next year?

The Framework: Reading Fear Across Time

Options don’t come in one flavor. You can buy protection expiring tomorrow, next month, or next year. The pattern of prices across these horizons—the “term structure”—reveals whether anxiety is focused on a specific catalyst or reflects deeper rot.

You check the 3-month options. Same $155 strike, but expiring in May.

Put/call ratio: 1.30x.

Still elevated, but notably lower than the 1.76x panic in next week’s options.

Okay, what does that mean?

Think of it like weather forecasting. If umbrellas for tomorrow cost 10x more than umbrellas for next month, people are worried about a specific storm—not climate change. That’s temporary fear.

If umbrella prices are equally high across all time periods, something structural is shifting.

QCOM is showing the first pattern:

  • 1-week: High fear (1.76x)

  • 3-month: Moderate concern (1.30x)

Translation: “Q1 earnings will disappoint, but the business isn’t dying.”

You decide not to buy. Not yet. You want to see the earnings first, then check if the pattern holds.

The Central Hypothesis:

Systematic analysis of options market microstructure—specifically put/call skew and term structure patterns—provides retail-accessible signals for identifying mispriced stocks after binary events.

What we don’t know:

  • Does elevated put/call skew predict underperformance? Or mean reversion?

  • Do term structure patterns reliably distinguish temporary from structural problems?

  • Is this signal regime-dependent (works in calm markets but not panic)?

  • What’s the win rate across 100+ events?

  • Can retail investors extract alpha after transaction costs?

This is testable. This is falsifiable. This needs validation.

The Research Design: Evidence Over Assumptions

Your students—fellows in the Mycroft program—need research projects that teach quantitative methods while producing career-relevant work. This checks every box.

Study 1: Cross-Sectional Skew Analysis

Research Question: In any given week, do stocks with elevated put/call skew underperform stocks with neutral skew over the next 2-4 weeks? Or do they mean-revert?

Universe: 50-100 large-cap tech stocks (liquid options, 2020-2025)

Weekly procedure:

  1. Calculate put/call price ratios at ATM strikes

  2. Flag stocks with ratio > 1.5x (elevated fear)

  3. Flag stocks with ratio < 0.8x (elevated speculation)

  4. Measure forward returns at 1-week, 2-week, 4-week horizons

Critical controls:

  • Market regime (VIX level—fear behaves differently in calm vs. panic)

  • Sector momentum (tech rotation vs. selloff)

  • Earnings proximity (exclude the IV crush confound)

  • Stock-specific volatility (beta adjustment)

Expected insight: We may find the direction of predictiveness varies by regime. High skew might predict weakness in low-VIX environments (informed positioning) but reversal in high-VIX environments (capitulation).

Study 2: Time-Series Signal Validation

Research Question: For a given stock, when put/call skew elevates above its own historical average, what happens next?

Design:

  • Build 52-week rolling database of skew for each stock

  • Calculate current reading’s percentile

  • Test: When skew > 80th percentile, measure forward returns

Refinement: Separate “skew rising” (momentum) from “skew elevated but stable” (already priced in).

QCOM application: The 1.76x reading was at the 95th percentile of its own history—extreme for QCOM specifically, not just generally high.

Study 3: Term Structure Pattern Recognition

Research Question: Do divergent term structures (near-term bearish, long-term neutral) predict post-catalyst recoveries?

Pattern definition:

  • “Temporary bearishness”: 1-week put/call > 1.4x, 3-month put/call < 1.2x

  • “Structural problem”: Both elevated (>1.3x)

  • “Hype reversal”: 1-week call-skewed, 3-month put-skewed

Measurement: Track performance from pattern detection through 30 days post-catalyst.

Comparison group: Stocks with uniform skew across maturities.

QCOM validation: Pre-earnings showed classic “temporary bearishness” (1.76x near, 1.30x far). Post-earnings recovery began within hours.

Study 4: Implied vs. Realized Move Analysis

Research Question: When stocks exceed their typical earnings move BEFORE the announcement, is the event risk already priced in?

Calculation:

  • Historical average implied move for each stock’s earnings

  • Flag instances where pre-earnings move exceeds 1.5x this average

  • Measure post-earnings returns vs. normal earnings periods

QCOM case: Typical earnings move ±8%. Pre-earnings actual move: -12.2% (from $172 to $151). Stock had moved 1.5x its risk budget before the event.

Outcome: Post-earnings gap was severe (-10%), but recovery began immediately (+4.9% in 3 hours). The volatility was front-loaded.

The Mechanics: Why This Might Work

Wait—if this is predictive, why hasn’t it been arbitraged away?

Good question. Here’s what the academic literature suggests:

Possible mechanisms:

Slow-moving capital: Pension funds and endowments can’t reposition instantly. Hedging demand persists even after news becomes public, creating temporary distortions.

Behavioral overreaction: Salient risks (earnings misses, FDA rejections) trigger disproportionate protection-buying that mean-reverts after fear proves overdone.

Market maker mechanics: When dealers are short gamma from selling puts, they must buy stock as volatility crushes. This creates automatic buying pressure (Vanna and Charm flows) independent of fundamental news.

Limits to arbitrage: Balance sheet constraints prevent professionals from fully eliminating mispricings, especially around binary events when capital is scarce.

We don’t claim to know which mechanism dominates. That’s what the backtest reveals.

Dizzy. Break down those Greeks for me—Vanna and Charm.

Okay. When institutions buy puts before earnings (hedging their long positions), market makers sell those puts. To stay neutral, market makers must short stock proportional to the puts’ delta.

Earnings happen. Implied volatility collapses (”IV crush”). The puts lose value rapidly.

Now the market makers are “over-hedged”—they’re holding too much short stock for the diminished put exposure. To rebalance, they must buy back shares.

This buying is mechanical. Automatic. Predictable.

Vanna: Sensitivity of delta to IV changes. When IV drops, put deltas drop, dealers buy stock.

Charm: Sensitivity of delta to time decay. As expiration approaches, put deltas naturally decay, dealers buy stock passively.

Result: After earnings, there’s often systematic buying pressure that creates a technical floor—even if the fundamental news is mixed.

QCOM’s bounce from $132.50 to $139 in three hours? That’s not just “optimism.” That’s mechanical positioning unwind creating buying pressure.

What Already Exists: Standing on Academic Shoulders

This isn’t speculation. The quantitative research base is substantial:

Predictive power of skew:

  • Academic papers document that risk-neutral skewness predicts cross-sectional returns with alpha up to 50 basis points per week

  • Stocks with the least negative skew outperform those with steep negative skew by 10.9% annually risk-adjusted

Term structure signals:

  • The slope of the IV term structure is a statistically significant predictor of future option returns (Fama-MacBeth regressions, p < 0.05)

  • One percentage point increase in slope (long IV minus short IV) correlates with 0.5-1.0% increase in straddle returns

Implied vs. realized move:

  • SPX realizes lower volatility than implied 72-78% of the time

  • The 5-8% of instances where realized exceeds implied by >2x are “regime separators” with mean reversion potential

Mean reversion on extremes:

  • Systematic strategies using -3σ VWAP deviations + volatility signals have generated 13.0% annualized returns vs. 9.2% buy-and-hold (Sharpe 2.11 vs. 0.85)

What doesn’t exist: Systematic validation of whether RETAIL investors can apply these frameworks using free tools and public data.

That’s the gap we’re filling.

What Makes This Different: The Popper Methodology

You’ve built your career on evidence-based frameworks. Your cancer biology textbook doesn’t “fact-check” assertions—it treats everything as hypothesis and gathers evidence both for and against. Rarely is there 100% certainty. You present the evidence and let readers decide.

This research uses the same approach:

Evidence FOR volatility surface analysis:

  • Academic validation of skew predictiveness

  • Mechanical dealer positioning creates floors

  • Term structure distinguishes temporary from structural

  • QCOM real-time validation

Evidence AGAINST:

  • Markets may be efficient (signals arbitraged away)

  • Retail noise distorts near-term surface

  • Transaction costs may eliminate alpha

  • Regime changes may invalidate historical patterns

  • Selection bias (did we cherry-pick one success?)

We test both. We publish both. We let the data decide.

This isn’t about confirming a belief. It’s about discovering what’s true.

The Research Question That Haunts Every Quantitative Trader

Does the past predict the future, or does the past merely describe the past?

Every backtest faces this epistemological challenge. Historical patterns might be:

  • Real edge (persistent market inefficiency)

  • Regime artifact (worked then, doesn’t work now)

  • Overfitting (random noise that happened to align)

  • Selection bias (we noticed it because it worked, ignoring all the times it failed)

The only way to know: test it systematically. Control for confounds. Be honest about limitations. Publish regardless of outcome.

This is what separates researchers from cheerleaders.
