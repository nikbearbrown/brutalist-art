The Problem No One Tells You About

International students get 80 days after graduation to find a company willing to sponsor their work visa. Eighty days to locate an employer with both the legal infrastructure and the financial commitment to file H-1B paperwork. Eighty days before the deadline becomes literal: stay or go home.

The first obstacle isn’t finding jobs. It’s finding the findable jobs. Most companies use Applicant Tracking Systems—software that processes, filters, and ranks applications before a human ever sees them. These systems have names: Greenhouse, Lever, Workday, iCIMS, Taleo. They operate differently. They parse resumes differently. They weight keywords differently. Send a resume optimized for human eyes to a Greenhouse system, and it might rank you at position 247. Send a resume optimized for Greenhouse’s keyword matching to a human recruiter, and they’ll think you can’t write.

You need to know which system a company uses before you apply. But how do you find that out for 25,000 companies extracted from SEC filings?

What Greenhouse and Lever Actually Do

Greenhouse and Lever dominate the startup and scale-up hiring landscape. They’re the systems used by the companies most likely to sponsor visas: tech firms with venture funding, growth-stage companies expanding engineering teams, organizations that understand immigration paperwork because they’ve filed it before.

Greenhouse has a public API. You can query it directly:

https://boards-api.greenhouse.io/v1/boards/{company}/jobs

Replace {company} with a normalized company name—strip the “Inc.” and “LLC” and commas and spaces—and if the company uses Greenhouse, you get back clean JSON listing every open position. If they don’t use Greenhouse, you get a 404. If they use Greenhouse but have no open roles, you get an empty array.

Lever works the same way:

https://api.lever.co/v0/postings/{company}

Same pattern. Same accessibility. Same clarity.

This isn’t an accident. Greenhouse and Lever succeed when companies hire people. Making their job data accessible helps third-party platforms like Indeed, Monster, and LinkedIn aggregate listings. The easier it is to find Greenhouse jobs, the more valuable Greenhouse becomes to employers. So they don’t lock down the data. They make it scrapable by design.

The Code: 200 Lines Per System

Both scrapers follow the same architecture. You feed them a list of company names—either from the command line or from a text file containing 25,000 SEC Form D filers. The script normalizes each name using regex patterns:

python

def normalize_company_name(name: str) -> str:
    suffixes = [
        r',?\s+Inc\.?',
        r',?\s+LLC\.?',
        r',?\s+Corp\.?',
        # ... 12 more patterns
    ]
    cleaned = name
    for suffix in suffixes:
        cleaned = re.sub(suffix, '', cleaned, flags=re.IGNORECASE)
    cleaned = re.sub(r'[,.\s\-&\']', '', cleaned)
    return cleaned.lower()

“Databricks, Inc.” becomes “databricks.” “Duolingo, Inc.” becomes “duolingo.” The script queries the API. If it gets data back, it creates a subdirectory, saves jobs.json with the full listing, and saves metadata.json with the original company name, the normalized name, the job count, and the public URL.

The scripts rate-limit themselves: 0.5 seconds between requests, configurable up or down depending on how aggressive you want to be. They handle encoding issues—UTF-8, UTF-16, Latin-1—because company name files come from SEC data that was never meant to be machine-readable. They print progress as they run:

The Triple Win

This approach delivers three things:

First, you know the ATS system. When you apply to Duolingo, you can tailor your resume for Greenhouse’s keyword matching algorithm. When you apply to a company not in the Greenhouse or Lever datasets, you know they’re either using one of the big enterprise systems (Workday, iCIMS, Taleo) or processing applications by hand. Either way, you adjust your strategy.

Second, you get the jobs themselves. Duolingo has 83 open positions. You don’t have to navigate their careers page, apply blind, or guess whether they’re hiring. The data tells you exactly what roles are open, what locations they’re in, and what skills they require. You can filter for positions that match your background before you waste time on applications that won’t work.

Third, you verify the company is real. SEC Form D filings list 568,707 companies. Most are shell entities, holding companies, or startups that raised $500K, hired two people, and dissolved. If a company has an active Greenhouse account with job listings, it’s operational. It has HR infrastructure. It’s hiring. That narrows your target list from 568,707 to maybe 15,000.

What This Doesn’t Cover Yet

Greenhouse and Lever together capture 15-20% of the job market. But they capture 60-70% of the companies that sponsor work visas for international students. That’s the important number.

The systems they don’t cover:

Workday: 37% of Fortune 500 companies. No public API. You have to scrape *.myworkdayjobs.com career pages, which are JavaScript-heavy and require browser automation. Complexity: high. Maintenance burden: high. But companies like NVIDIA, Walmart, and Citi use Workday.

iCIMS: 10.7% market share. No simple public API. Must scrape career page implementations. Complexity: medium-high.

Taleo/Oracle: 13.4% of Fortune 500. APIs exist but require enterprise authentication. Third parties scrape public pages instead. Complexity: medium-high.

These are the enterprise systems. They’re harder to scrape, harder to maintain, and disproportionately used by large corporations that rarely sponsor visas for entry-level positions. A Fortune 500 company with 80,000 employees and an army of H-1B lawyers doesn’t need to post on Greenhouse. A Series B startup with 200 employees and one overworked recruiter does.

The Decision Point

You can stop here. Greenhouse + Lever covers the companies that matter. You can integrate these scrapers into the verification pipeline, run them against the SEC Form D list, and see what the hit rate is. If 60% of your target companies are using one of these two systems, you ship it. You’ve built something useful.

Or you build the Workday scraper. That adds Fortune 500 coverage. It takes 3-4 times longer to develop. It requires browser automation (Selenium or Playwright), which means maintaining headless Chrome dependencies and handling anti-bot detection. It adds complexity. It adds maintenance burden. But it adds NVIDIA. It adds Citi. It adds companies with the resources to sponsor dozens of visas every year.

Or you pay for third-party aggregators. Services like Apify and Fantastic.jobs already scrape Workday, iCIMS, and Taleo. They charge per request. You trade engineering time for dollars. Maybe that’s smarter. Maybe it’s not.

What the Data Will Tell You

Run the Greenhouse and Lever scrapers against the 25,000 companies from SEC Form D filings. Count how many return job listings. Calculate the match rate.

If it’s 60% or higher, you’re done. You’ve covered the companies that sponsor visas. The companies that hire international students. The companies where your 80 days actually has a chance of turning into a work visa.

If it’s lower—if only 30% of your target companies use Greenhouse or Lever—then you need Workday. You need the enterprise systems. You need to decide whether the additional engineering effort is worth the additional coverage.

The code is 200 lines per system. It’s clean. It’s maintainable. It handles encoding edge cases, rate limits itself, and generates structured output. It works. Now you run it at scale and let the data decide what happens next.
