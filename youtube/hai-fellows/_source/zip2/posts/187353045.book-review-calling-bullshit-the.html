You’re scrolling through your feed when you see it: a graph showing violent crime rates plummeting after Florida’s Stand Your Ground law. Except the Y-axis runs backward—zero at the top, murders climbing upward as the line descends. The designer inverted reality itself, and you almost missed it. This is the territory where Carl T. Bergstrom and Jevin D. West have planted their flag.

“Calling Bullshit: The Art of Skepticism in a Data-Driven World” emerges from a crucible most academic books never see—a University of Washington course that became a cultural phenomenon precisely because it named what everyone sensed but couldn’t articulate. The world isn’t drowning in lies, exactly. It’s drowning in something more insidious: quantitative camouflage, statistical misdirection, the kind of truth-adjacent nonsense that arrives wrapped in the authority of numbers and the veneer of science.

The book’s origin story is itself a study in recognizing patterns. Two professors—one a computational biologist, the other an information scientist—watched fake news metastasize during the 2016 election and realized their students needed more than media literacy. They needed epistemic self-defense. What began as an experiment became a waiting list of thousands. What made students camp out for seats wasn’t the promise of easy credits but something rarer: permission to question authority when that authority speaks in percentages and pie charts.

The methodology here is deliberately hybrid. Bergstrom and West aren’t writing a statistics textbook; they’re constructing a field guide to a specific ecology of deception. Chapter by chapter, they anatomize different species of bullshit: the data visualization duck (form overwhelming function), the glass slipper (data shoehorned into inappropriate visualizations), the P-hacking epidemic that’s turned scientific literature into a minefield of irreproducible results. Each gets the same treatment—exposition of the technique, real-world examples with stakes, then practical tools for dismantling the deception.

Consider their handling of Andrew Wakefield’s autism-vaccine fraud. Most accounts treat this as a morality tale about one corrupt doctor. Bergstrom and West show how the mathematics of refutation itself was the weapon—how millions of dollars and countless research hours were needed to overcome one fabricated study, demonstrating Brandolini’s Principle in action: “The amount of energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude bigger than needed to produce it.” The asymmetry isn’t a bug; it’s the weapon.

The book’s most unsettling revelation concerns not malicious actors but well-intentioned scientists. Their chapter on the replication crisis—demonstrating how P-hacking and publication bias have rendered vast swathes of social psychology and biomedical research potentially meaningless—doesn’t blame individual researchers. It blames a system where survival depends on publishable results and “publishable” means “statistically significant.” When Eric Turner examined FDA records of antidepressant trials, he found 74 studies: 51 appeared in journals (48 positive, 3 negative), but FDA records showed 38 positive results, 12 questionable, 24 negative. The submerged two-thirds of the iceberg—that’s where truth drowns.

What distinguishes this from other critical thinking texts is its refusal to treat readers as helpless consumers. The “Spotting Bullshit” chapter provides not vague exhortations to “think critically” but specific heuristics: Question the source. Check if claims seem too good/bad to be true. Think in orders of magnitude. When Representative Mo Brooks suggested collapsing cliffs explain rising sea levels, the authors don’t just call this stupid—they work through the Fermi estimation showing the White Cliffs of Dover eroding for a year would raise global sea levels by three angstroms, roughly one water molecule’s height. The debunking is mathematical, merciless, memorable.

Yet the book’s most radical gesture may be its final chapter on calling bullshit versus merely spotting it. Here Bergstrom and West acknowledge what academic writing typically ignores: that refutation is performative, social, risky. They distinguish the bullshit-caller from the “well, actually guy”—relevance of objection, speaker’s intention, power dynamics, judiciousness. Calling bullshit, properly done, isn’t intellectual preening. It’s a moral imperative calibrated to protect audiences from deception while avoiding the cruelty of public humiliation for honest mistakes.

The stakes accumulate quietly until suddenly they’re everywhere. A figure showing rap artists die young turns out to be right-censored data (genres haven’t existed long enough for artists to die old). A study claiming cat owners earn more than dog owners mistakes correlation for causation. Facebook’s emotional contagion experiment, Chinese facial recognition systems, Google’s failed flu predictions—each case study compounds into an argument that data literacy isn’t a nice-to-have skill but essential infrastructure for democratic survival.

What the book ultimately offers is a vocabulary for the epistemological crisis of our moment. We don’t lack information; we lack the capacity to distinguish signal from noise when both arrive dressed in the rhetoric of rigor. Bergstrom and West don’t promise to make you immune to deception—their own errors pepper the text, acknowledged with admirable humility. Instead they offer something more valuable: a framework for systematic doubt, a method for decomposing truth-claims into testable components, and the recognition that at any given time, as media theorist Neil Postman warned, “the chief source of bullshit with which you have to contend is yourself.”

In an information environment where the firehose of falsehood operates at scale, where deep fakes and synthetic media dissolve the ground beneath empirical claims, the old fact-checking model fails. You cannot debunk faster than motivated actors can generate plausible-sounding nonsense. What you can do is teach people to think in terms of base rates and selection bias, to spot truncated axes and cherry-picked data ranges, to demand that extraordinary claims bring extraordinary evidence. This isn’t a book. It’s a training manual for an asymmetric information war where the other side has already been firing for years.
