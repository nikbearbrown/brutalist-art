Part 1: Chapter-by-Chapter Summaries

Prelude

The opening strikes with a scene from The West Wing—two aides discovering that their trusted world map is fundamentally distorted. The Mercator projection, designed for 16th-century sailors, has shaped generations’ understanding of geography despite its inherent mathematical impossibility: you cannot flatten a sphere without distortion. This cartographic deception becomes the book’s governing metaphor. Just as that map promotes a Eurocentric worldview (Europe appearing larger than South America when the reverse is true), so too has the history of mathematics been warped by bias, omission, and selective storytelling.

Authors Kate Kitagawa and Timothy Revell met in a London bookshop intending to write a straightforward mathematical history. They discovered instead a subject whose origins are “beautifully varied” rather than singular, whose progress moves in tangents and dead ends rather than linear progression. The traditional narrative elevates ancient Greece as the origin point of modern mathematics, relegating Chinese, Indian, and Arabian contributions to “ethno-mathematics”—a separate, lesser category. This framing is a product of Western imperialism, not mathematical truth. The real story is one of global collaboration, with ideas ignoring borders and traveling alongside trade routes.

The authors challenge foundational myths. Calculus, typically credited to Newton and Leibniz in the 17th century, has earlier claims: a 14th-century school in Kerala, India, where Madhava of Sangamagrama explored its key ideas. Recognition matters. Thomas Edison is remembered for imperfect early lightbulbs; Madhava deserves similar acknowledgment. The book also rescues mathematicians erased by prejudice, particularly women like Sofia Kovalevsky, whose biographers distorted her legacy with gender stereotypes rather than facts. She was a brilliant mathematician forced to collect tuition directly from students when universities refused to pay her. Playwright Olaf Strindberg called a female professor “a pernicious and unpleasant phenomenon.”

The prelude ends with a manifesto: mathematics requires imagination, diversity of thought is fundamental, and this retelling aims to present mathematics “as it really is—beautifully chaotic and collaborative.”

Chapter 1: In the Beginning

Human mathematical activity emerges around 20,000 BCE with tally marks on animal bones. The Ishango bone from modern-day Uganda/DRC features three columns of scratches totaling 48, 60, and 60, with one column displaying the prime numbers 11, 13, 17, and 19. Whether this represents sophisticated mathematics or coincidence remains uncertain, but it hints at our ancestors’ numerical sophistication. The bone may have tracked lunar cycles, menstrual periods, or seasonal flooding patterns.

Oral mathematical traditions existed for millennia without leaving physical traces. The Akan people of West Africa developed sophisticated weight-and-measure systems transmitted entirely through speech, demolished by the Atlantic slave trade. Only recently have researchers reconstructed fragments from museum artifacts. These systems likely represent countless others lost to time.

Babylonia (3000–1750 BCE) produced clay tablets recording everything from legal codes to consumer complaints. One tablet from 1750 BCE preserves Nanni’s scathing review of copper merchant Ea-nasir’s poor-quality ingots and rude service—consumer power immortalized in baked clay. Babylonian mathematicians used a sexagesimal (base-60) system inherited from Sumerians, which persists in our 360-degree circles and 60-minute hours. This positional system lacked zero but functioned through context. Base 60’s superiority lies in its divisibility: 60 has twelve factors, making fractions like 1/3 expressible as simple sexagesimal decimals (0.20 in base 60) rather than repeating decimals (0.333... in base 10).

The Rhind Papyrus (circa 1550 BCE) opens with magnificent ambition: “Accurate reckoning, the entrance into the knowledge of all existing things and all obscure secrets.” Scribe Ahmes compiled 84 problems covering pyramid slope calculations, division, multiplication, and volume—concepts recognizable as modern arithmetic, algebra, and geometry. Egyptian and Babylonian mathematics overlapped despite different systems and cultures, suggesting independent discovery of fundamental truths rather than active exchange.

The Maya civilization (2600 BCE onward) embedded mathematics in astronomy. Mathematicians appeared in war paintings clutching scrolls. They used a vigesimal (base-20) system with elegant symbols: dots (one) and bars (five). Three calendars governed life: the 260-day Tzolkin for rituals, the 365-day Haab for daily events, and the 5,125-year Long Count marking cosmic cycles. Maya astronomers calculated the lunar month as 29.5302 days (modern: 29.5306) and the solar year as 365.242 days (modern: 365.242198). The Caracol Observatory’s windows aligned perfectly with astronomical events like spring equinox sunsets.

In China, mathematics intertwined with divination and imperial authority. Legend has Emperor Yu discovering a turtle bearing a magic square—a 3×3 grid where rows, columns, and diagonals all sum to fifteen. This auspicious sign granted him the “mandate of heaven,” legitimizing his rule. Mathematics equaled power, with calculations determining harvest times, military strategy, and cosmic harmony.

Chapter 2: The Turtle and the Emperor

Chinese mathematics developed within a framework where celestial observation justified political authority. Emperors relied on mathematician-astrologers to interpret signs and schedule rituals. Oracle bones (14th century BCE) preserve the earliest Chinese numerals, inscribed with questions for gods, then heated until they cracked. Diviners read the patterns as divine answers.

Counting rods revolutionized calculation. These bamboo sticks used two symbol systems alternately to represent numbers, with predominantly vertical rods indicating units and predominantly horizontal rods indicating tens. By switching systems, mathematicians could imply zero as a placeholder centuries before a symbol existed. Merchants carried rod bundles, performing additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, and even square root extractions through physical rod manipulations. Red rods represented positive numbers, black represented negatives—concepts Chinese mathematicians grasped when most cultures couldn’t imagine “minus seven sheep.”

Emperor Yu’s flood-control project demonstrated mathematics’ practical power. Over thirteen years, he studied river flows and built canals that ended devastating floods, cementing his reputation as “Great Yu, who controlled the waters.” Legend credits his confidence to that turtle-borne magic square, a cosmic endorsement.

The I Ching (Book of Changes, circa 1000–750 BCE) aimed to decode the universe through divination. Practitioners threw yarrow stalks, interpreting their landing patterns as one of 64 hexagrams—symbols combining broken (yin) and solid (yang) lines. Each hexagram corresponded to a chapter offering wisdom, though interpretation remained maddeningly opaque. Astronomer Liu Xin (10 BCE) linked hexagrams to celestial movements in his Triple Concordant System, calculating the lunar month as 29.5309 days with remarkable accuracy.

When Jesuit missionary Joachim Bouvet showed Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz the I Ching hexagrams in the 17th century, Leibniz recognized them as binary representations of numbers 0–63. His binary system, rooted in Christian concepts of existence/non-existence (1/0), perfectly matched the hexagrams’ solid/broken lines (yang/yin). Binary also appeared in the Rhind Papyrus, 2nd-century BCE Indian mathematics, and 15th-century Mangareva (French Polynesia). Different origins, identical mathematics—a pattern that recurs throughout history.

Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art became Chinese mathematics’ foundational text. Compiled circa 3rd century BCE (though claiming 1000 BCE origins), it survived Emperor Qin Shi Huang’s book burnings. Third-century mathematician Liu Hui added commentaries and proofs, approximating pi as 3.14159 (correct to five decimal places) using a 3,072-sided polygon. Fifth-century mathematician Zu Chongzhi improved this to seven decimal places with a 24,576-sided polygon—a record lasting until 15th-century Arab mathematician Jamshid al-Kashi calculated sixteen decimal places.

Liu Hui proved the Gaogu theorem (often called Pythagoras’s theorem, though Nine Chapters contains the earliest written statement). His proof rearranged right-angle triangles ingeniously: four triangles form a square with a hole, calculable two ways. Method one: side squared (c²). Method two: inner square plus four triangles ((a-b)² + 2ab). Therefore c² = a² + b². QED.

For brief moments, Chinese mathematics pursued axiomatic reasoning. Philosopher Mozi (5th century BCE) and his Moist followers wrote on geometry starting from general assumptions rather than specific examples, building logical systems to prove properties. They created the “mountain with a peak” (Pascal’s triangle) and pioneered exponentiation rules. But Confucianism’s resurgence ended this approach. Chinese mathematics returned to example-led methods, practical applications taking precedence over abstract proofs.

Chapter 3: A Town Called Alex

Alexandria, founded by Alexander the Great in 332 BCE, became the intellectual capital of the ancient world with 300,000–500,000 inhabitants. Its library and Musaeum attracted brilliant minds, supported by rulers eager to bolster their legacies through knowledge accumulation. Power and learning were inseparable.

Euclid (circa 325 BCE) arrived after Ptolemy I declared himself Pharaoh. Almost nothing is known of Euclid’s life, yet his Elements became one of history’s most influential works—second only to the Bible in editions published. Its 13 books systematized geometry through strict logical progression: define axioms (points, lines, basic assumptions), build arguments, prove theorems. Euclid’s approach—starting with simplest axioms and building toward complexity—became mathematics’ central tenet, forming Europe’s geometry teaching basis until the mid-20th century.

When Ptolemy I asked for a shortcut to mastering mathematics, Euclid supposedly replied: “There is no royal road to geometry.” The Elements codified proof as mathematics’ foundation. Once proved, theorems stand forever—unlike scientific theories that evolve with new evidence. The Gaogu/Pythagorean theorem remains as true today as when first proved, though over 100 different proofs now exist.

Pandrosion (circa 300 CE) tackled doubling the cube: given a cube’s edge length, construct another with double the volume using only straightedge and compass. Her approximation method drew sarcastic criticism from contemporary Pappus, who mansplained that she’d confused problems with theorems. Historian Friedrich Hultsch later mistranslated Pappus’s grammatically feminine references to Pandrosion, assuming her gender was an error. Only Alexander Jones’s 1988 English translation restored Pandrosion as the earliest known female mathematician in this region—an accolade previously given to Hypatia.

Hypatia (circa 350–370 CE) inherited mathematical brilliance from her father Theon, head of Alexandria’s Musaeum. She surpassed him, becoming his colleague rather than remaining his student. Her commentaries on foundational texts—Diophantus’s Arithmetica (proto-algebra), Apollonius’s Conics (geometry), Ptolemy’s Almagest (astronomy)—extended their lifespans. She added student exercises on simultaneous equations to Arithmetica and improved Ptolemy’s long-division method using tables. Her work on Conics helped preserve Apollonius’s ideas until 17th-century astronomers like Johannes Kepler used ellipses to describe planetary orbits.

Hypatia also refined the astrolabe (predicting star/planet positions) and helped student Synesius build both a plane astrolabe and a hydrometer (measuring liquid density). By age 30, she’d taken over her father’s school, becoming Alexandria’s first known female intellectual consolidating social status through teaching. Her home became a gathering place for educated people of all religions, though she herself was pagan.

In 412 CE, Bishop Cyril seized power and expelled Jewish people from Alexandria. Roman Prefect Orestes opposed him; Hypatia, Orestes’s advisor, became Cyril’s target. Rumors spread that she’d bewitched Orestes, preventing reconciliation. In 415 CE, a Christian mob dragged her from her carriage, stabbed her with pottery shards, paraded her body through streets, and burned it—punishment reserved for the worst criminals. She was not a criminal but a mathematician caught in political crossfire.

Hypatia’s story was repeatedly distorted. Pagans made her a martyr; Christians defended the killing. Damascus mocked her philosopher’s robe. Victorian novelist Charles Kingsley sexualized her, depicting nude conversion and murder scenes that inspired paintings. Only Maria Dzielska’s 1995 biography Hypatia of Alexandria reconstructed her actual life from contemporary sources, revealing a philosopher and mathematician whose “heroism lies not in the brutality she suffered at the end of her life, but in the subtle barriers she overcame each day while she lived.”

Chapter 4: The Dawn of Time

April 1883: The University of Mumbai’s Senate convened to decide what time the new Rajabai clock tower would display. This wasn’t mere timekeeping but politics. India used Bombay time (solar-based), but railways and telegrams demanded standardization. Different cities operated on different times—some minutes apart—creating chaos. A universal Indian standard time, proposed for 1906, faced fierce resistance. Some resented colonial Britain’s imposition; others rejected being ruled by another location’s sunset. The Battle of the Clocks lasted decades. Cotton mill workers rioted. The Senate voted to keep Bombay time, but after 1947 independence, India adopted universal time.

For most of human history, tracking years/months/seasons mattered more than hours/minutes/seconds. Babylonian astronomers (14th century BCE) meticulously recorded celestial movements on clay tablets, using astrolabes for precision. Their calendar had twelve lunar months beginning with each new crescent moon, with leap months added when discrepancies appeared. Astronomer Kidinnu (3rd century BCE) improved calculations, discovering the moon’s speed varies periodically—knowledge that made the Babylonian calendar more reliable. Alexandria and Rome adopted it.

China’s Shang Dynasty (1600–1046 BCE) used a 360-day lunar calendar adjusted for seasons. Emperors issued decrees based on calendar dates for warfare, rituals, and harvests. The Maya used three calendars simultaneously: the 260-day Tzolkin (rituals/business/illness diagnosis), the 365-day Haab (18 months of 20 days plus 5 extra days), and the Long Count (5,125-year cycles from creation: 12 August 3113 BCE). The 2012 “Doomsday” panic misunderstood this: the Maya saw cycles of rebirth, not endings.

Recent archaeological work suggests the Hittite Empire’s Yazılıkaya sanctuary (before 1500 BCE) functioned as a three-dimensional calendar. Queen Puduhepa organized Hittite gods into a pantheon with sun goddess Arinna as queen. The sanctuary’s 90+ rock reliefs may have tracked lunar months: 12 identically shaped male deities (synodic months/year), 30 uniformly shaped deities (days/month), possibly 19 female deities (solar years before calendar adjustment). Skepticism remains, but the mathematical elegance is compelling.

Timekeeping technology evolved slowly. Sundials worked by day; water clocks by night (filling/emptying bowls with markings). Ismail al-Jazari’s 1206 elephant clock was mechanical marvel: a bowl slowly sank in a bucket over 30 minutes, pulling a string that released a ball into a serpent’s mouth, causing a bird to chirp and a human automaton to strike a symbol. Accurate but massive and expensive.

Sand glasses became popular on 16th-century sea voyages (30-minute increments). Sailors had to flip them every half hour—annoying but vital for navigation. Christian Huygens and Salomon Coster built the first pendulum clock (1656) based on Galileo’s 1602 observations that pendulum periods remain constant. Balance wheels, invented in Germany (1510), enabled pocket watches. Charles II of England popularized them in the 17th century; mass production eventually made them affordable.

In England, coach services ran on schedules, but solar-based local times (minutes apart between towns) created confusion. Railways worsened this. Greenwich Mean Time spread via telegraph lines alongside railways by mid-19th century. Other countries developed similar systems. India’s Battle of the Clocks ended with post-1947 universal time.

Einstein’s special relativity (early 20th century) revealed time isn’t universal but relative—it changes based on location/speed/mass. GPS satellites experience time dilation: their clocks would drift 38 microseconds/day (10-kilometer positional error) without adjustment. Atomic clocks (one-second error per 100 million years) now define the second: cesium atoms oscillating 9,192,631,770 times.

But what is time? Einstein’s general relativity treats spacetime as a physical, four-dimensional entity warped by gravity, producing time dilation. Yet equations have no inherent direction—time could run backward. Thermodynamics’ second law (entropy always increases) may explain time’s forward flow: things naturally jumble up. Reconciling gravity/time with quantum physics remains unsolved. When achieved, it will force fundamental rethinking of time’s nature—paradigm shifts as Earth-shattering as learning the planet is round.

Chapter 5: On the Origin or Origins of Zero

Early 2010s: A four-year-old sits before a computer screen showing two squares containing dots (0, 1, 2, 4, or 8). Task: select the box with fewer dots. When comparing boxes with 1+ dots, children succeeded 75% of the time. Add zero, and success dropped below 50%—random chance. Psychologists Elizabeth Brannon and Dustin Merritt’s experiment revealed something profound: zero doesn’t come naturally. Children grasp other numbers first, then zero later. This mirrors humanity’s journey.

Zero feels primitive, foundational—how could you build mathematics without starting at zero? But zero arrived long after other numbers, taking hundreds of years to be understood as a number rather than mere notation. This development was momentous and murky, with multiple origins and interpretations.

The Golden Age of India (4th–6th centuries) saw zero’s importance fully appreciated. Chandra Gupta I controlled vast northern territories, expanded by successors through invasions. Mathematics was highly valued: “As crests on peacocks’ heads, as gems on snakes’ heads, so is mathematics at the top of all branches of knowledge.” Astronomy and mathematics developed distinctly, though influenced by exchanges with Egypt, Babylonia, and China.

Few details survive about women’s scholarly roles. Upper-class women received education and could pursue study but weren’t considered equal and couldn’t pursue careers. Despite restrictions, Gupta women had more freedom than under some earlier dynasties, participating in religious rituals and public ceremonies.

Jain mathematicians (4th/3rd century BCE onward) specialized in large numbers, obsessed with measuring an eternal, continuous universe. Time periods like Shirsha Prahelika (756×10¹¹×8,400,000²⁸ days—a 208-digit number) and distances like Raju (roughly a million kilometers) demonstrated this fascination. They categorized numbers as enumerable-countable, enumerable-not-countable, and infinite. They split infinity into five types: infinite in one direction, two directions, area, everywhere, and perpetually—the first known consideration of multiple infinity sizes, an idea not revisited until Georg Cantor’s 19th-century work.

Jains examined permutations/combinations (63 ways to combine six tastes: bitter, sour, salty, astringent, sweet, hot) and discovered “Meru Prastara” (Pascal’s triangle). They pioneered exponentiation: “The first square root multiplied by the second square root is the cube of the second square root” (8^(1/2) × 8^(1/4) = 8^(3/4))—rediscovered by John Napier and Joost Bürgi circa 1600.

Aryabhata I (476–550 CE) worked at Nalanda, a Buddhist research complex resembling modern universities. His Aryabhatiya (circa 120 Sanskrit verses) synthesized mathematical knowledge, combining astronomy/cosmology/mathematics. He noted multiplication by 10 promotes numbers to the next higher place—evidence of the decimal system, though not fully realized. He provided methods for square/cube roots and tackled early algebra: “If the difference in known quantities of two persons is divided by the difference in their unknown amount, the results shall give the value of the unknown quantity.” Example: two farmers with 100 rupees + 6 cows versus 60 rupees + 8 cows (equal value)—what’s one cow worth?

Aryabhata approximated pi as 3.1415926535 (eight decimal places correct), though his method is unclear. He asserted Earth rotates on its axis, estimating 23 hours, 56 minutes, 4.1 seconds (remarkably accurate). This claim was largely ignored until 1851, when Léon Foucault’s pendulum demonstrated Earth’s rotation.

Aryabhata invented an alphabetic number system using Sanskrit’s 33 consonants (assigned to 1–9, 10, 20...100) and vowels (powers of 10). This allowed compact large-number representation. Hidden within is zero’s suggestion: calculations in Aryabhatiya require it.

Brahmagupta (7th century, western India) made zero explicit. Son of astronomer Vishnu Gupta, he inherited Aryabhata’s work and wrote Brahmasphutasiddhanta (”The Correctly Established Doctrine of Brahma”) at age 30. The section on zero was transformative.

Maya civilization (300–200 BCE) probably developed a zero placeholder first—a shell symbol. Babylonians had a symbol by 400–300 BCE (initially colon-like, later tilted cuneiform). Indian mathematicians began using a dot circa 6th/7th centuries. The mysterious Bakhshali manuscript (birch folios from Pakistan, discovered 1881) contains dots representing zero, but radio-carbon dating produced conflicting results: circa 300, 700, and 900 CE. Some argue this proves Indian zero by 300 CE; others say post-900. Ink hasn’t been tested.

Similar zero symbols appeared simultaneously elsewhere: Cambodia (683 CE), Indonesia (circa same period), India’s oldest circle zero at Gwalior Hindu temple (875 CE). The Sumatra region (Srivijaya/Khmer empires) was influenced by India/China/Islam/Buddhism. Zero may have been imported or independently developed locally, with transmission potentially running either direction.

Brahmagupta made zero a true number, not just placeholder. In Brahmasphutasiddhanta, he explained: “When zero is added to a number or subtracted from a number, the number remains unchanged; and a number multiplied by zero becomes zero.” In modern notation: x + 0 = x, x - 0 = x, x × 0 = 0. He described these using fortunes (positive) and debts (negative). Multiplying by zero gives zero. Division by zero: “Zero divided by zero is zero”—incorrect, but calculus (nearly a millennium later) would clarify this.

This shift from placeholder to true number was monumental. Digital technology—binary mathematics of zero and one—underpins modern computation. Every pixel, calculation, datum relies on zero’s power. But why only India? Philosophy may explain. While some cultures feared/disdained nothingness, India embraced it. Sanskrit word shunya (empty/void) comes from Buddhism. Words for atmosphere (akasha) and sky (kha) denoted zero. Aryabhata used kha for empty positions. Others called zero nirguna brahman (attributeless truth). Nothingness was important; the infinitude of the world emerges from emptiness. This motivated Brahmagupta to integrate the concept more deeply than anyone before.

Zero’s spread was slow. Arabic traders embraced it after reading Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi’s 9th-century Concerning the Hindu Art of Reckoning. Al-Khwarizmi saw zero’s utility and promoted Indo-Arabic numerals. Italian mathematician Leonardo Fibonacci encountered zero in late-12th-century Bougie (Béjaïa, Algeria) among Arabic traders, studying al-Khwarizmi’s book. His 1202 Liber Abaci (Book of Calculation) outlined the Indo-Arabic system including zero, translating Arabic sifr (empty) to Latin zephirum—Italians changed this to “zero.” But Fibonacci used zero only as placeholder, not true number. European intellectuals, prejudiced against Muslim scholars, didn’t take Arabic mathematics seriously. Florence banned zero and all Hindu-Arabic numerals as supposedly easier to alter fraudulently.

Europe eventually adopted Indo-Arabic numerals during the 16th-century Renaissance—usefulness couldn’t be ignored forever. East Asia, exposed via Arabic traders and Christian missionaries, resisted due to well-established large-abacus traditions. Change came in the 19th century to facilitate international trade/communication.

Elsewhere, mathematics was well-established. Sub-Saharan Africa’s Sankoré Madrassa (Timbuktu, Mali Empire) was a major learning center. Early-14th-century King Mansa Musa (one of history’s wealthiest people) invested in books from Arab traders, hiring scholars to study them. Mathematics wasn’t initially studied—base-2 and base-20 systems dominated Africa, so base-10 held little appeal. But local traders developed innovative mental calculation methods for large numbers (Susu/Tantine/Esusu). In Mesoamerica, the Inca Empire (13th century) used quipus—alpaca/llama fleece or cotton strings with knots recording census figures, tax allocations, names, stories, ideas. Using base-10, knots acted like abacus beads: string spreadsheets. Incas used quipus until 1583, when Spanish invaders banned them, believing they recorded offerings to non-Christian gods.

The first people outside India to appreciate Brahmagupta’s zero work lived on the Arabian Peninsula. Here, in the 8th century, a learning center unlike any other would rise, originating some of mathematics’ most important concepts.

Part 2: Bridge Section

What emerges from these opening chapters isn’t a linear march from primitive tally marks to sophisticated theorems but something far more interesting: a portrait of mathematics as simultaneous invention, cultural translation, and political power. The Ishango bone’s prime numbers may be coincidence or genius—we’ll never know for certain. What we do know is that from Babylon to Beijing, from Alexandria to Mesoamerica, humans independently discovered similar mathematical truths while embedding them in radically different cultural frameworks.

The tension between practical and abstract mathematics runs throughout. Chinese mathematicians prioritized example-led methods and computational efficiency with counting rods and abacuses. Greek mathematicians built axiomatic systems from first principles. Neither approach is superior—they’re complementary lenses on the same fundamental reality. When Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz recognized his binary system in the I Ching‘s hexagrams, he wasn’t discovering that Chinese and European mathematics were identical but that mathematical truth transcends cultural origin.

Gender and power shaped who could practice mathematics and whose contributions survived. Pandrosion’s work was mocked by Pappus; her gender was erased by translators. Hypatia’s brilliance was weaponized in political conflict, her legacy distorted into nude martyrdom paintings rather than celebrated for conquering daily barriers. Ban Zhao taught mathematics to empresses while remaining invisible in observatory records. The pattern repeats: exceptional women broke through, then were written out or caricatured.

Zero’s origin story encapsulates the book’s larger argument. The Maya had placeholder zero first. Babylonians developed symbols. But Brahmagupta made it a true number because Indian philosophy valued nothingness. European Christianity, viewing void as godless, resisted for centuries. Florence banned zero as fraudulent. Mathematical truth is universal, but cultural context determines when and where breakthroughs occur.

The stage is now set for mathematics’ next great leap: the synthesis of global knowledge in medieval Baghdad’s House of Wisdom, where scholars from across continents would translate, critique, and combine Greek, Indian, Chinese, and Persian mathematics into something greater than the sum of its parts.

Part 3: The Literary Review Essay

The Map We Trusted Was Wrong: On the Beautiful Chaos of Mathematical History

There’s a peculiar violence in the way we teach mathematics. Not in the theorems themselves—those are eternal, proven, immutable—but in the stories we tell about their origins. Open any standard textbook and you’ll find a Greatest Hits collection: Pythagoras and his triangles, Euclid and his elements, Newton and Leibniz battling over calculus. The narrative arc bends predictably from ancient Greece through Renaissance Europe to modern America, with occasional exotic detours (usually involving the number zero) before returning to the main road. It’s clean. It’s linear. It’s fundamentally dishonest.

Kate Kitagawa and Timothy Revell’s The Secret Lives of Numbers does what the best revisionist histories do: it doesn’t simply add footnotes to the accepted story but reveals that the accepted story is itself a footnote to something vastly larger and stranger. Meeting in a London bookshop to discuss a “straightforward” mathematical history, the authors quickly discovered that straightforward is precisely what mathematics’ past is not. The subject’s origins aren’t singular but “beautifully varied.” Progress doesn’t march forward but “jumps around the planet, goes off on tangents, on adventures, and sometimes down dead ends.” Most importantly, the elevation of ancient Greece as the wellspring of modern mathematics turns out to be not historical fact but the successful colonization of mathematical memory by Western imperialism.

Consider calculus, that crown jewel of mathematical achievement that lets us describe how things change over time—everything from rocket trajectories to blood flow. Ask who invented it and you’ll hear about the spectacular feud between Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in 17th-century Europe, complete with accusations of plagiarism, national pride, and Princess Caroline of Wales attempting to broker peace. The Royal Society investigated; Newton, as its president, drafted the report declaring himself first inventor. The dispute turned from mathematics into politics into religion, fueled by their opposing ideas about Protestant reunification. Both men maintained until death that they’d gotten there first. History accepts they worked independently, building on others’ work. Case closed.

Except. In 14th-century Kerala, India, a school led by Madhava of Sangamagrama was exploring the key ideas that make calculus possible: infinite series, rates of change, finding limits. His work was refined by successive mathematicians and recorded in texts like Rationales in Mathematical Astronomy, which contains actual proofs using tools intrinsic to calculus—differentials, summations, limits. The calculations are there. The techniques work. This wasn’t primitive proto-calculus but sophisticated mathematical theory, developed three centuries before Newton and Leibniz ever put pen to paper.

Kitagawa and Revell don’t claim Madhava “invented” calculus in the modern sense—the Kerala school’s work was neither complete nor perfect. But then, neither were the first lightbulbs, which burned out quickly and blackened glass due to design flaws. We still recognize Thomas Edison’s role in that 19th-century invention. Why not Madhava’s? The answer has nothing to do with mathematics and everything to do with which knowledge systems European academies deemed worthy of attention. “The Catholic Church was so opposed to Confucian traditions,” the authors note, “that they rejected Chinese knowledge in its entirety, whatever the subject matter.”

What makes this omission particularly galling is that mathematical truth is supposed to be universal. A theorem proved in 3rd-century China is as valid as one proved in 20th-century America. Unlike scientific theories that evolve with new evidence, mathematical proofs stand forever. The Gaogu theorem (often called Pythagorean, though Nine Chapters contains the earliest written statement) remains true whether you prove it by rearranging triangles in ancient India or drawing diagrams in Renaissance Italy. Over 100 different proofs now exist. The mathematics doesn’t care about authorship. But history does.

The book’s central metaphor—the distorted Mercator projection map from The West Wing—works because it captures both the what and the why of historical distortion. That map was designed for 16th-century sailors navigating oceans, not for understanding geopolitics. It served its purpose. But when it became the dominant representation, people began believing Europe was larger than South America (it’s half the size) and that Germany sat in Earth’s middle rather than its northernmost quarter. The map didn’t lie, exactly. It just presented one perspective as if it were objective truth.

So too with mathematical history. The focus on ancient Greece wasn’t entirely fabricated—Euclid’s Elements genuinely influenced European education for two millennia; his axiomatic method (state assumptions, build arguments, prove theorems) became mathematics’ foundation. But presenting this as the mathematical tradition erases the simultaneous sophistication of Chinese counting-rod calculations, Babylonian sexagesimal astronomy, Maya calendrical precision (lunar month: 29.5302 days; modern: 29.5306), and Indian algebraic innovations. Each culture developed mathematics suited to its needs: flood control in China, inheritance law in Babylon, agricultural timing in Mesoamerica, zero as philosophical concept in India.

The book’s structure mirrors this multiplicity. Rather than forcing global mathematics into a linear timeline, Kitagawa and Revell move geographically and thematically, letting each chapter illuminate a different aspect of mathematical development. We see Oracle bone numerals in Shang Dynasty China (base-10 but non-positional), then jump to Mesoamerican vigesimal systems (base-20), then circle back to Babylonian sexagesimal (base-60) and Egyptian hieroglyphic numbers. The effect is disorienting at first—where are we? when?—but ultimately liberating. Mathematics doesn’t have a single origin story. It has hundreds, unfolding simultaneously across continents, sometimes influencing each other through trade routes, sometimes arriving independently at identical conclusions.

This simultaneous invention is mathematics’ most beautiful feature. When Leibniz saw the I Ching‘s 64 hexagrams in 1703, he was “astounded” to find they perfectly matched his binary system. He’d developed binary from Christian concepts of existence/non-existence (1/0); Chinese philosophy had generated it from yin/yang. The underlying mathematics was identical. Binary also appears in ancient Egypt’s Rhind Papyrus, 2nd-century BCE Indian mathematics, and 15th-century Mangareva (French Polynesia) counting systems. The origins differed; the mathematical fundamentals remained the same.

Yet this universality makes the erasure of certain mathematicians more, not less, troubling. If mathematical truth transcends culture, why do we still frame its history so provincially? Partly because mathematics is never practiced in isolation from the people doing it—and some people have been systematically excluded or distorted in the telling.

Take Hypatia of Alexandria, whose story Kitagawa and Revell rescue from layers of ideological sediment. Born circa 350–370 CE, she was daughter of mathematician Theon but quickly surpassed him, becoming colleague rather than student. She wrote commentaries on Diophantus’s Arithmetica (adding student exercises on simultaneous equations), Apollonius’s Conics (preserving ideas that influenced Kepler’s elliptical orbits), and Ptolemy’s Almagest (improving astronomical calculation methods). She refined the astrolabe, helped students build scientific instruments, and by age 30 had taken over her father’s school—the first woman to consolidate social status as Alexandria’s renowned female intellectual.

In 415 CE, Bishop Cyril’s political feud with Roman Prefect Orestes turned murderous. Hypatia, Orestes’s advisor, became Cyril’s target. Rumors spread that she’d “bewitched” him with magic. A Christian mob dragged her from her carriage, stabbed her with pottery shards, paraded her body through streets, and burned it—treatment reserved for the worst criminals. She wasn’t a criminal. She was a mathematician caught in political crossfire, teaching in a cosmopolitan city descending into religious violence.

But that’s not how history initially remembered her. Pagans made her a martyr for their persecuted faith. Christians defended the killing. Damascus mocked her philosopher’s robe (no such quips about men’s attire). Seventeenth-century scholar John Toland praised her as a model educated woman—then used her story to attack Christianity. Christian writer Thomas Lewis retaliated, calling her “a most impudent school mistress.” Victorian novelist Charles Kingsley stripped her mathematical talents from his popular novel, focusing instead on her body: the climax features nude conversion and murder scenes beneath Christ’s image. These inspired nude paintings. Later 19th-century works finally depicted her wearing robes.

Only in 1995 did Maria Dzielska’s biography Hypatia of Alexandria reconstruct her actual life from contemporary documents, “undistorted by historical idealisation.” The assessment: Hypatia was a philosopher and mathematician whose “heroism lies not in the brutality she suffered at the end of her life, but in the subtle barriers she overcame each day while she lived.” Subtle barriers. Excellent phrase for what women mathematicians faced: not always dramatic exclusion but constant low-level resistance. Being denied university positions despite superior qualifications. Having their names excluded from publications they authored. Being described as “impudent” or sexualized rather than recognized for their work.

Sofia Kovalevsky’s story follows similar contours. Russian child prodigy whose strict father believed educated daughters brought family shame. She studied calculus on wallpaper (her father’s old mathematical notes) and entered a white marriage to escape his control. At Berlin, Karl Weierstrass tested her with problems for his most experienced students; she solved them easily. She produced groundbreaking work on Saturn’s rings (using infinite series to show fluid rings would be egg-shaped, not symmetric ovals) and other topics—more than enough for a doctorate, except Berlin barred women from the required oral defense. Weierstrass packaged three papers and sent them to University of Göttingen, which granted doctorates in absentia if work was exceptional enough. It was.

Yet when Kovalevsky won the 1888 Prix Bordin for her “mathematical mermaid”—solving spinning-top motion that had stumped Euler and Lagrange—some biographers dismissed her achievement. Influential mathematician Felix Klein claimed her work merely mimicked supervisor Weierstrass’s style, suggesting he deserved credit. Others emphasized her physical appearance rather than mathematics: “exceptionally beautiful woman, whose physical attraction no doubt contributed to her appeal.” Playwright Olga Strindberg called female professors “a pernicious and unpleasant phenomenon.” After Kovalevsky’s death, biographers portrayed her as femme fatale using looks to manipulate great male mathematicians rather than having mathematical talent herself.

There is currently little evidence Kovalevsky was romantically involved with Weierstrass or colleague Mittag-Leffler (though Weierstrass had feelings for her). But even if she had been, it shouldn’t alter our view of her mathematics. Personal relationships influence all careers—men recommend friends, hire former collaborators, nominate colleagues for honors. Kovalevsky succeeded in a system not designed for her. As she wrote: “It seems to me that the poet has only to perceive that which others do not perceive, to look deeper than others look. And the mathematician must do the same thing.” Looking deeper than anybody had looked before—that’s the real story, not her appearance or alleged affairs.

The book’s commitment to factual accuracy sometimes feels almost aggressive, particularly around the thorny question of who gets credit for what. When discussing the Kerala school’s work on calculus, Kitagawa and Revell carefully note that “we are still uncovering exactly how much Madhava and his school in Kerala knew” and that historian George Gheverghese Joseph’s suggestion that Newton and Leibniz may have been influenced by Kerala (via Jesuit missionaries bringing manuscripts from India) “is not fully underpinned by existing evidence and is still under investigation.” They refuse easy answers: “There is still work to be done on understanding exactly who knew what and when.”

This scrupulousness extends to their handling of zero’s murky origins. Maya civilization probably developed placeholder zero first (shell symbol, 300–200 BCE). Babylonians had a symbol by 400–300 BCE. Indian mathematicians began using a dot circa 6th/7th centuries. The Bakhshali manuscript’s zero might date to 300 CE or 900 CE—radio-carbon dating of different folios gave conflicting results, and ink hasn’t been tested. Similar symbols appeared simultaneously in Cambodia (683 CE) and Indonesia. “The exact story of how the modern symbol for zero came into existence,” they write, “all rather murky.”

But Brahmagupta’s 7th-century achievement is clear: making zero a true number, not just placeholder. In Brahmasphutasiddhanta (”The Correctly Established Doctrine of Brahma”), he explained arithmetic with zero: x + 0 = x, x - 0 = x, x × 0 = 0. He got division wrong (0/0 = 0), but that would take calculus to sort out. The shift from placeholder to number was “monumental”—without it, digital technology (binary mathematics of zero/one) wouldn’t exist. Every pixel, calculation, datum relies on zero as true number.

Why only India? Philosophy. While some cultures feared/disdained nothingness, India embraced it. Sanskrit shunya (empty/void) comes from Buddhism. Words for atmosphere (akasha) and sky (kha) denoted zero. Others called it nirguna brahman (attributeless truth). Nothingness was important; infinitude emerges from emptiness. This motivated Brahmagupta to integrate the concept more deeply than anyone before. Mathematical truth may be universal, but cultural context determines when/where breakthroughs occur.

The book’s most radical historiographical move is treating mathematics as fundamentally collaborative—not despite cultural differences but because of them. When 9th-century Baghdad’s House of Wisdom assembled scholars to translate Greek, Syriac, Sanskrit, and Persian texts into Arabic, they weren’t just preserving knowledge but actively synthesizing it. Caliph al-Ma’mun funded observatories to verify contradictory astronomical data from different traditions. The Banu Musa brothers questioned Ptolemy’s solar-year calculation; Thabit ibn Qurra refined it to 365 days, 6 hours, 9 minutes, 12 seconds (off by two seconds). Mathematician al-Khwarizmi wrote Concerning the Hindu Art of Reckoning (introducing Indian decimal system to Arabic readers) and Al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr wal-Muqabala (giving us “algebra” from al-jabr, meaning completion/balancing).

This synthesis didn’t happen in Europe. The Catholic Church opposed Confucian traditions, rejecting Chinese knowledge entirely. European academics, prejudiced against Muslim scholars, ignored Arabic mathematics. Florence banned zero as fraudulent. When Jesuit missionary Joachim Bouvet brought the I Ching to Europe and showed Leibniz the hexagrams, the Catholic Church banned further Chinese learning, believing European and Chinese histories couldn’t share gods. Leibniz was fascinated (recognizing binary) but politically isolated. Only in China itself did grand synthesis occur: mathematicians like Mei Wending (17th century) carefully comparing Chinese, Western, and Muslim astronomy, concluding “number and principle are united, and China and the West do not differ.”

What emerges is less a history of mathematics than a portrait of what mathematics could be if we took its global origins seriously. Not “ethnic mathematics” versus “real mathematics,” but integrated traditions where base-60 Babylonian sexagesimal astronomy converses with base-10 Indian decimal notation and base-2 binary systems from multiple origins. Where example-led Chinese computational methods complement Greek axiomatic proofs. Where practical Egyptian surveying techniques inform abstract geometric theorems.

The book’s title—The Secret Lives of Numbers—undersells this ambition slightly. These aren’t secret lives so much as suppressed or ignored ones, histories deemed unimportant by those writing the dominant narrative. Numbers themselves have no secrets; they’re magnificently indifferent to human politics. But the people who discovered them, developed them, taught them? Their lives were shaped by gender, race, empire, religion, geography, timing. Hypatia died in political violence. Kovalevsky couldn’t get paid positions. Benjamin Banneker’s almanac endorsement noted his race: “a very extraordinary performance, considering the colour of the author.” David Blackwell sent 100+ job applications; received three offers. Alan Turing, after cracking Enigma and pioneering computing, was chemically castrated for homosexuality and died at 41.

Mathematics is supposed to be the most meritocratic discipline—prove your theorem and it stands forever, regardless of who you are. In practice, who gets to prove theorems, whose work gets published, whose names attach to discoveries, whose contributions are remembered versus erased—all this remains depressingly political. The book’s epilogue notes that although women constitute ~50% of PhD students across all subjects in OECD countries, in mathematics the proportion is far lower: 29% in US (2013–14), 32% in Europe. The first woman to win a Fields Medal (arguably mathematics’ highest honor) was Maryam Mirzakhani in 2014—90 people had won it previously; 88 were men. The second woman, Maryna Viazovska, won in 2022. The first woman to win the Abel Prize was Karen Uhlenbeck in 2019, for the prize’s 20th awarding.

These are improvements. They’re also damning statistics for a discipline that claims to value only truth and proof. If mathematical ability were truly gender-neutral, we’d expect rough parity. The disparity suggests systemic exclusion—not always dramatic (burning books, murdering teachers) but constant low-level resistance. Being paid half what men earn for the same work (Harvard computers). Being denied professorships despite qualifications (Kovalevsky unpaid for years). Having credit stolen or achievements dismissed (Klein undermining Kovalevsky; obituaries sexualizing her). Being literally banned from universities (Emmy Noether, despite David Hilbert’s exasperation: “I do not see that the sex of the candidate is an argument against her. After all, we are a university, not a bathhouse”).

Kitagawa and Revell don’t dwell on these injustices with righteous anger—their tone stays measured, almost clinical—but the cumulative weight is crushing. They let the facts speak: dates of first female PhD (1678, Elena Lucrezia Cornaro Piscopia, philosophy; 1874, Sofia Kovalevsky, mathematics); dates of first female academy memberships (Royal Society 1945, French Academy 1979); dates women could attend Cambridge lectures (Gertrude Colles 1873, unofficially); dates women received Cambridge degrees (1948). The timeline is absurd. Universities existed for centuries before admitting women. When they finally did, women proved immediately capable—sometimes surpassing male peers—yet faced continued discrimination in hiring, pay, and recognition.

The book ends hopefully, citing Organizations like CIMPA (International Centre for Pure and Applied Mathematics) promoting research in low/middle-income countries, and the African Institute for Mathematical Sciences providing postgraduate training through a Pan-African network. Mathematics is “opening up,” becoming “more global than ever before.” More women pursue mathematics; more countries develop research capacity. Unsolved problems await: Riemann hypothesis, P versus NP, Navier-Stokes, twin prime conjecture. The James Webb Space Telescope will see the universe’s first light; the Shinshan telescope will catalog a billion galaxies. “We are standing on the surface of a small, oblate spheroid staring into the mathematical cosmos, with twinkling theorems out there waiting to be discovered.”

This optimism feels earned because it’s tempered by clear-eyed assessment of how far we’ve come and how far remains. The book’s greatest achievement is demonstrating that mathematical history doesn’t require cheerleading or special pleading—just accuracy. Tell the full story: Madhava’s calculus alongside Newton’s, Kovalevsky’s spinning tops alongside Euler’s, al-Khwarizmi’s algebra alongside Descartes’ coordinates, Ban Zhao teaching empresses alongside Euclid teaching at Alexandria’s library. Don’t invent accomplishments; don’t minimize barriers. Simply refuse to accept that the traditional story—ancient Greece to Renaissance Europe to modern America—represents mathematical truth.

Because here’s what’s exhilarating: once you abandon that narrow narrative, mathematics explodes into something vastly richer. You discover counting rods and magic squares and hexagram binary and sexagesimal astronomy and palace trigonometry and quipu string accounting. You find Ishango bone prime numbers that may be genius or coincidence but either way connect you to humans 20,000 years ago scratching mathematics into baboon fibula. You encounter the Kerala school proving theorems while Europe still fumbled with Roman numerals. You watch Chinese mathematician Mei Wending in 17th-century Beijing carefully comparing three mathematical traditions and concluding they’re fundamentally compatible: “number and principle are united, and China and the West do not differ.”

That sentence could serve as the book’s thesis. Mathematical truth is universal. The paths to discovering it are beautifully varied. Progress isn’t linear but chaotic, collaborative, jumping continents and centuries. Some ideas emerge simultaneously (binary in China/India/Egypt/Polynesia); others travel slowly through trade routes (Arabic numerals from India through Baghdad to Europe over centuries). Some mathematicians work in isolation (Ramanujan’s notebooks); others in teams (Hardy-Littlewood’s 100+ papers); still others are erased then rediscovered (Pandrosion’s gender, Madhava’s calculus).

The map we trusted was wrong. Not maliciously, perhaps—Mercator designed it for sailors, not geopolitics—but wrong nonetheless. Kitagawa and Revell have drawn a new one, messier and more accurate. Europe is no longer oversized. Greece is no longer the origin. Mathematics doesn’t flow in one direction (West to East, ancient to modern, simple to complex) but multidirectionally, recursively, in tangents and circles and spirals. The resulting picture is harder to teach, harder to summarize, harder to fit into satisfying narratives of progress.

It’s also true. And in mathematics, truth is supposed to matter more than convenience.
