I used to think editing meant running Grammarly and fixing the red squiggles. Then I published my first Substack post. The post wasn’t bad, exactly, it just didn’t do anything. No hook. No structure. No reason for a stranger to keep reading past the third sentence.

That’s the gap nobody talks about when they tell you to “just start writing online.” The gap between having ideas and presenting them in a way that earns attention. I didn’t know how to close it until I tried a tool called Eddy the Editor.

What Eddy Actually Is

Eddy is an AI-powered editorial mentor built as a custom prompt system for Claude, designed by Prof. Brown as part of the Humanitarians AI toolkit. It reflects the same philosophy behind all of Prof. Brown’s educational tools: don’t do the work for the student, give them a structured framework and let them develop judgment through practice.

Think of Eddy as a writing coach, a structural editor, and a Substack publishing strategist rolled into one, except it never gets tired, never sugarcoats, and never charges by the hour.

You paste in your draft. Eddy runs it through a seven-stage review framework that covers everything from your headline to your publish timing. But here’s what makes it different from every other “AI writing assistant” you’ve tried: Eddy doesn’t rewrite your article for you. It tells you exactly what’s broken, exactly where it’s broken, and exactly how to fix it, then leaves the writing to you.

That distinction matters, and it’s central to how Prof. Brown thinks about AI in education. As he’s written extensively on Substack and elsewhere, the goal isn’t to automate thinking, it’s to develop what he calls “irreducibly human judgment.” Tools that rewrite your work make you a worse writer. Tools that teach you to see your own problems make you a better one.

The Seven Stages (and Why the Order Matters)

Most beginners make the same mistake I did: they fiddle with word choice in a paragraph that shouldn’t exist in the first place. Eddy’s framework is designed to prevent that. It works top-down, from the biggest problems to the smallest, so you never waste time polishing a sentence that’s getting cut.

Here’s what each stage does:

Stage 1 - The Quick Verdict. Before anything else, Eddy answers four questions: What is this piece actually about? Is the central idea clear? What is the single biggest problem? And it assigns a Substack Readiness Score from 1 to 10. This alone is worth more than most feedback you’ll get in a college workshop, because it forces a diagnosis before treatment.

Stage 2 - Structural Edit. This is the bones. Eddy evaluates your headline (does it make a specific promise or just name a topic?), your hook (do the first three sentences pass the eight-second rule?), your section flow (does each section earn its place?), and your call to action. It even checks whether your sections fall in the 150–300 word sweet spot that keeps readers scrolling.

Stage 3 - Line Edit Highlights. Only after the structure is solid does Eddy look at sentences, and even then, it limits itself to five specific passages. It tells you the line, the issue, and the fix. No vague “this could be stronger.” Just: “Your transition in paragraph four repeats the setup from paragraph two. Cut the first sentence and lead with the example instead.”

Stage 4 - Substack SEO & Discoverability. This is where most student writers go blank. Eddy checks your title for search keywords, recommends a clean URL slug, suggests three to five tags, and audits whether your formatting follows an answer-first structure that performs well on Substack’s recommendation algorithm.

Stage 5 - Image & Visual Direction. You get a hero image concept, a ready-to-use Midjourney prompt, alt text, and technical specs. No more Googling “free stock photo” and settling for something generic.

Stage 6 - Publish Strategy. Eddy doesn’t just say “post on Tuesday morning.” It tailors the window to your niche and gives you a 60-Minute Surge Protocol: publish, let the email auto-send, drop a pull quote on social 30–60 minutes later, and respond to every comment in the first hour. That first hour matters more than most people realize for Substack’s distribution.

Stage 7 - Publish-Ready Checklist. A final sweep across content quality, technical setup, visuals, and ethics. It flags the one item you’re most likely to forget at publish time, which, if you’re anything like me — is alt text.

How It Changed My Writing Process

Before Eddy, my process was: write a draft, reread it twice, convince myself it was fine, and publish. After Eddy, I actually have a process.

The biggest shift was learning to separate structural thinking from sentence-level thinking. The Quick Verdict in Stage 1 acts like a phase gate, it forces you to confront the macro problems before you disappear into the micro. I can’t tell you how many times I’ve pasted in a draft feeling confident, only to read Eddy’s verdict and realize my “article about remote work” was actually three different articles stitched together with no central argument.

The hook feedback in Stage 2 was humbling. I had a habit of opening with throat-clearing sentences. “In recent years, many people have started to…” that Eddy flags as instant-exit hooks. It taught me that the first three sentences aren’t an introduction. They’re an audition.

And the SEO stage revealed something I’d never considered: my headlines were naming topics instead of making claims. “Remote Work in 2026” tells the reader nothing. “Remote Work Is Making Junior Employees Worse at Their Jobs” gives them a reason to click, agree, or argue. Every one of those responses is good for a writer.

Why Every Student Writer Needs an AI Editor

There’s a kindness problem in writing education. Workshop feedback tends to cluster around two poles: “I really liked this” (unhelpful) and “this didn’t work for me” (vague). Neither one teaches you to see the structural machinery underneath your own prose.

Eddy occupies a third space. It’s direct without being cruel. It focuses on the writing, not the writer. And it’s relentlessly specific, every critique names the exact line, paragraph, or section, and every problem comes with a fix attached.

This fits Prof. Brown’s broader argument about AI literacy. Through Humanitarians AI and his courses at Northeastern, he’s pushed the idea that students learn best when they’re building real things under real constraints, not passively consuming tutorials. Eddy applies that same “learn by doing” ethos to writing: it doesn’t lecture you about craft in the abstract. It hands you back your own draft with the problems marked and says, now fix it.

This matters for student writers especially, because we’re at the stage where habits are forming. If you learn to self-edit with a structural framework now, you carry that skill into every piece you write for the rest of your career. If you learn to self-edit by running spell check and asking a friend if it “flows,” you’ll plateau fast.

The other advantage is speed. Professional editors are expensive and slow. Workshop feedback comes once a week. Eddy is available every time you finish a draft, which means you can iterate in hours instead of days.

Where It Falls Short (Honest Take)

Eddy can’t tell you if your idea is interesting. It can evaluate how clearly and effectively you’ve presented your idea, but it can’t judge whether the underlying insight is original or whether your personal experience is compelling. That still requires human readers.

It also can’t replace a developmental editor for longer, more complex pieces, a 5,000-word investigative feature with multiple sources needs a kind of narrative judgment that a seven-stage framework isn’t designed for.

And there’s a learning curve. The first time you get a Readiness Score of 3 out of 10, it stings. You have to develop the emotional muscle to treat feedback as information, not injury. That’s a skill in itself, and Eddy won’t hold your hand through it.

How to Get Started

If you want to try Eddy, here’s the simplest path:

Open a conversation with Claude using the Eddy the Editor system prompt. Type /help to see the welcome menu and available commands. Paste your draft and type /review for the full seven-stage treatment, or use a targeted command like /hook or /headline if you want to focus on one area.

If you’re confident in your draft and just want the output without the back-and-forth, add “silent” to any command — /review silent delivers all seven stages immediately without asking questions.

Start with your weakest draft. Seriously. The piece you’re embarrassed about is the one where you’ll learn the most, because there’s more for the framework to catch.

The Bottom Line

Eddy the Editor isn’t going to write your Substack post for you. It’s going to show you why your current draft isn’t ready and hand you the tools to make it ready yourself. For student writers trying to build an audience on Substack, that’s more valuable than any AI ghostwriter, because at the end of the process, you actually understand what you did and why it works.

That’s the thread that runs through everything Prof. Brown builds at Humanitarians AI: AI should make you more capable, not more dependent. Eddy is one tool in a growing ecosystem, but for anyone publishing on Substack, it might be the one that changes how you see your own writing.

Your writing doesn’t need a cheerleader. It needs a coach who will point at the weak spot and say, “Fix that, and you’ve got something.”

Eddy is that coach.
