I teach a course called Branding & AI at Northeastern University, College of Engineering in Boston. The whole premise is that engineers can learn to think like brand strategists, and that AI tools are part of how you get there. We use Claude, ChatGPT, Midjourney, n8n, V0. I’m not hiding it. It’s in the course title.

So when I recently drafted an email to a colleague and was told to take out the em dashes because they “look like AI wrote it,” I had a moment.

Not a big moment. A small one. The kind where you realize a cultural shift has happened while you weren’t paying attention, and now you’re on the wrong side of it.

Here’s what happened: the em dash, that long horizontal line that’s been part of English punctuation for over four centuries, has become the scarlet letter of AI-generated text. If your writing has em dashes in it, a growing number of people will assume a chatbot wrote it.

Reddit started it. The ChatGPT subreddit, specifically, where users began pointing out that ChatGPT seemed to love em dashes more than any human possibly could. “Nobody uses them in email or comments,” one user wrote. “Keyboards don’t even have a key for them. Is this the ultimate ChatGPT tell?”

And like all internet theories that are roughly 30% true, it spread everywhere.

NPR covered it. The Washington Post covered it. McSweeney’s published a satirical first-person essay from the em dash’s perspective that became their most-read piece of 2025. Writers on TikTok started advising people to strip em dashes from their work. Reddit users asked, in apparent seriousness, whether they should stop using em dashes entirely so people wouldn’t think they were bots.

Let me say that again: human writers started changing how they write so they wouldn’t be confused with machines.

If that’s not a branding problem, I don’t know what is.

———

A thousand years of punctuation, cancelled by Reddit

Before we get to keyboards and AI, the em dash deserves its history back. Because the people calling it a bot tell are unwittingly trashing one of the oldest and most expressive tools in the English language.

The word “dash” itself traces back to the Middle English “dashen,” meaning to strike violently. That energy was always the point. A dash wasn’t polite. It was a mark that interrupted, that broke a sentence open so something urgent could get through.

The conceptual ancestor of the modern dash dates back nearly a millennium. But the em dash as a specific typographic character emerged in the early 1600s, when printers began extending hyphens for emphasis because the limited character sets of early printing presses didn’t give them much to work with. Ben Jonson was among the first writers to use something resembling it in the late 1500s.

The name “em” comes from metal typesetting. An em was a unit of measurement equal to the full height of a piece of metal type, which also corresponded to the width of a capital letter M in that typeface. So the em dash was literally a dash the width of an M. The en dash, its shorter sibling, was the width of an N. These weren’t abstract ideas. They were physical objects. A typesetter pulled a metal slug from a drawer, set it into a composing stick, and pressed it into the page. One piece of metal. One motion.

By the 1700s, dashes were everywhere. Jonathan Swift mocked the trend in verse, lumping dashes in with other typographical affectations. Shakespeare’s printers used long dashes of various lengths, apparently cobbled together from three or four hyphens jammed side by side. A century later, the Romantic poets ran with them. Byron. Shelley. The dash became the sound of dramatic feeling on the page.

And then there was Emily Dickinson.

Dickinson didn’t just use em dashes. She built her entire poetic architecture out of them. One scholar observed that the “em” in “em dash” might as well stand for “Emily.” Her poems are so saturated with dashes that when her work was first published in 1890, the editors removed most of them, replacing some with commas because they didn’t know what to do with a poet who punctuated like that. They were wrong. The dashes weren’t errors. They were the breathing. Her lines gasp and pause and lean forward and stop short, and the dashes are doing all of it. “I’m Nobody! Who are you? / Are you—Nobody—too?” That rhythm doesn’t work with commas. It barely works with anything else.

Virginia Woolf used em dashes to let thoughts unspool across pages. Faulkner used them like someone leaving doors open in every room of a house. Kurt Vonnegut, who famously said never to use semicolons because “all they do is show you’ve been to college,” was pro-dash. Vladimir Nabokov used them to craft some of the most precise prose of the 20th century.

In the mid-1800s, the Linotype machine made it possible to produce em dashes with mechanical precision, and they became standard in publishing. Then the typewriter arrived and nearly killed them. Christopher Latham Sholes patented the first typewriter in the 1860s, and there was no room on the keyboard for an em dash. Just the hyphen. Writers adapted. Two hyphens side by side became the workaround, and that convention carried forward into the digital age, where word processors eventually started auto-converting the double hyphen into a proper em dash.

But the damage was done. The typewriter separated the em dash from everyday writers. It became a mark you’d see in published books and professional typesetting, not in letters or memos or anything typed quickly. It went from being, as one writer put it, “the everyman of punctuation” to something only editors and typesetters thought about.

Until AI brought it back. And then got blamed for it.

———

The keyboard problem nobody talks about

Here’s a detail that gets lost in the discourse: most people don’t use em dashes because they literally don’t know how to type them.

I had to ask Claude how to even make one. I co-teach a course called Branding & AI. I use AI tools every day. And until last week, I had no idea how to type an em dash. That should tell you everything about why most people don’t use them.

On a Mac, it’s Option + Shift + Hyphen. A three-key chord. On Windows, it’s Alt + 0151 on the number pad, which requires you to have a number pad and also to have memorized a four-digit code. On a phone, you long-press the hyphen and hope for the best.

The em dash was never designed for people who actually type for a living. The typewriter didn’t have a key for it. Modern keyboards don’t either. The typesetter’s one-motion metal slug became a three-key chord, and most people never learned it.

So when people say “nobody uses em dashes in casual writing,” they’re not wrong. But the reason isn’t that em dashes are unnatural or robotic. The reason is that keyboards made them inaccessible. The punctuation mark didn’t change. The input device did.

AI doesn’t have a keyboard problem. AI just thinks the em dash into existence. And because it learned to write by reading millions of books by the writers I just described, it uses them constantly.

The machines learned the habit from Dickinson and Woolf and Nabokov. And now we’re punishing ourselves for it.

———

This is a branding lesson

I keep coming back to this because it’s exactly the kind of problem I teach my students to see.

In branding, there’s a concept called “signal vs. substance.” A signal is a surface-level indicator that people use as a shortcut to make a judgment. Substance is the actual quality underneath. Good branding aligns the two. Bad branding lets them drift apart.

The em dash panic is a case study in signals overtaking substance.

People have decided that the em dash is a signal of AI-generated text. So now, when they encounter an em dash, they stop evaluating the substance of the writing and jump straight to a verdict: bot. It doesn’t matter if the sentence is well-constructed, the argument is original, or the voice is distinctive. The em dash triggered the pattern match, and the pattern match wins.

This is the same mistake brands make when they focus on what something looks like instead of what it does. It’s the same mistake hiring managers make when they screen resumes for formatting instead of reading them. It’s the same mistake my students are tempted to make when they put more energy into choosing a color palette than defining their audience.

Surface-level pattern matching kills nuance. That’s true in branding. It’s true in hiring. And it’s true in how we’re learning to read in the age of AI.

———

The real tells are harder to spot

Here’s the thing: if you actually want to know whether something was written by AI, the em dash is a terrible indicator.

One researcher tested six different AI systems with the same prompt. ChatGPT, Copilot, and DeepSeek used em dashes heavily. Claude used two. Gemini and Meta AI used none. So it’s not even an “AI” tell. It’s a ChatGPT tell that got generalized to everything.

The real indicators of AI-generated text are subtler and require you to actually read the work. Lack of typos. Overuse of words like “delve,” “meticulous,” and “commendable.” Perfectly balanced paragraph lengths. An absence of genuine surprise. Sentences that are technically correct but emotionally flat.

In other words, the tells aren’t typographic. They’re qualitative. They require judgment, not a find-and-replace search for long dashes.

But judgment is slow, and pattern matching is fast. So here we are.

———

What I tell my students

In my class, we use AI tools openly. We also talk constantly about voice, authenticity, and what it means to build a brand that’s yours. Those two things aren’t in conflict. You can use a power tool and still build something with your own hands.

When a student turns in work that reads like default ChatGPT output, I can tell. Not because of the em dashes. Because the thinking is generic. The examples are vague. The voice sounds like everyone and no one. That’s the real tell, and it has nothing to do with punctuation.

The best student work I’ve seen this semester was built with AI tools from start to finish, and it’s unmistakably theirs. The strategy is specific. The stories have real numbers and real moments. The brand voice is consistent across every touchpoint. One student built her entire final presentation as a custom HTML slide deck, hand-coded, because the medium was the message. Another student’s Substack post opened with a line that could only have come from someone who’d actually lived through the problem he was describing.

That’s what I’m grading. Not whether there’s a long dash on page three.

———

Free the em dash

The Ringer published a piece last year arguing that it would be a tragedy if writers stopped using em dashes out of fear of sounding like AI, because em dashes are one of the best tools writers have for not sounding robotic in the first place.

I agree. The em dash is expressive. It creates pace. It lets you interrupt yourself mid-thought, which is one of the most human things writing can do. It’s the punctuation equivalent of leaning forward in your chair and saying, “wait, here’s the real thing.” Dickinson knew that. Woolf knew that. Every typesetter who pulled that metal slug from the drawer knew that.

If we surrender it to the machines, we don’t just lose a punctuation mark. We lose a piece of how humans have sounded on the page since Shakespeare's printers were jamming hyphens together and Ben Jonson was fighting over punctuation.

So I’m going to keep using them. I absolutely love them, and I don’t want to take them out of my text. Not for Reddit. Not for the algorithm. Not for anyone who thinks a long dash means a machine wrote my sentences.

Besides, I just learned the keyboard shortcut. I had to ask an AI how to type the punctuation mark that supposedly proves AI wrote it. If that’s not a perfect circle, I don’t know what is.
