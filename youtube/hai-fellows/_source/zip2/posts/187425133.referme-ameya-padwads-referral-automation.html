Ameya is building ReferMe because she’s living the problem. Right now, with 60-something days left on her visa, she spends hours every night checking if any of her 300+ LinkedIn connections work at companies that might sponsor her. She opens spreadsheets. She cross-references names. She crafts personalized messages. By 2 AM, she’s sent maybe three outreach messages and she’s exhausted.

The “80 Days to Stay” project already identified 30,000 funded companies capable of visa sponsorship. That’s the discovery part. But discovery doesn’t equal access. Ameya needs to know which of those 30,000 companies employ someone she actually knows, whether they’re hiring, and how to ask for a referral without looking desperate or spammy.

What ReferMe Actually Does

The system takes five data sources and creates a knowledge graph that doesn’t exist anywhere else:

  1. Her LinkedIn connection export (people she knows)

  2. The “80 Days to Stay” database (companies that sponsor)

  3. Real-time career page scraping (what jobs exist)

  4. Public H-1B records (proof they actually sponsor)

  5. Job posting analysis (what they’re looking for)

Then it generates contextually-aware outreach messages calibrated to relationship strength, the connection’s position, company stage, and cultural context.

PROF BEAR’S NOTE: Stop. This is the classic move where she describes the dream architecture instead of Minimum Viable Logic. She has FIVE data sources and “real-time” scraping and “contextually-aware” message generation and a “knowledge graph.” In Week 1, she’s parsing CSVs. In Week 4, she’s doing basic job scraping. When exactly does the knowledge graph get built? When does the cultural context calibration happen? She’s describing the final vision, not the 30-day build.

PROF BEAR’S NOTE: Also, notice what’s missing here: the actual decision the user faces. Is the decision “which connections to message”? Or “what to say to them”? Or “which jobs to apply to”? These are three different tools. Which one is she actually building?

How She Knows It’s Working

Ameya has thought hard about ground truth because she doesn’t want to build a bullshit generator. Here’s what she’ll measure:

  1. Company Career Website Discovery: Can she find the right career page? Target: 70% accuracy. She’ll manually verify a sample.

  2. Job Fetching: Are the scraped jobs actually real? Target: 80% accuracy. Manual verification again.

  3. Job Matching: Do users mark the flagged jobs as relevant? Target: 70% marked “relevant.” Users explicitly rate each match.

  4. Message Quality: Do messages get responses? Baseline cold message response rate is 5-10%. Target: 30% response rate. Users log sends and responses.

PROF BEAR’S NOTE: Her ground truth thinking is solid - she’s actually defining measurable outcomes and she understands the baseline. The 30% response rate target is aggressive (3x improvement over baseline), which makes me skeptical, but at least she’s being specific about what success looks like.

PROF BEAR’S NOTE: But here’s the problem: these are all lagging indicators. She won’t know message quality until Week 6 or 7 when people have actually sent messages and waited for responses. What’s her leading indicator that tells her in Week 2 whether she’s on track? How does she know by Week 3 if she should keep building or pivot?

The Circuit Breaker

The critical failure point is false company identification. LinkedIn exports contain free-text: “Google,” “Google LLC,” “Alphabet,” “Google (via contractor),” “Freelance at Google.” If she misidentifies “Morgan Stanley” as “Morgan” and sends a referral request to the wrong company, she’s just burned a professional bridge and embarrassed the user.

PROF BEAR’S NOTE: Excellent. This is the kind of specific failure mode thinking I want to see. She’s identified the exact point where things go catastrophically wrong, not just “poorly.” But her circuit breaker is mostly manual verification - “low confidence matches go to a review queue.” That’s fine for MVP, but it means ReferMe doesn’t actually save time if users are manually verifying 20-30% of company matches. Is the tool still valuable if it automates 70% and requires manual work on 30%?

Her circuit breaker has two layers:

  1. Confidence scoring: Exact name matches + domain verification. Low confidence (<80%) goes to a manual review queue.

  2. Pre-send sanity check: Before any message sends, prompt: “You’re asking [Name] for a referral at [Company]. According to LinkedIn, they work at [Company Text]. Is this correct?”

PROF BEAR’S NOTE: The pre-send sanity check is smart defensive design. But I notice she’s solving for “wrong company” and not for other failure modes. What happens when the career page scraper breaks because a company changed their website? What happens when the H-1B database is out of date? What happens when the LLM generates a tone-deaf message? She’s built one circuit breaker, but her system has five data dependencies.

The 4-Week Build

Week 1: Import LinkedIn CSVs and store them. Success: Import 3 real LinkedIn exports accurately.

Week 2: Map connection companies to websites and verify career pages exist. Success: Find career pages for ≥60% of companies.

Week 3-4: Scrape career pages and match jobs to user profile. Success: Identify 5 real, relevant jobs for test user.

Week 5: Generate referral messages and demonstrate end-to-end. Success: One Fellow uses the tool and sends one real referral request.

Finding jobs is interesting but not sufficient - the core value proposition is the referral request, right? So why isn’t that Week 3-4?
