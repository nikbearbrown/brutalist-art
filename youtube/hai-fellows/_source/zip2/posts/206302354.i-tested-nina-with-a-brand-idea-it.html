Who Is Nina?

Nina is an AI brand identity system built by Humanitarians AI. It’s named after Nina Harris, a Brand Director and Creative Director with over 40+ years of experience at Charles Schwab, Publicis, McCann-Erickson, and Saatchi & Saatchi. She co-teaches Branding and AI at Northeastern with Prof. Nik Bear Brown. The tool was built live in class. 26 commands. Six phases. A complete pipeline from intake to style guide.

I wanted to test it. So I invented a brand from scratch and walked in like a founder who thinks they know what they have.

Nina did not agree.

The First Thing That Threw Me Off

I opened the conversation, and Nina spoke first before I typed a word.

“40+ years doing this. Schwab, Publicis, McCann, Saatchi & Saatchi. I don’t flatter, I don’t say ‘great question,’ and I won’t hand you visual direction before the strategic foundation is solid.”

That caught me off guard. Every other AI tool waits for you to speak. Nina introduced herself, set expectations, and made it clear this was going to be a structured conversation, not a chatbot interaction. It felt like sitting across from a real strategist who had no interest in making me comfortable before making me think.

Eight Questions. One at a Time.

I ran the intake command. Nina asked me eight questions, one at a time, and waited for my answer before moving on.

“Brand name. What is it?”

Kaya.

“One plain sentence: what does Kaya do? No positioning language, no adjectives doing the heavy lifting. Just what it actually does, the way you’d explain it to a stranger at a dinner party.”

I said: a fashion brand that makes luxury outfits with abstract prints for both men and women.

“Who is it for? I want a specific person, not a demographic. Not ‘millennials who care about fashion.’ Picture one real person who’d wear this.”

And here’s where I slipped. I said: someone who can afford luxury clothes, because it’s expensive and unique.

Nina didn’t let that slide.

“That’s a Price Filter, Not a Person”

After all eight questions, Nina gave me a summary. And then she flagged the problem I hadn’t seen.

She said: “Luxury” and “for someone who can afford it” describe a price point, not a person. The real audience insight you’ve actually given me is someone who dresses for private confidence first and public recognition second. Someone who’s tired of clothes with no depth to them.

She was right. I had described a price tag and called it an audience. That’s the kind of mistake that makes it into pitch decks all the time. “Our target audience is high-income individuals who value quality.” That tells you nothing about why someone would choose your brand over any other luxury option.

Nina caught it in the intake. Not in a vague “you might want to refine your audience” way. She named the actual gap: you gave me a bank balance, not a belief.

The Opportunity I Buried

Then came the part that really got me.

I had mentioned, almost in passing, that Kaya lets customers bring in their own artwork or abstract designs and have them turned into garments. I treated it as a feature. Nina saw it as the entire proposition.

“The commission model. Almost no one in the luxury print space lets a customer hand over a painting or an abstract idea and get it back as a garment. That’s not a feature. That could be the entire proposition, if we’re brave enough to lead with it instead of burying it under ‘luxury outfits.’”

I had the most interesting thing about this brand sitting right there, and I was leading with generic luxury positioning instead. Nina pulled it out, held it up, and essentially said: this is the thing. Stop hiding it.

Creator With an Outlaw Edge

Nina assigned the archetype: Creator primary, Outlaw secondary.

The reasoning made sense. Kaya isn’t just selling clothes. It’s selling origination. The customer doesn’t browse a collection. They bring something that matters to them and get it back as something they can wear. That’s a Creator truth. The Outlaw edge comes from the refusal to operate the way luxury fashion normally does. No seasonal collections. No trend chasing. No safe choices.

But the part that stuck with me was the shadow risk. Nina flagged that the Creator’s shadow is the Perfectionist, the brand that gets so obsessed with craft and originality that it becomes inaccessible. And the Outlaw’s shadow is the brand that mistakes being different for being difficult.

I’ve seen both of those failures in real brands. Naming them upfront changes how you make decisions later.

What I Learned About Brand Strategy by Watching AI Do It

Here’s what surprised me. I’ve worked in marketing for seven years. I’ve built brand strategies for clients. I know what a positioning statement is and how to write a creative brief.

Nina still caught things I missed. Not because the AI is smarter than me. Because the structure forced a sequence I would have skipped.

Left to my own process, I would have jumped to visuals. Abstract prints, bold colors, editorial photography. I would have built a mood board before I figured out who Kaya is actually for. Nina wouldn’t let me. Discovery before strategy. Strategy before identity. Identity before build. That’s the rule, and the system enforces it.

The tool doesn’t generate brand identities for you. It makes you build one properly. That’s the difference.
