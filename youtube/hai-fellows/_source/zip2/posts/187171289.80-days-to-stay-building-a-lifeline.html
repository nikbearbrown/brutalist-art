Part I: The Weekend Everything Changed

On a Friday afternoon, an engineer at a major tech company receives confirmation: she’ll be hiring for her group. Budget approved. Headcount secured. She goes home planning interview questions.

Monday morning, she’s handed two weeks notice.

This isn’t a story about performance. It’s not about merit. It’s about AI-driven restructuring, cost optimization, and the brutally efficient math of quarterly earnings reports. The engineer is one of thousands cut in 2024-2025 layoffs across Microsoft, Meta, Google, Amazon—companies with dedicated immigration teams, companies that understand H-1B sponsorship inside and out.

But here’s where the timeline becomes a countdown: she’s on H-1B status. The two-week notice period plus the 60-day grace period before her visa expires adds up to roughly 80 days. Not 80 days to consider options. Not 80 days to upskill or network casually. 80 days to find another company willing to sponsor her transfer, or book a flight home.

For Professor Nik Bear Brown at Northeastern University, watching this happen to someone he knows well, “I’m sad” felt inadequate. He doesn’t do grief. He does systems. He builds things. So he asked himself: what can I build in 80 days that might actually help?

The answer became 80 Days to Stay.

Part II: The Invisible Market

Here’s the structural problem, expressed in numbers:

On one side of the equation:

  • Thousands of H-1B workers laid off in 2024-2025 tech restructuring

  • 60 days to find new sponsorship before deportation

  • Must find jobs at similar salary levels (USCIS requirement)

  • Competing against each other for positions at the ~100 large companies everyone knows will sponsor

On the other side:

  • 568,000 companies filed SEC Form D (private offering) in the last decade

  • Filtered for relevance: ~50,000 funded startups in biotech, healthcare tech, AI, data science

  • Typical funding rounds: $5M to $50M

  • Funding purpose: to hire and scale

  • Percentage that regularly sponsor international workers: roughly 10-25%

  • Percentage that could sponsor but think it’s impossible: 75-90%

The market failure isn’t about scarcity. There are jobs. There is talent. There is money. The failure is information asymmetry.

Startups think: “Visa sponsorship is expensive/complicated/risky. We can’t do it.”

The reality: H-1B transfer costs ~$5,000-$10,000 in legal fees. For a company that just raised $15 million, this is a rounding error. For a company hiring a senior engineer at $120,000/year, it’s 4-8% of annual compensation. Less than the recruiter’s placement fee.

And for the first 1-3 years? Many international workers are on OPT (Optional Practical Training), which requires zero employer sponsorship. They’re already work-authorized. They’re FICA-exempt, meaning the employer saves ~7.65% in payroll taxes compared to hiring a US citizen. At an $80,000 salary, that’s $6,120/year saved.

But hiring managers at Series A startups don’t know this. So when they see “requires visa sponsorship” on an application, they delete it.

Part III: What Gets Built in 80 Days

The 80 Days to Stay project is, at its technical core, a data integration pipeline. But what it reveals is more valuable than the code.

Data Source 1: SEC Form D Filings

Every private company in America that raises money from investors must file Form D with the Securities and Exchange Commission. It’s public. It includes: company name, address, industry, funding amount, filing date, sometimes investor names.

Brown and his team at Humanitarians AI—a 501(c)3 nonprofit he founded—downloaded every Form D they could access. Over 500,000 companies. Then filtered:

  • Remove companies outside the US

  • Remove industries unlikely to hire tech talent (restaurants, real estate, oil and gas)

  • Remove companies with under $1M in funding (too small to reliably hire)

  • Focus on: biotech, pharmaceuticals, healthcare technology, AI/ML, data science, financial tech

Result: ~50,000-60,000 companies with money, hiring intent, and relevance.

Data Source 2: Department of Labor LCA Database

Every company that wants to sponsor an H-1B, H-1B1, or E-3 visa must file a Labor Condition Application (LCA) with the Department of Labor. This data is public and goes back to 1970.

Each LCA filing includes:

  • Company name

  • Job title being sponsored

  • Location

  • Filing date

  • Sometimes salary range

The team downloaded decades of this data—millions of records—and mapped it to the SEC companies.

Now, for each startup, they know:

  • Total visa applications filed (ever)

  • Applications filed in last 3 years

  • Job titles historically sponsored

  • Trend (increasing, stable, or decreasing sponsorship)

Data Source 3: The Actual Websites

They wrote crawlers to infer domain names from company names (BioStartup Inc → biostartup.com), then verified them with HTTP requests. Success rate: 62%. That’s 25,748 working websites out of 41,299 targets.

For each verified domain:

  • Crawl up to 50 pages (prevents infinite loops)

  • Save as HTML, convert to plain text

  • Identify careers pages, job listings, team pages

  • Detect which Applicant Tracking System (ATS) they use, if any

Then feed all this text—along with SEC metadata—to a large language model with a simple instruction: Analyze this company as if you were evaluating them for an international student job search. Do they hire technical roles? Do they mention sponsorship? What signals do you see?

What emerges:

A database where you can answer questions like:

  • Which biotech companies in Boston have raised $10M+ and sponsored at least 3 H-1Bs in the last 3 years?

  • Which Series A AI startups have never sponsored but have $20M+ in funding?

  • Which companies use Greenhouse vs. Workday vs. no ATS at all?

Traditional job boards can’t answer these questions. LinkedIn can’t answer these questions. Their algorithms optimize for engagement (clicks, applications, time-on-site), not for matching international workers to employers who actually understand sponsorship.

Part IV: What the Data Reveals

Here’s what 50,000 companies look like when you sort them by historical visa sponsorship:

~12,000 companies (24%): 1+ LCA filing in last 3 years These are the “proven sponsors.” They know the drill. They have immigration counsel or HR staff who handle it. Visa sponsorship isn’t scary—it’s Tuesday.

~8,000 companies (16%): Historical LCA filings, but none recent These are the “former sponsors.” They did it before. Maybe the team changed. Maybe they’ve been in heads-down product mode. But institutional knowledge exists.

~30,000 companies (60%): Zero LCA history, ever These are the “never sponsors.” They have funding. They’re hiring. They could afford the $5K-$10K in fees. They just don’t know it’s possible.

Now cross-reference with funding:

  • Of the 12,000 proven sponsors, ~4,000 raised money in 2024-2025

  • Of the 30,000 never-sponsors, ~18,000 raised money in 2024-2025

There are 4.5x more funded companies that have never sponsored than companies that regularly do.

This is the invisible market.

Part V: The Excel File Problem

Currently, 80 Days to Stay is a CSV file. You download it. You open it in Excel or Google Sheets. You manually filter by state, by industry, by funding amount. You copy company names into Google. You find their websites yourself. You click through to careers pages.

It works. It’s better than nothing. Students are using it right now.

But it’s also 2026, and this should be a searchable web application.

What it needs to become:

A platform where you can:

  • Search by location: “Boston biotech companies”

  • Filter by sponsorship history: “Show me companies that filed 3+ LCAs in last 3 years”

  • Sort by funding recency: “Raised money in last 12 months”

  • Filter by job type: “Currently hiring ‘machine learning engineer’ or similar”

  • See ATS detection: “Uses Greenhouse” vs. “No ATS detected—email founder directly”

  • Export custom lists: “Here are my top 50 targets, ranked by sponsorship propensity score”

  • Track applications: “Mark as applied, phone screen scheduled, rejected”

Technical requirements:

  • Frontend: React or similar, fast filtering/search

  • Backend: PostgreSQL database with the merged SEC + LCA + website data

  • API: RESTful endpoints for search, filter, company detail pages

  • Hosting: Vercel (frontend) + Railway/Supabase (backend) on free/cheap tiers

  • Authentication: Optional—allow anonymous search, require login for tracking

  • Mobile-responsive: Many users will search on phones between interviews

Current state of development: The data exists. It’s structured. It’s in JSON and CSV formats on GitHub. What’s missing is the interface layer—the thing that turns “50,000 rows in a spreadsheet” into “here are the 15 companies you should apply to this week, ranked by match score.”

Part VI: How to Extend This Project

The team working on 80 Days to Stay is almost entirely Humanitarians AI volunteers—international students themselves, many actively job hunting. This creates unusual motivation: you’re not building a tool for hypothetical users. You’re building the tool you need right now.

If you want to contribute, here’s what needs to be done:

1. Build the Search Platform (CRITICAL NEED)

  • Skills: React, Node.js/Python, PostgreSQL, API design

  • Time commitment: 10-15 hours/week for 6-8 weeks

  • Impact: Transforms CSV → usable product

The GitHub repo has the data pipeline scripts. What’s needed: someone to architect and build the web interface. This doesn’t need to be beautiful—it needs to be functional. Think “Craigslist for visa-sponsoring companies.” Ugly but useful beats pretty but delayed.

2. Improve Domain Inference

  • Skills: Python, web scraping, regex/NLP

  • Current success rate: 62%

  • Goal: 80%+

Many company names don’t directly map to domains. “Crossbow Therapeutics” → crossbowtherapeutics.com works. But “Corrine NX” might be corrinehealthcare.com or corrinx.com or something else entirely. Better heuristics, Google Search API integration, or even manual validation by volunteers could close this gap.

3. Enhance LLM Analysis

  • Skills: Prompt engineering, Python, API integration

  • Current state: Basic analysis (”Does this look like a tech company?”)

  • Goal: Structured extraction

The LLM currently analyzes website text freeform. It should extract:

  • Current job openings (title, date posted, URL)

  • Tech stack mentioned (languages, frameworks, tools)

  • Team size indicators

  • Visa sponsorship language (if mentioned explicitly)

  • Recent news/funding announcements

This requires better prompting and structured output parsing.

4. Add Real-Time Job Scraping

  • Skills: Web scraping, Selenium/Playwright, async Python

  • Goal: Daily updates of job postings

Right now, job listings are captured once during initial website scraping. But companies post new jobs constantly. A daily or weekly re-scraper that checks careers pages for changes would keep the database current.

5. Build Sponsorship Propensity ML Model

  • Skills: Python, scikit-learn, pandas

  • Current state: Rule-based scoring

  • Goal: Predictive model

Use the historical LCA data to train a model: given a company’s funding amount, industry, location, team size, and website content, predict the probability they’ll sponsor in the next 12 months. Features might include:

  • Text similarity between their job postings and historical LCA job titles

  • Funding round recency

  • Presence of “diversity” or “international” language on website

  • Number of .edu email domains in their team (indicates they hire new grads)

6. Create Educational Content

  • Skills: Writing, video editing, documentation

  • Goal: Help users understand the data

Many international students don’t know what an LCA is, or why “filed 5 LCAs in 2024” is a strong signal. Create:

  • Video walkthroughs of how to use the tool

  • Blog posts explaining sponsorship propensity scores

  • Case studies: “How I used 80 Days to Stay to find my job”

7. Manual Data Validation

  • Skills: Internet research, attention to detail

  • Time: 2-5 hours/week

  • Impact: Improves data quality

The automated domain inference and LCA matching isn’t perfect. Sometimes “BioStartup Inc” maps to the wrong company. Sometimes the LCA data has typos or refers to a subsidiary. Manual spot-checking and corrections improve accuracy.

Part VII: The Bigger Stakes

The 80 Days to Stay project is, nominally, about helping international workers avoid deportation during layoffs. But it’s also a case study in what public data can reveal when properly integrated.

The SEC publishes Form D filings to ensure transparency in private capital markets. The Department of Labor publishes LCA data to allow public scrutiny of wage practices. Neither agency designed their data to be cross-referenced. Neither imagined someone would download half a million records, map them together, scrape 25,000 websites, and build a job-search tool.

But the data was public. The information was always there. It just needed someone to connect it.

What this reveals about labor markets:

There isn’t a talent shortage. There’s a visibility shortage. Companies with $15M in the bank are rejecting candidates because they don’t understand OPT. Candidates with MS degrees from top US universities are applying to 200 companies and getting ghosted because they’re targeting the wrong 200.

The friction isn’t supply and demand. It’s discovery.

What this reveals about immigration policy:

The H-1B system is often framed as “big tech importing cheap labor.” But the data shows most H-1B sponsorship is concentrated in ~500-1,000 large companies that understand the system. Meanwhile, 30,000+ funded startups—companies that desperately need talent—aren’t participating because they think it’s too hard.

The current system creates a knowledge moat that benefits incumbents. If you’re Google, you have an immigration team. If you’re a 40-person biotech startup, you don’t even know LCAs exist.

What this reveals about education’s role:

Every Humanitarians AI volunteer is currently job searching. They’re international students, recent grads, people on OPT facing the end of their work authorization. They’re building this tool because they need it to exist.

This is the “learn AI by doing AI” philosophy in action: you don’t learn data engineering by reading documentation. You learn it by downloading 500,000 SEC filings at 2am and realizing your JSON parser is choking on malformed XML.

The project is pedagogy. But it’s also survival.

Part VIII: The 80-Day Constraint

Why 80 days? Partly the H-1B timeline (60 days + 2 weeks notice). Partly an homage to Jules Verne’s Around the World in 80 Days—a race against time, a wager on the possible.

But also: artificial constraints force prioritization.

If you have unlimited time to build the perfect platform, you’ll spend six months on UI/UX debates. If you have 80 days, you ship the CSV file on day 5. You ship the domain inference script on day 8. You ship the LCA mapping on day 14. Not because it’s perfect. Because someone needs it today.

As of this writing, the project is past day 80. The friend who got laid off on that Monday? She found a new sponsor. But others haven’t. The CSV file is in use. The GitHub repo has 300+ stars. Students are forking it, extending it, building regional variants.

The web platform still doesn’t exist. That’s the critical gap. Because a CSV file helps 100 people. A searchable web application helps 100,000.

If you’re reading this and you know React, or PostgreSQL, or web scraping, or data visualization:

The GitHub repo is here: https://github.com/nikbearbrown/80-Days-to-Stay
The data is already structured.
The need is immediate.
The impact is measurable: every successful job placement is someone who gets to stay.

This isn’t charity work. This is infrastructure. You’re building the roads that connect talent to opportunity when the existing roads—LinkedIn, Indeed, corporate career pages—have tollbooths that say “US citizens preferred.”

You have 80 days. Or 80 hours. Or 80 commits.

The constraint isn’t time. It’s deciding whether the problem matters enough to do something about it.

Because here’s what the data shows: the invisible market is real. The companies exist. The jobs exist. The funding exists. What’s missing is the interface—the search tool, the sorting algorithm, the thing that says, “Here. Apply here. They’ve sponsored before. They’ll understand.”

Build that, and you’re not just helping one person avoid deportation.

You’re fixing a market.
