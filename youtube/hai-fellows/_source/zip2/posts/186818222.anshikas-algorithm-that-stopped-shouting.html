You’ve sent 200 applications. Maybe 300. The job boards blur together—the same keywords, the same requirements, the same automated rejections or, worse, the hollow silence. You refresh your email. Nothing. You wonder if anyone is even reading these. You wonder if you exist.

Anshika Khandelwal stopped wondering. She started building.

By 9 AM every morning, before most professionals have finished their first coffee, her creation wakes up. It visits TechCrunch. It visits VentureBeat. It reads every new article published in the last 24 hours. It filters for investment keywords. It classifies industries. It structures chaos into signal. It sends an email digest of every significant AI funding announcement you need to know about—automatically, reliably, without fail.

The tool saves more than ten hours per week. It never misses a story. And it got her featured by Zyte, a major web data platform, for technical excellence.

She didn’t get there by submitting resumes.

The Problem Lives in the Silence

The contemporary job market operates like a one-way mirror. You shout your credentials through the glass. Occasionally, something happens on the other side—a form letter, a ghosting, a “we’ve decided to move forward with other candidates.” More often: nothing. The void doesn’t answer. The void doesn’t even acknowledge you screamed.

Khandelwal, a data and AI analyst working with Humanitarians AI—a Boston-based nonprofit building ethical, open-source AI solutions for public good—recognized the asymmetry. Applying wasn’t differentiating her. Applications measure persistence, not capability. So she did what data analysts do when faced with an intractable problem: she reframed it.

The question shifted from “How do I get someone to notice my resume?” to “What can I build that demonstrates what I actually know how to do?”

The Architecture of Attention

She built a component for Mycroft, a Funding Intelligence Agent. Not a toy project. Not a portfolio piece that works once for a screenshot. A production system that runs daily, handles complex web content, processes unstructured data, and delivers actionable intelligence.

The technical architecture tells its own story of sophistication:

Extraction layer: Zyte API integration through n8n workflows, handling dynamic web content from multiple sources. Not simple scraping—robust, scalable extraction that doesn’t break when websites change their layouts.

Classification layer: AI-driven analysis that doesn’t just collect articles but understands them. Investment-related keywords trigger the filter. Industry classifiers sort the signal. The system builds knowledge, not just a database.

Intelligence layer: Structured, usable insights. Clean downstream data. Format designed for actual business and investment research, not academic demonstration.

The whole system orchestrates through n8n, Python, and AI models. It runs automatically. Every single day. Without supervision.

When Khandelwal reached out to Luke Bassett at Zyte early in the development process with a straightforward question—”How can I integrate the Zyte API effectively into n8n workflows?”—that conversation shaped the entire design. She built for reliability from day one. She built for scale. She built for the real world.

The Feature That Changed Everything

Zyte noticed. Not because she asked them to. Not because she submitted an application to be featured. Because the work itself demanded attention.

They published a feature highlighting how her Funding Intelligence Agent combined automated web data extraction with AI-driven analysis. The writeup emphasized the production-ready nature of the system—this wasn’t a demo, it was a tool colleagues actually used.

The recognition validated a principle: building in public, asking questions, and sharing your work creates opportunities that applications never will.

Think about the mathematics of visibility. When you apply for a job, you compete in a pool where your resume is one of hundreds, maybe thousands. The selection mechanism optimizes for keywords and credential matching. Your actual capabilities—your ability to architect systems, solve novel problems, integrate complex APIs, design workflows that run reliably at scale—compress into bullet points that may never be read by a human.

When you build and share, you compete in a different pool. Much smaller. The selection mechanism rewards demonstration of capability. The proof isn’t your resume. The proof is the thing itself, working, documented, available for inspection.

The Fellowship Model Hidden in Plain Sight

Humanitarians AI runs a Fellows Program. Not an internship. Not an employment track disguised as volunteerism. An actual fellowship model where participants work on real projects with real-world applications under expert guidance.

Khandelwal worked under the direction of Nik Bear Brown, an Associate Teaching Professor at Northeastern University who founded the nonprofit specifically to develop ethical AI solutions for education, healthcare, nonprofits, and the arts. The guidance wasn’t theoretical. It was architectural. It was about building systems that actually work.

The fellowship structure creates something traditional employment often fails to provide: the space to build substantial projects that demonstrate sophisticated technical capability. Not coding tests. Not take-home assignments designed to be completed in a weekend. Systems that take weeks to architect properly. Tools that require understanding the full stack—from data extraction to AI classification to reliable automation to user experience.

For international students like many of Humanitarians AI’s fellows, the stakes reach beyond résumé building. OPT visa status requires practical training directly related to their field. The projects need to be substantive, documented, demonstrable. The work needs to matter.

Mycroft matters. It saves colleagues ten-plus hours per week. It delivers intelligence they’d otherwise miss. It runs in production, not in a GitHub repository collecting dust.

The Automation That Runs While You Sleep

Every night, after the tech news sites have published their final stories, after the editors have posted their last updates, the system activates. By morning, the digest arrives.

You don’t scan TechCrunch manually anymore. You don’t maintain a spreadsheet of funding announcements. You don’t spend two hours every morning trying to catch up on what you missed. The algorithm does it. Perfectly. Consistently. While you sleep.

This is the actual value proposition of intelligent automation—not replacing humans, but handling the tedious, time-consuming pattern recognition that computers excel at so humans can focus on analysis, strategy, decision-making. The parts that actually require human judgment.

Khandelwal documented everything. The YouTube walkthrough shows exactly how the workflow operates. The shared code means anyone can build their own version. This isn’t proprietary magic. It’s replicable expertise.

What Changed the Equation

The Zyte feature didn’t happen because Khandelwal was lucky. It happened because she made contact—reached out with a specific technical question, engaged with the tools she was using, built something worth noticing, then shared it publicly where people could find it.

Each step matters. The technical question demonstrated she was working at a level worth engaging with. The quality of the build justified the eventual feature. The public sharing created the discovery mechanism. None of these steps alone produces the outcome. Together, they create a path that bypasses the traditional application funnel entirely.

This path isn’t available to everyone equally. It requires technical skills to build something substantial. It requires time—time that many job seekers, especially those facing visa deadlines or financial pressure, may not feel they have. It requires access to mentorship and guidance, which Khandelwal found through Humanitarians AI but which remains scarce for many.

Yet the principle generalizes beyond this specific case: demonstrable capability outperforms claimed capability. The thing you built beats the thing you say you can build. Every time.

The Model That Scales

Humanitarians AI’s fellowship model creates a structure where this approach can scale. Multiple fellows working on different projects. Each project addressing real organizational needs—the funding intelligence agent that tracks startup activity, the educational AI tools, the automation systems that help nonprofits operate more efficiently.

The nonprofit provides the framework, the mentorship, the real-world requirements. The fellows provide the implementation, the innovation, the drive to build something that works. Both sides benefit. The organization gets production tools. The fellows get portfolio projects that demonstrate genuine capability.

This isn’t new conceptually—apprenticeship models have existed for centuries. But applying it systematically to AI and data engineering, with explicit focus on ethical applications and public good, creates something distinct from both traditional internships and typical employment.

The fellows aren’t doing busywork. They’re not fetching coffee or attending meetings or filling in spreadsheets. They’re architecting systems, integrating APIs, designing workflows, solving real problems. The kind of work that actually demonstrates whether you can do the job.

The Email That Arrives at 9 AM

While Khandelwal’s algorithm runs, while it visits news sites and filters articles and classifies industries and structures data, thousands of other job seekers are refreshing their email, hoping for responses that won’t come. They’re optimizing resumes, tweaking cover letters, researching companies, following up on applications sent weeks ago.

All of that matters. All of that may be necessary. But none of it differentiates like a production system running in someone else’s organization, solving their actual problems, saving them measurable hours every week.

The morning digest that arrives at 9 AM represents something more than automation. It represents proof. Proof that the person who built it understands web data extraction, API integration, workflow orchestration, AI classification, reliable automation, and user experience. Proof that they can take an ambiguous problem—”help colleagues stay current on AI funding trends”—and convert it into a working solution.

Proof, ultimately, that they can do the work.

That proof opens doors that no resume can.

The Question You Should Ask

If you’re reading this while job searching, the question isn’t “How do I build something like Mycroft?” The question is: “What real problem can I solve for someone, and how can I build the solution in a way that demonstrates my actual capabilities?”

The answer will be different for everyone. Different skills, different problems, different contexts. But the principle remains: stop shouting into the void. Start building something the void can’t ignore.

Khandelwal’s algorithm runs every morning at 9 AM. Automatically. Reliably. Demonstrably. That’s not a resume. That’s a statement.
