You’re in your second year of graduate school, learning about neural networks and gradient descent, filling notebooks with equations that describe how machines learn. Your professor assigns another problem set. You dutifully implement backpropagation on a toy dataset. You get an A. And somewhere in the back of your mind, a question gnaws: When do I build something that matters?

The statistics suggest you’re not alone in asking. As of 2024, 58% of students report lacking sufficient AI knowledge for the workplace. Another 48% don’t feel ready for AI-enabled professional settings. Meanwhile, in just the first ten months of 2023, AI-related job postings increased twenty-fold. The gap between what universities teach and what the world demands has never been wider—or more consequential.

For most students, the answer comes after graduation—if it comes at all. But at Northeastern University, a professor named Nik Bear Brown looked at this chasm between classroom theory and industry reality and decided to do something radical: eliminate it entirely.

Not by replacing education with experience. By fusing them.

School AND Doing

“In my 2.5 years at Northeastern, I have not come across another organization quite like AI Skunkworks,” says Abhishek Maheshwarappa, former president of the program. “It is uniquely dedicated to skill development. It offers students not just peer connections, but also industry exposure.”

The distinction matters. AI Skunkworks doesn’t ask students to choose between mastering theory and building real systems. It demands both, simultaneously. You still take your courses, still fill those notebooks with equations, still grapple with the mathematical foundations of machine learning. But alongside that formal education, you’re also shipping code, managing technical debt, collaborating with researchers at Harvard Medical School, and publishing tools that people across the world actually use.

This isn’t extracurricular in the traditional sense—a resume line you add after your “real” work is done. This is parallel education, running on a separate track with different metrics of success. Your transcript measures what you’ve learned. Your portfolio measures what you’ve built. The modern economy increasingly cares more about the latter.

The Growth Trajectory

The numbers tell their own story. When AI Skunkworks began in 2019, it started with roughly 300 students. By the time Abhishek stepped into leadership, that number had grown to over 940. This isn’t the steady, predictable growth of a well-funded program expanding according to plan. This is viral adoption—students telling other students that something different is happening here, that there’s a way to build real capabilities before graduation forces you into the job market.

The systemic disconnect runs deeper than simple curriculum lag. Modern AI education focuses on sanitized problem sets that lack the ambiguity and technical debt inherent in real-world systems. Students achieve high marks for implementing algorithms that exist in isolation from the broader data ecosystem. This academic insulation produces a particular kind of graduate: impressive transcript, empty portfolio.

The numbers tell a stark story. While the vast majority of knowledge workers now use AI tools daily, two-thirds of business leaders remain hesitant to hire individuals who lack deep, demonstrable proficiency in building and managing AI systems. Only a negligible percentage of employers believe higher education currently provides adequate preparation for an AI-driven future. The traditional model—study theory now, apply it later—has become a luxury the market no longer accommodates.

AI Skunkworks materialized in 2019 from a simple, almost defiant premise: students learn AI by doing AI, not by solving problems that disappear the moment the semester ends. Brown—who holds a PhD in Computer Science from UCLA and completed postdoctoral work in Computational Neurology at Harvard Medical School—had watched too many brilliant students graduate with impressive transcripts and nothing to show for it but good grades.

The Research Group Architecture

You arrive at AI Skunkworks not knowing what you don’t know. Maybe you’re a computer science student who’s implemented algorithms but never shipped code that people actually use. Maybe you’re from business, or arts, or life sciences, wondering if there’s space for you in the AI revolution. Either way, you’re channeled into one of several research groups, each focused on a specific domain.

Causal Inference: Students explore the thorny question of correlation versus causation, building tools to distinguish true causal relationships from statistical coincidence. The work led to a published ebook, A Guide to Causal Inference, authored by AI Skunkworks members Abhishek Dabas and Abhijit Krishna Menon.

Reinforcement Learning: Groups meet weekly to tackle sequential decision-making problems. Students implement everything from basic Q-learning to complex actor-critic architectures, applying these techniques to domains ranging from game-playing to portfolio management.

Kaggle: Competition-focused groups where students develop the specific skills that make them effective in data science contests—rapid prototyping, feature engineering, ensemble methods, the art of squeezing the last 0.1% of accuracy from a model.

Explainable AI: Perhaps the most crucial research area for AI deployment in regulated industries. Students investigate how to make black-box models interpretable, developing methods like SHAP values, partial dependence plots, and individual conditional expectation visualizations.

These groups meet weekly, establishing a rhythm of collaboration and accountability. “Through these projects, students, including myself, gained valuable skills to enhance their resumes,” Abhishek explains. “This experience directly contributed to securing part-time and full-time job opportunities. That is a major step forward in any professional career.”

The structure creates something traditional coursework rarely provides: sustained engagement with a single problem over months rather than weeks, collaboration with peers at different skill levels, and the experience of bringing a project from conception through deployment to maintenance.

What Gets Built in a Skunkworks

The projects sprawl across domains with an almost reckless ambition. Consider what happened when AI Skunkworks students connected with the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard. The challenge: single-cell RNA sequencing provides exhaustive profiles of individual cells, but the dissociation process destroys their spatial context within tissue. Spatial transcriptomics preserves location but sacrifices resolution and genomic coverage. How do you get both?

The answer became Tangram, a Python package written in PyTorch that uses deep learning to align single-cell data onto spatial datasets. The mathematics involves optimal transport algorithms and Kullback-Leibler divergence minimization—the kind of theoretical work that fills graduate coursework. But the students didn’t just derive equations. They built a tool that researchers across the world now use to reconstruct genome-wide, anatomically integrated spatial maps at single-cell resolution.

The work earned publication in Nature Methods in 2021. Independent benchmarks showed Tangram outperforming competing imputation methods in both accuracy and scalability. This wasn’t a class project that ended when the semester did. This was infrastructure that changed how biologists conduct research.

Or consider the medical imaging work with Harvard Medical School and Boston Children’s Hospital. Traditional diffusion MRI requires long scan times to characterize tissue microstructure—problematic when your patients are infants who can’t stay still or fetuses in utero. Students developed deep learning methods to estimate diffusion parameters from a fraction of the usual data, potentially eliminating the need for sedation in pediatric populations.

These aren’t hypothetical exercises. They’re attempts to bend technology toward saving lives—attempts that happen in parallel with, not instead of, formal education.

The Skills Nobody Teaches

“AI Skunkworks has not only helped students enhance their technical skills but also develop interpersonal and management abilities within the organization,” Abhishek notes. This distinction reveals something crucial about what experiential learning actually provides.

Technical skills—implementing neural networks, optimizing hyperparameters, debugging gradient explosions—these you can learn from coursework, though practice helps. But the interpersonal and management capabilities emerge only through actual collaboration on consequential projects.

You learn how to navigate disagreements about architectural decisions. How to review someone else’s code constructively. How to explain technical concepts to collaborators from different disciplines. How to manage scope creep when a project grows more ambitious than initially planned. How to handle the moment when you discover a fundamental flaw in your approach three months into development. How to transition a project to someone else when you graduate or move on.

These capabilities don’t appear in course catalogs. They emerge from sustained collaboration on projects that actually matter to people beyond your immediate team.

The Mentorship Engine

The architecture reveals itself through Brown’s approach to mentorship. Faculty mentor advanced students. Advanced students mentor newcomers. The cycle sustains itself, creating what researchers call “near-peer mentoring”—and the academic literature on this approach provides validation for what Brown built intuitively.

“Professor Brown has played a pivotal role mentoring students one-on-one and guiding projects,” Abhishek explains. “He has helped students share their work publicly through Medium articles, ebooks, and research publications. This is what sets AI Skunkworks apart.”

The one-on-one mentorship addresses a specific gap in graduate education. In large courses, you might interact with a professor during office hours or after class. In AI Skunkworks, you have someone invested in your specific project’s success, someone who will read your code, critique your approach, challenge your assumptions, and help you navigate the inevitable moments when nothing works.

But perhaps more importantly, the near-peer mentorship creates a sustainable ecosystem. Near-peer mentors enhance mentees’ sense of belonging to a scientific community and foster professional identity. For underrepresented groups in computing, these relationships reduce attrition and promote equity. Mentees feel less anxiety performing technical skills in front of someone who recently struggled with the same concepts. The process is mutually beneficial: mentors reinforce their own knowledge, develop leadership abilities, and increase their programming self-efficacy.

This structure enabled the explosive growth—from 300 to 940+ students—without collapsing under its own weight. Each cohort of advanced students can mentor the next wave of newcomers, creating a knowledge transfer pipeline that scales with the community’s growth.

The Agent Ecosystem

The most ambitious projects emerging from AI Skunkworks involve what they call “agentic frameworks”—systems where specialized AI agents coordinate to tackle domain challenges. These aren’t academic exercises in multi-agent systems. They’re attempts to build production-grade intelligence architectures.

Mycroft: Investment Intelligence
The framework explores how specialized agents can analyze the AI sector and implement investment strategies. Analytical agents gather market intelligence. Portfolio agents test approaches to transform knowledge into trades with risk management. Advisory agents explore human-AI interaction through conversational financial advising. Intelligence agents monitor news, sentiment, financial reports, and regulatory developments to maintain market awareness.

Students learn portfolio theory, natural language processing for sentiment analysis, and the discipline of systematic risk management. Some discover they’re better at building trading infrastructure than actually trading. That discovery itself counts as learning.

Madison: Marketing Intelligence
The Madison framework uses agents to transform branding and advertising. Intelligence agents provide market insights. Content agents create and optimize marketing materials while maintaining brand voice. Research agents uncover customer insights through synthetic persona development. Experience agents enhance customer journeys through AI concierge systems.

This isn’t abstract research. Students work with actual marketing challenges: maintaining consistent brand voice across channels, optimizing ad spend, predicting customer churn, personalizing content at scale. They learn that the hard part of marketing AI isn’t the algorithms—it’s understanding what customers actually want.

Dayhoff: Bioinformatics
Named for Margaret Oakley Dayhoff, pioneer of computational biology, this framework orchestrates agents for genomic analysis, epidemiological tracking, and molecular modeling. Students work with real genomic datasets, confronting the ethical and technical challenges of medical AI development. They discover that biology doesn’t respect clean data boundaries, that clinical applications demand interpretability over raw accuracy, that lives depend on their code not failing.

Dewey: Educational Intelligence
The Dewey framework applies the agentic model to education itself, creating content generation, tutorial, and assessment agents designed for personalized learning. The system includes Ada (calculus), Newton (physics), and Grace (algorithms)—specialized tutors that use Socratic questioning and adaptive scaffolding rather than providing direct answers.

Tools for Builders

The productivity of AI Skunkworks depends on infrastructure that makes complex AI development accessible to students from any discipline. The projects here draw inspiration not from academic computer science but from visual effects and gaming.

Klee, an open-source procedural AI engine, uses visual node-based programming inspired by Houdini and Unreal Engine. Data flows become visual nodes that can be connected and rearranged. Researchers in biology or other fields can develop custom AI tools without drowning in low-level code. The approach has proven itself in VFX—entire games like Fortnite are built using visual programming. Why not machine learning pipelines?

Kindo serves as a high-level API for reinforcement learning, bridging the divide between Stable Baselines 3 (PyTorch) and TF-Agents (TensorFlow 2.X). Students can switch frameworks based on what their specific environment demands while maintaining a consistent interface. The goal: let people experiment with complex problems like autonomous navigation without getting bogged down in framework compatibility issues.

These tools embody a philosophy: lower the barrier to building things, and more things get built. Students who might struggle with low-level PyTorch implementations can still architect sophisticated reinforcement learning systems using Kindo. Biologists who understand their domain deeply but lack years of programming experience can still build AI analysis pipelines using Klee.

Public Knowledge, Public Impact

One of Brown’s distinctive practices: encouraging students to share their work publicly. Not just in academic venues—though papers in Nature Methods and the British Machine Vision Conference certainly count—but through Medium articles, ebooks, and open-source repositories.

This public sharing serves multiple purposes. It forces students to articulate their work clearly for a general audience, developing communication skills that most technical education ignores. It creates a portfolio that’s immediately visible to potential employers. And it contributes to the broader ecosystem of AI knowledge, making insights accessible to people who will never attend an academic conference or pay for journal access.

The result: dozens of articles explaining everything from Deep Q-Learning to the ethics of algorithmic bias, each written by students who recently grappled with these concepts themselves. The best explanations often come from people who remember what it was like not to understand, who can anticipate confusion because they recently experienced it.

The Cognitive Dimension

Not all projects involve genomics or financial markets. The Cognitive Type Project investigates something humans interact with constantly but rarely analyze: typography. By developing computational tools to design typefaces with measurable cognitive properties, students bridge creative design and empirical data science.

The research led to Typography-MNIST (TMNIST), an MNIST-style image dataset for categorizing glyphs and font styles. The PANOSE dataset annotates thousands of typefaces with metrics like serif style, weight, proportion, and stroke variation. The goal: enable designers to create fonts that could improve click-through rates on digital ads, enhance reading levels in children’s books, or support individuals with dyslexia through personalized typography.

Other socially-oriented projects sprawl across unexpected domains:

The Economics of Happiness annotates countries with economic indicators to predict World Happiness Report scores, then uses model interpretability to determine which factors most influence national well-being. Is this economics? Data science? Psychology? Policy research? Yes.

TwitterNet and EmojiNet create lexical dictionaries for social media communication, analyzing everything from hashtag synonyms to the ethnic and gender neutrality of baby names. The goal: identify and mitigate bias in machine learning models before they perpetuate discrimination at scale.

The Warhol Project focuses on computational art and AI-assisted design, exploring intersections that traditional departmental structures keep carefully separated.

The RAMAN Effect investigates whether Raman spectroscopy paired with machine learning could detect not just COVID-19 in wastewater, but recreational drugs, sexually transmitted disease markers, and dozens of other public health indicators simultaneously.

These projects share a common thread: they attempt things cautious students might avoid. They’re ambitious swings at hard problems, undertaken with the understanding that partial solutions and illuminating failures both constitute progress.

The Transition Zone: Humanitarians AI

“Humanitarians AI is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit that allows fellows to continue doing research, taking workshops, and learning post-graduation by coding for a cause and continuing STEM AI research,” Abhishek explains. This addresses a specific challenge facing international students: “This allows people to continue to do research to stop the OPT clock while continuing to learn until they get a great post-graduate job offer.”

The architecture is deliberate. Students build portfolios and develop skills at AI Skunkworks during their academic years. After graduation, many transition to Humanitarians AI to continue working on projects with sustained social impact. The pipeline transforms student experimentation into consequential contribution while providing a bridge between graduation and full-time employment.

“For mentors concerned that Humanitarians AI will leave their research projects,” Abhishek notes, “the fellows let us know at least a month beforehand that they are taking that next step in life, and another AI fellow comes in to shadow to take over the project.”

This continuity matters. It means projects don’t die when their original creators graduate. It means institutional knowledge accumulates rather than evaporating every semester. It means the tools and systems built in AI Skunkworks can evolve into production infrastructure serving real users through Humanitarians AI.

Projects like Ada, Newton, Grace, and CatBot (a cat adoption assistant that matches potential adopters with suitable cats and promotes special-needs adoptions) demonstrate this continuum. What begins as a student experiment in the Skunkworks can evolve into a deployed system helping actual users solve actual problems.

  • Over 300 students mentored through Red Hat training materials, earning the singular Red Hat Academy Director’s Award out of thousands of participating institutions worldwide

  • Papers published in Nature Methods and the British Machine Vision Conference

  • The RISE Award 2022 for procedural content generation with DC-GANs

  • Northeastern’s singular Fostering Engineering Innovation in Education Award in 2022

  • Collaborations with Harvard Medical School, Boston Children’s Hospital, and the Broad Institute

  • Datasets on Kaggle, code repositories on GitHub, dozens of Medium articles explaining complex algorithms

  • Multiple research groups meeting weekly on causal inference, reinforcement learning, Kaggle competitions, and explainable AI

But the deeper impact lies in the career trajectories of students who passed through the program. “This experience directly contributed to securing part-time and full-time job opportunities,” Abhishek confirms. When these students interview, they don’t gesture vaguely at coursework. They point to deployed systems, open-source contributions, published research.

The job market provides validation. As of 2024, 81% of employers report using skills-based hiring, and 95% expect it to become the norm. Credentials alone no longer suffice. Companies want people who can demonstrate they can perform on day one. AI Skunkworks produces exactly that: graduates who have already built real systems, already collaborated with research institutions, already managed projects from conception through deployment.

The portfolio has become the credential. Experience has become the degree. But crucially, this happens alongside traditional education, not instead of it.

The Elite Comparison

Traditional elite institutions like MIT, Stanford, and Carnegie Mellon dominate AI research. But their experimental “skunkworks” projects often remain the domain of specialized research labs or senior PhD students. Northeastern’s approach is distinctive: it integrates the experiential playground into the broader graduate student community, making it accessible to students from computer science, business, arts, and life sciences.

MIT’s Media Lab produces high-level research with industry sponsorship, deeply specialized and focused on academic output. Stanford’s Human-Centered AI Initiative emphasizes interdisciplinary work and ethics. Carnegie Mellon’s foundational research travels to industry primarily via “human heads”—the alumni who leave with knowledge. These are all valid models. They’re just not the same model.

AI Skunkworks is a “do tank” rather than a think tank—a space where new paradigms are created, explored, and launched to serve society. While institutions like Georgia Tech and Arizona State have expanded their digital footprints for economies of scale, Northeastern has expanded its experiential footprint, prioritizing the quality of student portfolios over the quantity of degree production.

The comparison reveals something important: there’s more than one way to train the AI workforce. Not everyone needs to publish in top-tier conferences. Some people need to learn how to ship working code, manage technical debt, collaborate across disciplines, and turn ambiguous requirements into functioning systems. The academy has traditionally been better at the former than the latter.

AI Skunkworks doesn’t ask students to choose. It demands both.

What You Build When You’re Not Afraid

Three core principles structure the AI Skunkworks environment:

Learning by Doing: Students gain expertise by working on authentic projects with real-world applications rather than just theoretical exercises.

Trust but Verify: Critical thinking and rigorous validation of AI solutions through systematic testing and evaluation.

Try It: A safe space for experimentation, innovation, and productive failure where students can learn from both successes and mistakes.

These aren’t just aspirational slogans. They’re structural commitments that change what becomes possible. When failure carries no permanent academic penalty, students attempt things they otherwise wouldn’t. They write parsers. They develop APIs. They create visualization pipelines. They investigate technologies at the frontier of what’s known.

Some experiments succeed spectacularly, leading to Nature Methods publications. Others fail, teaching expensive lessons cheaply. Both outcomes count as learning. Both contribute to the student’s portfolio. Both develop the capacity to navigate ambiguity—perhaps the most valuable skill in an AI-mediated world.

Now AND Later

“Under Professor Brown’s leadership, I truly believe it will continue to make a lasting impact,” Abhishek concludes. The belief rests on observable outcomes: students securing positions they wouldn’t have otherwise gotten, projects continuing beyond their creators’ graduation, knowledge accumulating rather than evaporating.

The traditional model isn’t wrong. It’s incomplete. Students need theoretical foundations—the mathematics of neural networks, the principles of statistical learning, the formal analysis of computational complexity. AI Skunkworks doesn’t replace this education. It complements it with something equally essential: the experience of building systems that people actually use.

You’re still in that second year of graduate school. Still filling notebooks with equations. Still getting As on problem sets. But now you’re also debugging production code, explaining your architectural decisions to collaborators from different disciplines, managing the scope of a project that started small and grew ambitious, writing Medium articles that teach others what you recently learned yourself.

The gap between theory and practice doesn’t have to wait four years to close. It doesn’t have to close at all—in the sense of choosing one over the other. It can remain open, productive, generative: theory informing practice, practice revealing gaps in theory, both advancing together.

That’s not revolutionary. It’s just comprehensive. School AND doing. Learning AND building. Theory AND practice.

The Skunkworks isn’t waiting for permission. It’s demonstrating what becomes possible when you refuse to choose between education and experience, when you demand both simultaneously, when you assume students can handle real problems if you give them appropriate support and the structural space to fail productively.

AI Skunkworks grew from 300 students to over 940 not through marketing but through results. Students told other students that something different was happening here. That there was a way to graduate with both a transcript and a portfolio, with both theoretical understanding and practical capability, with both classroom credentials and real-world experience.

In an economy where AI-related job postings increased twenty-fold in ten months, where 81% of employers use skills-based hiring, where portfolios increasingly matter more than transcripts, this approach isn’t optional. It’s essential.

You can study AI. You should study AI. But you must also do AI. The conjunction matters. The “AND” is the point.

The question isn’t when you get to build something that matters. The question is why you’d wait.
