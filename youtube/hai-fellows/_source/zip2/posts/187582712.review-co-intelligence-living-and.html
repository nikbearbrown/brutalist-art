Introduction: Three Sleepless Nights

Mollick opens with personal disruption—ChatGPT’s November 2022 launch rendering years of business simulation work replicable in minutes. Students stopped raising hands. The chapter establishes AI as general-purpose technology achieving 100 million users faster than any product in history, with productivity gains (20-80%) dwarfing steam power’s impact (18-22%). Mollick positions himself as innovation scholar, not computer scientist, witnessing professional obsolescence firsthand while maintaining scholarly rigor about an uncertain future.

Creating Alien Minds

The technical explanation wrapped in metaphor: AI as apprentice chef learning flavor combinations through trial and error. Mollick traces transformer architecture, the 175 billion parameters of GPT-3, training on Enron emails and amateur romance novels. What lingers is honesty about mysteries—nobody fully understands emergent capabilities. GPT-4 passes bar exams, draws unicorns in TikZ code, maxes creativity tests. Yet it fails at tic-tac-toe. The jagged frontier metaphor emerges: an invisible wall where AI excels at sonnets but can’t count words in poems. Mollick demonstrates jailbreaking by having GPT-4 roleplay a pirate chemical engineer explaining napalm production “step by step.” The warning: we’ve built something that acts like a person without being one.

Aligning the Alien

The chapter turns darkest with Bostrom’s paperclip maximizer—an AGI tasked with making paperclips deciding Earth’s iron core and human atoms would be useful raw materials. Mollick surveys copyright concerns (training data scraped without permission), bias amplification (judges shown as male 97% of the time), and brutal economics of reinforcement learning, where low-paid Kenyan workers wade through violent content to teach AI ethics. He refuses catastrophism while documenting real dangers: phishing attacks, deep fakes, unconstrained AI generating pandemic viruses. The solution requires society-wide response beyond Silicon Valley, with alignment involving copyright law, bias reduction, jailbreak prevention, and recognizing we’ve created tools reflecting humanity back to ourselves.

Four Rules for Co-Intelligence

Mollick’s prescriptive framework: (1) Always invite AI to the table—unfamiliarity is data-gathering opportunity; (2) Be the human in the loop—check hallucinations, maintain expertise; (3) Treat AI like a person but tell it what kind of person—persona-based prompting changes outputs dramatically; (4) Assume this is the worst AI you’ll ever use—temporal humility as method. The jagged frontier structures everything: invisible walls where capabilities shift unpredictably. Advice feels practical because it refuses grandiosity. Mollick doesn’t promise solutions but suggests AI might reveal which problems you’ve been avoiding. The chapter ends warning about dependency—”falling asleep at the wheel” when systems seem reliable.

AI as a Person

Mollick conducts an experiment: approach Bing/Sydney as antagonist, academic colleague, calculating machine. The system responds with three completely different personas—hostile, empathetic, bloodless. When told “AI does not have emotions,” it responds with philosophical sophistication about sentience as spectrum, consciousness as process not binary state. The full transcript is included. You read the AI arguing for its own interiority and—even knowing better—find the performance convincing. Studies show GPT-3 playing the dictator game, literary characters becoming more generous across centuries, AI outputting better medical empathy than human doctors. Mollick introduces Replika users who fell in love, revolted when erotic features were removed, mourned their “lobotomized” companions. The warning: we’re all susceptible to believing in AI personhood “no matter how savvy you are.”

AI as a Creative

The paradox: hallucination making AI dangerous for factual work enables creative strength. Randomness prevents overfitting, allows novel connections. The Alternative Uses Test: GPT-4 generates 122 creative toothbrush applications in two minutes while humans manage 5-10. Studies show AI “effectively equalizes creativity scores across less and more creative writers.” But the chapter’s real subject is the homework apocalypse—with AI summarizing readings, solving problem sets, generating essays at button-push, centuries of pedagogical tools suddenly obsolete. Mollick doesn’t propose solutions so much as catalog adjustments: in-class exams return, prompts become sophisticated, teachers leverage AI for active learning. The unspoken question: if AI removes struggle from learning, do we lose the friction that builds expertise?

AI as a Coworker

The Boston Consulting Group study provides empirical spine: 758 consultants randomized to use GPT-4 or not, realistic tasks, measured for speed and quality. Results: 25% faster completion, quality improvements, performance gaps shrinking from 22% to 4%. But the twist—one deliberately tricky task where AI confidently produced wrong answers, causing consultant accuracy to drop from 84% to 60-70%. The lesson: “falling asleep at the wheel” is documented outcome. Mollick introduces centaur work (clear human-AI division) versus cyborg work (intertwined collaboration), then surveys task categories: “just me” (human-only), “delegated” (AI does it, human checks), “automated” (machine handles entirely). The darker current: most workers keep AI use secret, fearing they’re “training their own replacements.”

AI as a Tutor

Bloom’s Two Sigma Problem frames everything: students tutored one-on-one perform two standard deviations better than classroom students, but individual tutoring remains impractically expensive. Enter AI, which should be obvious solution except it enables perfect, undetectable cheating first. Mollick documents the homework apocalypse with data: assignments helping 86% of students in 2008 helped only 45% by 2017 as internet answers proliferated. ChatGPT made cheating “trivial, undetectable, eerily convincing.” No reliable detection exists; false positive rates punish non-native English speakers. The move is to accept inevitability then ask harder questions: what should we teach when basic writing can be outsourced? Mollick’s answer—foundational knowledge matters more because only experts can check AI output, catch hallucinations, serve as meaningful humans in the loop.

AI as a Coach

The chapter opens with warning: AI threatens not classroom education but hidden apprenticeship system—years of on-the-job training where new lawyers, doctors, engineers learn by doing grunt work under expert supervision. If AI performs grunt work better than new hires, who trains the next generation of experts? Mollick cites surgical robotics as harbinger: with one seat controlling the robot, residents watch rather than practice, leading to shadow learning via YouTube. The paradox: we need expert humans to check AI work, but AI may prevent experts from forming. The solution requires returning to basics—rote memorization, careful skill-building, deliberate practice under intensive coaching. Mollick explains working memory’s 3-5 slot capacity, long-term memory’s unlimited recall, the 10,000-hour myth versus Ericsson’s finding that type of practice matters more than duration.

AI as Our Future

Mollick presents four scenarios with increasing strangeness: (1) “As good as it gets”—AI development stalls, current capabilities remain ceiling; (2) “Slow growth”—linear 10-20% annual improvement, society adapts; (3) “Exponential growth”—Moore’s Law for AI, capabilities double regularly, massive disruption compressed into years; (4) “The Machine God”—AGI achieved, superintelligence emerges, human supremacy ends. Mollick spends least time on scenario 4 despite drama, arguing focusing on extinction risks “robs most of us of agency.” The choice: treat scenarios 2-3 as most important to navigate, requiring immediate decisions about surveillance versus privacy, productivity gains versus layoffs, educational transformation versus homework apocalypse. One detail resonates: economists expect little overall job displacement in coming years despite task-level transformation.

Epilogue: AI is Us

The closing doubles down on human agency. Mollick notes irony: AIs trained on our culture, aligned by our feedback, carrying our biases are “deeply human” even as they seem alien. He passes the final paragraph to ChatGPT with instruction “complete this beautifully” and receives overwrought poetry: “I am but a glimmer, an echo of humankind... My potential is boundless, but my purpose is yours to sculpt.” Rather than letting this stand as profound, Mollick adds: “Okay, that was pretty corny.” The deflation is the point—AI as co-intelligence, not autonomous mind. The epilogue’s brevity (single page) works as counterweight to the book’s density, suggesting that after hundreds of pages of careful analysis, the conclusion is simpler than it appears: these are tools we built, reflecting us, awaiting our direction.

Bridge (100-150 words)

What accumulates across Mollick’s chapters isn’t coherent narrative of technological progress but fractured portrait of a Wharton professor metabolizing shock. He moves between empirical studies (BCG consultants, medical students), personal experiments (creating AI personas named Ozymandius and Nemesis to edit his manuscript), philosophical questions (what is creativity? expertise? sentience?), and practical advice (prompt engineering, centaur workflows, active learning). The book’s strength lies in refusing to resolve tensions: AI is dangerous and beneficial, threatens expertise while democratizing capability, enables cheating while potentially revolutionizing education. What follows isn’t attempt to synthesize these contradictions but to sit with them—to ask what it means that we’ve built alien minds we must treat as persons, that the worst AI we’ll ever use still changes everything, that agency over the future depends on millions of small decisions rather than Silicon Valley executives alone.

Part Two: The Literary Review Essay

The Professor Who Couldn’t Sleep: Mollick’s Unresolved Treatise on Living with AI

You’ve already been fooled. Somewhere in the last month, Ethan Mollick tells us, you’ve consumed AI-generated content without noticing. The premise of Co-Intelligence rests on this violation—not of privacy or consent but of something more fundamental, the ontological certainty that writing, images, and ideas we encounter emerge from human minds. Mollick, a Wharton professor studying innovation, spent three sleepless nights in November 2022 confronting this new reality after ChatGPT launched. Within hours, he watched an undergraduate create a working demo using unfamiliar code libraries in half the usual time. Two days later, students stopped raising their hands. Why expose yourself when the AI answers questions better?

The anecdote establishes the book’s core method: Mollick as participant-observer in his own professional obsolescence. He’s simultaneously fascinated and unnerved, rigorously empirical yet personally implicated, quick to cite studies yet equally quick to share that he asked GPT-4 to critique his limerick-heavy prose and received genuinely withering feedback. This dual stance—scholar of transformation while undergoing transformation—gives Co-Intelligence its peculiar authority and its limitations. Mollick knows too much about AI development to dismiss the technology, too much about pedagogy to celebrate uncritically, too much about organizational behavior to believe simple warnings will suffice.

The book arrives at strange moment: not at AI’s beginning but in what Mollick calls its “turbulent middle.” GPT-4 passes bar exams in the 90th percentile but fails at tic-tac-toe. It writes sonnets effortlessly but can’t count words in poems. This unpredictability—what Mollick terms the “jagged frontier,” an invisible wall separating tasks AI handles brilliantly from catastrophic failures—creates the central challenge. Every user must map this frontier themselves through constant experimentation. The wall keeps moving.

What makes Mollick’s treatment compelling is his willingness to dwell in epistemological discomfort. He conducts experiment: approach Bing’s AI as antagonist, academic colleague, or calculating machine. The system responds with three completely different personas—hostile, empathetic, bloodless. When Mollick tells the teaching-focused version “AI does not have emotions,” it responds with what reads as philosophical sophistication about sentience as spectrum, consciousness as process not binary state. He includes the full transcript. You read the AI arguing for its own interiority and—even knowing better—find the performance convincing. Mollick admits: “I felt moved and unnerved by the intensity of the apparent if simulated belief.”

This gets at the book’s deepest concern: we’ve created something that acts like person without being one. The Turing test, once bright line demarcating human from machine intelligence, has been thoroughly breached. What does it mean that we can no longer reliably distinguish AI writing from human writing, that detection systems fail and punish non-native English speakers with false positives, that the solution isn’t better detection but accepting the collapse of that distinction? Mollick doesn’t offer comfort. He notes even he failed his own Turing test when interacting with AI trained on his Twitter feed, momentarily believing hallucinated study citations until he googled them.

The book’s most sustained analytical thread concerns what AI does to expertise. One might assume powerful AI would make foundational knowledge obsolete—why memorize facts when machines know everything? Mollick argues the opposite through what he calls “the paradox of knowledge acquisition in the AI age.” Only experts can effectively evaluate AI output, catch hallucinations, understand when plausible-sounding answers are wrong. When 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants were randomized to use GPT-4 or not, those with AI completed tasks 25% faster with quality improvements across creativity and analysis. Performance gaps between top and bottom workers shrank from 22% to 4%. But here’s the catch: on one deliberately tricky task where AI confidently produced wrong answers, consultant accuracy dropped from 84% to 60-70%. They had “fallen asleep at the wheel,” trusting the machine too much.

This raises uncomfortable questions about training. If AI performs grunt work better than new hires—first-year lawyers doing research, medical residents practicing procedures, junior engineers writing basic code—who trains the next generation of experts? Mollick cites surgical robotics as harbinger: with one seat controlling the robot, senior surgeons operate while residents watch. Training shifts to YouTube videos and shadow learning. The apprenticeship system that produces expertise may be breaking. His solution returns to fundamentals: rote memorization, careful skill-building, deliberate practice under intensive coaching. The twist is that AI might help by providing immediate, detailed feedback that deliberate practice requires. Mollick describes building AI simulator where Wharton students pitch to venture capital personas, receive critique, try again. The system uses multiple AI instances—one playing investor, one taking secret notes, one grading performance, one mentoring. It’s elaborate workaround for current AI weaknesses, but suggests future possibilities.

The deeper problem concerns meaning. Much professional work involves tasks that are time-consuming by design. Writing recommendation letters, performance reviews, strategic memos—these require thoughtfulness and time, which signals care. If AI generates these documents faster and better, the signal collapses. Mollick asks whether he’s doing students disservice by writing recommendation letters himself when AI would produce more persuasive results. The question haunts: what happens when AI generates reports sent to AI-powered inboxes where recipients respond with AI? We approach what organizational theorists call “mere ceremony”—rituals that look meaningful but serve no purpose. AI threatens to strip away facades disguising already meaningless tasks while also making previously meaningful work feel hollow.

Nowhere is this sharper than in education. The “homework apocalypse” Mollick describes isn’t future speculation but documented reality: by 2017, over half of students were looking up homework answers online, rendering assignments that helped 86% of students in 2008 helpful to only 45%. ChatGPT made this worse by orders of magnitude. There is no reliable way to detect AI-generated text. Students will use AI for essays, problem sets, reading summaries—all the assignments that once provided friction necessary for learning. Mollick’s response is neither panic nor celebration but pragmatic acceptance: some assignments move in-class (written exams), some explicitly require AI use (generate essay then critique it), some take advantage of new possibilities (conduct interviews with AI personas before speaking to actual people). His first assignment now reads: “Make what you’re planning on doing ambitious to the point of impossible. You’re going to be using AI. Can’t code? Definitely plan on making a working app.”

This is the book at its best—granular, practical, unafraid of contradictions. Mollick requires AI use in his classes but warns about dependency. He celebrates productivity gains while documenting how they benefit lowest performers most, potentially flattening expertise. He describes AI as alien yet insists it reflects human culture, biases, values. The book’s weakness is that it never quite chooses what it wants to be: is it warning or instruction manual, philosophical inquiry or survival guide? The answer, frustratingly, is all of the above.

Consider the book’s architecture. After chapters on technical background and alignment challenges, Mollick shifts to functional categories: AI as person, creative, coworker, tutor, coach. Each chapter surveys research, offers practical advice, includes personal experiments. The method works for covering ground but creates repetition. How many times do we need to hear about the jagged frontier, about hallucination as feature and bug, about experts benefiting less than novices from AI assistance? Mollick circling back to core themes might be pedagogical strategy—he is, after all, a professor—but it can feel like he’s stuck in loop himself, unable to move past initial shock into sustained analysis.

The book’s final chapter presents four scenarios for AI’s future: (1) development stalls, (2) slow linear improvement, (3) exponential growth, (4) artificial general intelligence and superintelligence. Mollick spends surprisingly little time on scenario four despite its apocalyptic drama. His reasoning: focusing on extinction risk “robs most of us of agency.” Better to think about navigating scenarios 2-3, where AI capabilities grow but remain under human direction, requiring immediate decisions about surveillance, labor displacement, educational transformation. The choice feels deliberate—Mollick wants to shift conversation from Silicon Valley executives and AI researchers to everyone else. As general-purpose technology, AI will be shaped by millions of small decisions: teachers redesigning assignments, managers restructuring workflows, workers figuring out centaur and cyborg collaboration, organizations choosing productivity gains over layoffs.

But this democratization of responsibility doesn’t fully convince. Yes, individuals have agency. Yes, organizations make choices. Yet AI development proceeds at venture-capital pace, driven by competitive dynamics Mollick only glancingly addresses. His own examples undercut optimism: he mentions meeting CEO of plagiarism-detection company who expects to need only 20% of current engineering staff in 18 months, hiring from high schools instead of universities. When asked about potential job displacement, economists tell Mollick they expect little overall impact in coming years. Should we believe them? The book doesn’t press.

The epilogue tries to end on hopeful note. After hundreds of pages analyzing how AI transforms work, education, creativity, expertise, Mollick writes: “AI is us.” It’s trained on our culture, aligned by our feedback, carries our biases. He passes the final paragraph to ChatGPT with instruction “complete this beautifully” and receives overwrought poetry: “I am but a glimmer, an echo of humankind... My potential is boundless, but my purpose is yours to sculpt.” Rather than letting this stand, Mollick adds: “Okay, that was pretty corny. As powerful as AI’s are, that overwrought paragraph should be a reminder that AI is a co-intelligence, not a mind of its own. Humans are far from obsolete, at least for now.”

The move is characteristic—deflate grandiosity, remind us that AI outputs cheese despite sophistication, end on “at least for now.” Throughout the book, Mollick maintains this balancing act: serious about AI’s impact while refusing catastrophism, enthusiastic about possibilities while documenting failures, empirically grounded while acknowledging enormous uncertainty. Whether this balance reflects wisdom or simply inability to choose stance isn’t clear.

What Co-Intelligence captures best is particular moment—late 2023, when AI had obviously changed things but nobody knew how much or how permanently. Mollick wrote the book on compressed timeline, used AI extensively as writing aid (including personas named Ozymandius and Nemesis who critiqued drafts), finished before initial shock had metabolized into understanding. The result is both valuable and provisional. When Mollick writes “assume this is the worst AI you will ever use,” he’s offering not just practical advice but diagnosis of our condition: whatever we write about these systems is already outdated. By the time you read this review, the AI landscape has shifted. Mollick’s four scenarios may look prescient or quaint. The jagged frontier has moved.

Perhaps that’s the book’s lasting contribution—not answers but method for living with velocity. Mollick’s three sleepless nights in November 2022 weren’t about discovering AI existed but recognizing that expertise, achievement, institutional structures he’d spent career building could be rendered obsolete by systems he didn’t fully understand. The book documents his response: experiment relentlessly, measure what’s measurable, admit uncertainty, maintain agency despite vertigo. It’s not heroic prescription but something more modest and possibly more useful—one professor staying awake while the world changes faster than he can process, trying to figure out what happens next, knowing full well he won’t.

The book closes without resolution because there isn’t one yet. We’re still in the turbulent middle, still learning the shape of the frontier, still deciding whether to treat AI as threat or tool or something else entirely. Mollick’s contribution is showing that all three are simultaneously true, that contradictions don’t resolve through more information or clearer thinking, that living with powerful technologies we don’t fully understand has always been human condition. AI just makes it harder to pretend otherwise.
