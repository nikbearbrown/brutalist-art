Every time I tell someone I use AI for social media, I can see the assumption forming in real time. They think I open ChatGPT, type “write me a caption”, hit enter, copy-paste, and move on with my day.

Here’s what actually happens - and why running social media with AI is so much messier, more human, and more interesting than people think.

The prompt is the last thing I do

This is the part that surprises people. By the time I’m typing anything into an AI tool, most of the work is already done.

I read the source material first. All of it. If I’m writing about a story from Homes of Hope India, I need to understand who this person is, what happened, what matters about it, and what the audience needs to feel - not just know. If I’m writing for Humanitarians.ai, I need to understand the program, the context, and why it matters right now.

That part isn’t optional and AI can’t do it for me. If I skip it, the output sounds like exactly what it is — a machine summarizing something it doesn’t care about.

Tone isn't a setting you toggle

I manage content for two organizations with completely different audiences. Those two audiences need different everything. Different language. Different emotional register. Different sentence rhythm. A caption that works perfectly for one will completely miss on the other.

AI doesn’t know that automatically. And honestly? Even when you tell it, it doesn’t always get it right. I’ve prompted the same tool to be “warm and personal” and gotten back something that sounds like a Hallmark card written by a consultant. Close, but wrong in a way that’s hard to explain until you’ve trained yourself to hear it.

That ear — knowing when something sounds almost right but isn’t — that’s the actual skill. Not prompting.

I'm editing for what AI can't see

Even after the draft comes back, the work isn’t done. AI doesn’t know what happened in last week’s post. It doesn’t know that the audience reacted strongly to a particular phrase last time. It doesn’t know that this specific story needs to be handled gently because there’s a real person behind it. It doesn’t know the comments section.

I do. So I edit with all of that in my head — the context that lives in me, not in the tool.

Sometimes that means rewriting half the draft. Sometimes it means scrapping it and starting over with a different tool. Sometimes the AI nails it and I just clean up a couple of lines. But I’m never just accepting what comes out. The draft is a starting point, not a finished product.

What people actually get wrong

The biggest misconception isn’t that AI writes bad content. It can write pretty good content. The misconception is that using AI means you’re doing less work.

The work just shifted. Instead of staring at a blank page trying to find the first sentence, I’m reading deeply, making judgment calls about tone, choosing between tools, editing with context the AI doesn’t have, and making sure the final thing sounds like it came from a person who actually cares, because it did.

AI didn’t replace my judgment. It gave me a faster way to express it. But the judgment has to be there first, or the whole thing falls apart.

So what does my actual process look like?

Since people always ask:

1. Research. I read the article, the story, the brief, whatever the source material is. I make sure I actually understand it, not just surface-level.

2. Decide the tone. Before I open any tool, I know what this piece needs to sound like. Warm? Authoritative? Urgent? Gentle? That decision is mine, not the AI’s.

3. Pick the tool. Claude for professional and structured. I have been using Wilkes for intimate and story-driven and trying different tools.

4. Prompt with context. I give the tool what it need, not just “write a caption” but the emotional core, the audience, the angle I want.

5. Listen to the output. This is where most people stop. I read it out loud (actually out loud, not in my head). If something sounds off, I figure out why, wrong tone? Wrong tool? Wrong angle?

6. Edit with everything the AI doesn’t know. Past audience reactions, the person behind the story, what’s been posted recently, what the comments have been saying. That’s all me.

7. Post. Finally.

It’s not glamorous. But it’s real. And it works.

The tools are powerful, genuinely. But scrolling past a caption and thinking “that felt real” has nothing to do with the tool and everything to do with the person who knew what “real” was supposed to sound like before the tool ever opened.

That’s the part AI can’t do. And honestly? That’s the part I love.
