So you got laid off. Or your OPT is running out. How long do you have?

If you’re on H-1B and just got terminated, you have 60 days to find a new sponsoring employer. Add in a couple weeks of notice period, and you’re looking at roughly 80 days total. Hence the name: 80 Days to Stay.

That’s not a lot of time to apply to 200 companies and hope one of them understands visa sponsorship.

Wait, most companies don’t understand sponsorship?

Correct. About 75-90% of US employers have never filed for an H-1B visa. Not because they can’t afford it—a typical H-1B filing costs maybe $5,000-$10,000 in legal fees and government charges. That’s pocket change for a startup that just raised $15 million.

They don’t sponsor because they don’t know how. They think it’s complicated, risky, or prohibitively expensive. Most hiring managers have never heard of OPT (Optional Practical Training), which is the work authorization you get automatically after graduating with an F-1 visa. On OPT, you can work for 1-3 years without the employer needing to sponsor anything.

But if HR sees “requires visa sponsorship” on your application, they often just... delete it. Even though you’re telling them “I don’t need sponsorship right now.”

So I’m invisible to most companies?

Pretty much. Unless you know which companies have already figured this out.

That’s what 80 Days to Stay does.

What 80 Days to Stay Actually Built

Okay, so what is this thing?

It’s a database. But not a normal job board. It’s a map of which companies have provably hired international workers before.

Here’s how they built it:

Step 1: Find every funded company in America

The SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) requires any company that raises money from investors to file something called Form D. It’s public data. It includes:

  • Company name

  • Address

  • Industry

  • How much money they raised

  • When they raised it

They downloaded every Form D filing they could find—over 500,000 companies. Then filtered to the ones most likely to hire tech talent: biotech, software, healthcare AI, data science companies. That got them to about 50,000-60,000 relevant targets.

Step 2: Find every visa sponsorship in US history

The Department of Labor maintains something called the LCA (Labor Condition Application) database. Every time a company wants to hire someone on H-1B, H-1B1, or E-3 visa, they have to file an LCA. This data is public and goes back to 1970.

Each LCA filing includes:

  • Company name

  • Job title being sponsored

  • When they filed

  • Location

  • Sometimes even the salary range

They downloaded millions of these records and mapped them to the SEC companies.

Step 3: Scrape the actual company websites

They wrote crawlers to visit ~25,000 company websites (62% success rate from domain inference). For each site, they:

  • Grabbed up to 50 pages

  • Identified if the company has a careers page

  • Detected which ATS (Applicant Tracking System) the company uses, if any

  • Found current job postings

Then they fed all that text to an LLM (large language model) to analyze: Does this company actually hire the kind of people we’re looking for?

Wait, so for each company you have...

  • How much funding they have ✓

  • How many visa applications they’ve filed (and when) ✓

  • What job titles they sponsored ✓

  • Whether they use an ATS (and which one) ✓

  • Whether they’re currently hiring ✓

Exactly.

That’s... kind of a superpower.

It is. Because now you can do something traditional job seekers can’t: target companies based on proven behavior, not hope.

The Three Kinds of Companies

So how do I use this data?

First, you need to understand that not all companies are the same. Based on their visa filing history, companies fall into three tiers:

Tier 1: Proven Recent Sponsors These are companies that filed 3+ LCA applications in the last 3 years.

Example:

  • Company: BioStartup Inc

  • LCA filings 2022-2024: 8 total

  • Last filing: August 2024

  • Job titles sponsored: “Research Scientist,” “Bioinformatics Engineer”

  • Trend: Increasing

  • Detected ATS: Lever

What this means: They know how to sponsor. They do it regularly. They’ve probably got immigration counsel or an HR person who handles it. When you apply here, visa sponsorship isn’t a scary unknown—it’s Tuesday.

Tier 2: Former Sponsors Companies with historical LCA filings, but none in the last 3 years.

Example:

  • Company: DataTech Labs

  • Total LCA filings: 5

  • Last filing: 2020

  • Recent funding: $12M Series A (2024)

  • Detected ATS: Greenhouse

What this means: They’ve done it before, but maybe the team changed, or they’ve been in “heads down building” mode for a few years. With fresh funding, they’re probably scaling again. They just need a reminder that they already know how this works.

Tier 3: Never Sponsored Companies with significant funding but zero LCA history.

Example:

  • Company: NewBio Therapeutics

  • LCA filings: 0

  • Funding: $20M Series B (2025)

  • Industry: Biotech

  • Detected ATS: None (probably email or basic web form)

  • Team size: ~30 people

What this means: They can afford to sponsor (they just raised $20M), but they’ve never done it. They might think it’s impossible, or they might not even know what OPT is.

These need different approaches, right?

Exactly. And this is where most students mess up—they send the same resume to all three types.

How ATS Systems Actually Work (And When They Matter)

Before we talk strategy, I need to know: will a robot delete my resume?

Maybe. Depends on the company. Let’s break down what an ATS actually is.

An Applicant Tracking System is software that companies use to manage job applications. Think of it as a filing cabinet that tries to automatically organize resumes.

When you upload your resume:

  1. The ATS tries to parse it—extract your name, email, work history, education, skills

  2. It stores this in database fields (like a spreadsheet)

  3. Recruiters search or filter these fields

The key insight: most ATSs are not rejecting you automatically. They’re just making you searchable.

Wait, so there’s no “resume killer robot”?

Not exactly. The robot isn’t deciding—it’s filing. But if it files you incorrectly (puts your email in the “skills” field, or can’t read your work history because you used a fancy two-column layout), then when a recruiter searches for “Python,” your resume won’t show up. Even though “Python” is right there on your resume.

You’re not rejected. You’re just invisible.

Okay, so which ATS systems are actually out there?

Three main ones, each with a different personality:

Workday: The Strict Professor

Used by: Large enterprises (Fortune 500), companies with 200+ employees

What it’s like: Imagine a professor who only accepts papers in 12-point Times New Roman, double-spaced, with 1-inch margins. Workday is an ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) system first, recruiting tool second. It’s designed to feed data directly into payroll and benefits systems.

It demands:

  • Standard section headers only: “Experience,” “Education,” “Skills” (not “My Professional Journey”)

  • Dates in MM/YYYY format

  • No tables, no columns, no text boxes, no headers/footers

  • Contact info in the body of the document

If you put your email in a header, Workday might not see it. If you use a two-column layout, it might read left-to-right and merge both columns into gibberish.

Greenhouse: The Smart Assistant

Used by: Tech startups, mid-size companies (50-500 employees)

What it’s like: Greenhouse uses multiple fine-tuned AI models to extract data. One model identifies skills, another recognizes dates, another parses job titles. It’s more forgiving of formatting because it’s actually trying to understand your resume, not just pattern-match.

But here’s the catch: Greenhouse is obsessed with keywords. When a recruiter searches “Python AND Machine Learning,” your resume better have both terms explicitly. The system can infer some things (it knows “K8s” = “Kubernetes”), but it’s not magic.

Also: Greenhouse shows recruiters your original PDF alongside the extracted data. So design still matters, just less than content.

Lever: The Middle Ground

Used by: Startups in growth mode (50-200 employees)

What it’s like: Better parsing than old systems, some AI assistance, but not as heavy-duty as Greenhouse. Most startups choose Lever because it’s affordable and emphasizes relationship-building. The ATS isn’t trying to filter you out—it’s helping the recruiter manage their pipeline.

None: The Founder’s Inbox

Used by: Companies under 50 people, research labs, early-stage startups

What it’s like: Your resume goes to an email address or a basic web form. A human reads it. That’s it.

Here’s the important part:

Of your 50,000+ target companies in the 80 Days to Stay database:

  • ~70-80% (small companies): No ATS or basic forms

  • ~15-20% (mid-size): Lever, Ashby, or basic Greenhouse

  • ~5-10% (large): Workday, enterprise Greenhouse

So I’m optimizing for a problem most companies don’t have?

Bingo. If you spend hours making your resume “ATS-proof” for Workday, you’re solving for maybe 10% of your targets.

For the other 90%, the human wants to see your work, not count how many times you said “leveraged.”

The Sponsorship Propensity Score

Okay, so how do I actually decide which companies to target first?

You calculate a sponsorship propensity score. It’s like a dating app match percentage, but for visa sponsorship.

Here’s the logic:

Let me break that down in English:

Recent Filings (40% of score):

  • 5+ LCA filings in last 3 years → 50 points

  • 3-4 filings → 40 points

  • 1-2 filings → 25 points

  • 0 filings → 0 points

Why this matters most: If they’ve sponsored 5 people in the last 3 years, sponsorship is routine for them. They have a process. They have contacts at immigration law firms. They know the timeline.

Historical Filings (20% of score):

  • 10+ total filings ever → 20 points

  • 1-9 filings → 10 points

  • 0 filings → 0 points

Why this matters: Even if they haven’t filed recently, institutional knowledge persists. Maybe the HR person who handled visas is still there. Maybe the company founder is from another country and gets it.

Funding Recency (30% of score):

  • Raised money in 2024-2025 → 15 points

  • Raised in 2022-2023 → 10 points

  • Raised before 2022 → 5 points

Why this matters: Fresh capital means they’re hiring right now. If they raised $15M six months ago, they’re probably in “aggressive growth mode.”

Trend (10% of score - bonus):

  • Filings increasing year-over-year → +10 points

  • Stable → 0 points

  • Decreasing → -5 points

Example calculation:

Company A: BioStartup Inc

  • 8 LCA filings in last 3 years → 50 points (40% weight) = 20

  • 12 total filings historically → 20 points (20% weight) = 4

  • Raised $15M in 2024 → 15 points (30% weight) = 4.5

  • Trend: Increasing → 10 points (10% weight) = 1

Total: 29.5 out of 30 points possible ≈ 98% match

This should be your first application.

Company B: NewBio Therapeutics

  • 0 recent filings → 0 points

  • 0 historical filings → 0 points

  • Raised $20M in 2025 → 15 points (30% weight) = 4.5

  • No trend data → 0 points

Total: 4.5 out of 30 ≈ 15% match

This is a long shot. Apply only after exhausting higher-scoring targets.

Dizzy again. So I’m literally sorting companies by math?

Yes. And the math reflects reality. Companies that have sponsored 8 people in the last 3 years are objectively more likely to sponsor you than companies that have never filed.

The traditional approach is: apply to 200 companies and hope. This approach is: apply to 50 companies ranked by evidence.

The Resume Strategy by Company Tier

Alright, I’ve scored my companies. Now what do I actually put on my resume?

It depends on the tier. Remember: everything on your resume is factually identical—same projects, same skills. You’re just changing emphasis and order.

For Tier 1 (Proven Recent Sponsors):

These companies KNOW what OPT is. They’ve hired people like you before.

Your visa status should be prominent and confident:

JOHN SMITH
john@northeastern.edu | (617) 555-0123 | Boston, MA
F-1 OPT: Work authorized through May 2027 (no sponsorship required)
GitHub: github.com/jsmith | Portfolio: jsmith.dev

Notice: you’re not apologizing or explaining. You’re stating a fact. This company has filed 8 LCAs in 3 years—they know what this means.

Your projects section should mirror their sponsored job titles:

If their LCA history shows they’ve sponsored “Bioinformatics Engineer” and “Research Scientist,” and you’re applying for a bioinformatics role, lead with your bioinformatics project. Even if your other projects are equally impressive, this is what they’ve hired before.

Format: Clean and professional, but you can have some personality. If they use Lever or no ATS, a well-designed PDF is fine. If they use Greenhouse, keep it simple but readable.

For Tier 2 (Former Sponsors):

These companies have done it before, but it’s been a while. They need a gentle reminder.

Your visa status should be clear but explanatory:

JOHN SMITH
john@northeastern.edu | (617) 555-0123
F-1 OPT — work authorized through May 2027 (sponsorship not required for 2-3 years)

You’re adding context because they might have forgotten the OPT timeline.

Your projects should emphasize current relevance over historical match. They haven’t sponsored since 2020—their tech stack might have changed. Focus on what they’re hiring for now, not what they hired for 5 years ago.

Format: Conservative if they use Greenhouse or Workday. If no ATS detected, you have more flexibility.

For Tier 3 (Never Sponsored):

These companies need education. You’re not just applying for a job—you’re teaching them something about their own hiring options.

Your visa status should lead with benefits:

JOHN SMITH
john@northeastern.edu | (617) 555-0123
F-1 OPT: Already work-authorized through May 2027
- No sponsorship required for 2-3 years
- FICA-exempt (employer saves ~7.65% in payroll taxes vs. US citizen)

Wait, what’s the FICA thing?

The FICA Exemption

Students on F-1 visas are exempt from FICA taxes (Social Security + Medicare), which are 7.65% of salary. That’s 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare.

Here’s the math:

  • Salary: $80,000/year

  • FICA for US citizen: $80,000 × 0.0765 = $6,120 (employer pays half, employee pays half)

  • FICA for F-1 student: $0

The employer saves ~$3,000/year by hiring you over a US citizen with identical skills. Most startups don’t know this.

For Tier 3 companies, your resume should link to everything:

  • Every project title is a hyperlink to GitHub

  • Include a portfolio URL

  • Add a LinkedIn that shows consistent history

Why? Because these companies are skeptical. They’ve never sponsored anyone. They’re thinking, “Is this person real? Can they actually do the work?” Links to verifiable projects answer that question instantly.

Format: Human-first. These companies probably don’t use an ATS. Your resume should be readable and interesting, not keyword-stuffed and boring.

The Cover Letter Strategy

Do I need a cover letter?

For Tier 1 and small companies: yes, if you want to stand out.

For Tier 3: absolutely yes. You’re educating them.

For applications through giant corporate ATSs: probably not—many don’t even have a field for it.

What do I say?

Tier 1 Cover Letter (Proven Recent Sponsors):

Subject: MS Bioinformatics → Research Scientist position

Dr. [Hiring Manager],

I saw that BioStartup has successfully sponsored several bioinformatics 
researchers in the past 3 years, including a Research Scientist in August 
2024. This suggests you value international talent and understand the visa

I'm currently on F-1 OPT (work authorized through May 2027), so no

I'll have proven my value to the team.

[Then: 2-3 sentences about your relevant technical work, linking to GitHub]

Would you be open to a brief call to discuss how I could contribute?

Best,
[Your Name]

What you’re doing: Acknowledging they already know how this works. You’re not explaining OPT—you’re showing you researched them.

Tier 2 Cover Letter (Former Sponsors):

Subject: Computational Biologist role at DataTech

Hi [Name],

I noticed DataTech previously worked with international talent (LCA filings 
2018-2020) and just raised $12M to scale your platform. Congratulations on

[Then: Technical pitch with links]

What you’re doing: Reminding them they’ve done this before + gently educating about current benefits they might have forgotten.

Tier 3 Cover Letter (Never Sponsored):

I'm reaching out about the Bioinformatics Engineer role. I saw NewBio just

Quick note that might be helpful: I'm on F-1 OPT, which is work

- I'm already authorized to work through May 2027 (no sponsorship needed now)
- I'm FICA-exempt (you save ~$3,000/year in payroll taxes at typical salaries)
- H-1B isn't needed for 2-3 years, giving time to prove my value
- If/when H-1B is needed, it's straightforward (~$5K-$10K in fees)

Many biotech startups don't realize OPT exists or how employer-friendly it is.
I know visa sponsorship can feel complicated, but OPT removes that barrier 
for the first 2-3 years of employment.

[Then: Technical pitch, heavy on portfolio links]

What you’re doing: Proactively answering their objections. You’re teaching them that what they thought was “impossible” is actually easy.

When ATS Actually Matters

Okay, so when do I need to care about keyword optimization and Workday parsing?

Here’s the decision tree:

If the company is Tier 1 or Tier 2 (proven or former sponsors):

Check the ATS:

  • Workday detected: Use conservative formatting. Contact info in body, no tables/columns, standard headers, dates in MM/YYYY.

  • Greenhouse detected: Clean formatting, but focus on keywords. If the job description says “Python, React, PostgreSQL,” those exact terms need to appear in your resume.

  • Lever/Ashby detected: Balanced approach. Clean but not paranoid.

  • No ATS detected: Human-first formatting. Portfolio links prominent.

If the company is Tier 3 (never sponsored):

Assume they’re reading manually. ATS optimization is irrelevant. Instead:

  • Make your resume interesting

  • Link to everything

  • Show, don’t tell

The reality check:

Of your 50,000 target companies:

  • ~40,000 (80%) don’t use enterprise ATS

  • ~8,000 (16%) use Lever/Greenhouse

  • ~2,000 (4%) use Workday

You should have one Workday-safe resume template for that 4%. For everyone else, optimize for humans.

The Application Math

How many applications should I send?

Traditional advice: “Apply to 100-200 companies.”

That’s burn-out territory.

Better approach: Target 30-50 companies, sorted by sponsorship score.

Here’s the expected math:

Tier 1 companies (score 80-100):

  • Apply to: 15-20 companies

  • Expected response rate: ~30-40%

  • Expected phone screens: 5-8

Tier 2 companies (score 50-79):

  • Apply to: 10-15 companies

  • Expected response rate: ~15-25%

  • Expected phone screens: 2-4

Tier 3 companies (score 0-49):

  • Apply to: 5-10 companies (only if you have energy left)

  • Expected response rate: ~5-10%

  • Expected phone screens: 0-1

Total: 30-45 applications → 7-13 phone screens

Compare to traditional approach:

  • 100 blind applications → 3-5 phone screens (if you’re lucky)

You’re 3x more efficient because you’re targeting based on evidence, not hope.

The Surprising Truth About Resumes

One last question: If I do all this—tier scoring, ATS optimization, custom cover letters—will I definitely get a job?

No. Because resumes aren’t the bottleneck anymore.

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: in 2026, everyone’s resume looks good. AI tools (including the one you’re talking to right now) can polish any resume to perfection. Hiring managers know this. They’re skeptical of polished resumes because they’ve seen a thousand ChatGPT-generated applications.

What does matter:

  1. Targeting the right companies (this is what 80 Days to Stay solves)

  2. Provable work (GitHub, portfolio, live demos)

  3. Network effects (referrals, warm intros)

  4. Technical skill (you still need to pass the interview)

The resume’s job isn’t to prove you’re qualified. It’s to get you into the room where you can prove it.

And if you’re applying to Tier 1 companies—companies that have sponsored 5-10 people in the last 3 years—you’re not competing against 500 applicants anymore. You’re competing against maybe 50, because most students don’t know those companies exist.

That’s the real advantage: You found companies that want to hire people like you, but are invisible to everyone else.

So the resume is just... the entry ticket?

Exactly. 80 Days to Stay gives you the right venues to show that ticket. The ATS knowledge helps you not get accidentally filtered out. But the actual game is played after you get the phone screen.

Your resume gets you seen. Your projects get you respected. Your interview performance gets you hired.

The database just makes sure you’re knocking on doors that actually open.
