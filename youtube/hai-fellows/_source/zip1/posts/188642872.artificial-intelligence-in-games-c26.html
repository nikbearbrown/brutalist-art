PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

CHAPTER 1: Introduction

Core Claim: Game AI and academic AI share a toolkit but pursue fundamentally different goals—game AI optimizes for believability, fun, and real-time performance; academic AI optimizes for intelligence and novelty.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Game AI must maintain 60fps, which imposes hard computational constraints that academic AI does not face

  • The “illusion of intelligence” is defined as AI that is believable to the specific player, regardless of underlying sophistication

  • The sniper game anecdote: enemies exposing themselves while a sniper is present demonstrates the tension between AI cleverness and behavioral plausibility

  • The COD: Strike Team example shows how camera perspective changes player expectations and therefore the requirements of the AI

Logical Method: Definitional differentiation followed by a three-constraint framework (believability, fun, framerate) with a proposed problem-reduction methodology (reduce, spread, or remove).

Logical Gaps:

  • The “illusion of intelligence” claim is stated as a design goal but never operationalized. How do you measure whether a player “buys in”? The author never specifies a falsifiable criterion.

  • The assertion that neural networks are “not as reliable as you might think” in games is stated without evidence. This framing colors the entire book’s treatment of deep learning but rests on assertion rather than demonstration.

  • The table mapping AI techniques to game genres (Image 1.1) is presented as a design reference, but the criteria for inclusion are unstated. These are the author’s professional judgments, not derived from systematic analysis.

Methodological Soundness: This chapter functions as advocacy framing. The distinctions drawn are pedagogically useful but should be read as practitioner heuristics, not formal claims.

CHAPTER 2: Math

Core Claim: The mathematical foundations needed for game AI—vectors, magnitude, normalization, dot product, rotation—can be derived from first principles and provide a complete basis for subsequent chapters.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Pythagoras’ theorem grounds magnitude calculation directly

  • The dot product derivation proceeds from definition through the simplified unit-vector form

  • The left/right turn problem is resolved by introducing the right-facing vector and interpreting the sign of the dot product result

  • Rotation around an arbitrary point is shown as: local offset = offset − rotation point → rotate the offset → add rotation point back

Logical Method: Worked examples with explicit numerical values at each step. The logical chain is: definition → formula → example → interpretation → edge case.

Logical Gaps:

  • The book states that “in games programming, this is usually a step we do not need or want to do” regarding square roots. This is an efficiency heuristic presented as near-universal practice. Some applications—particularly steering forces and normalized velocities—explicitly require the actual magnitude, not the squared value. The blanket claim overstates.

  • The unit circle explanation correctly establishes that the dot product alone cannot resolve directionality but introduces the right-facing vector without discussing how this vector is maintained as the agent rotates. This is a significant implementation dependency left implicit.

Methodological Soundness: Among the strongest chapters logically. The mathematical derivations are correct and the worked examples verify the formulas. The transition from theory to practical application (VectorMath.cs) is clean.

CHAPTER 3: Steering Behaviours

Core Claim: Complex agent movement is built by combining simple, single-purpose force-generating behaviors, prioritized by importance, rather than writing monolithic behaviors.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Each behavior (Seek, Flee, Arrive, Wander, Collision Avoidance, Evasion, Intercept) is defined as a force vector with clear mathematical derivation

  • The maximum force accumulation with early-exit pruning prevents combined forces from producing physically implausible movements

  • Flocking (Reynolds, 1987) is decomposed into three independently implementable forces—Separation, Alignment, Cohesion

  • The priority order (avoidance > flocking > seek) reflects a design principle: constraint satisfaction before goal pursuit

Logical Method: Bottom-up compositional design. Each behavior is specified formally, then the composition rules are specified separately. This is methodologically sound for reusability.

Logical Gaps:

  • Arrive uses a deceleration scalar multiplied against the vector-to-target magnitude, but the relationship between fDECELERATION = 0.1f and actual stopping distance is never characterized. A practitioner needs to know: at what maximum speed will the agent overshoot? This is derivable but left unaddressed.

  • Collision Avoidance calculates repulsion from the obstacle center, not from the whisker penetration vector. The author states this prevents counteracting the seek force. True—but it also means agents treat obstacles as infinite repellers (force scaled by penetration, direction from center), which can produce orbiting behavior around convex obstacles. This failure mode is not discussed.

  • The Wander behavior generates a random direction and projects it ahead, but the radius-must-be-smaller-than-projected-distance constraint is mentioned only once without explaining what happens when violated. The consequence (Wander positions behind the agent, producing erratic reversal) is hinted at but not demonstrated.

  • Flocking’s neighborhood FOV check (dot product > −0.5) is presented as a fixed design choice. The sensitivity of flocking behavior to this threshold is not explored.

Methodological Soundness: Adequate for teaching. The behaviors are correctly specified. The composition framework is well-reasoned. Edge cases are present but underexplored.

CHAPTER 4: Terrain Analysis

Core Claim: Influence maps and heat maps provide AI agents with spatially distributed world-state data that enables context-sensitive, terrain-aware decision-making without per-agent full-world queries.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Influence maps are demonstrated with a concrete propagation model (drop-off = 0.5, cutoff = 0.1) producing visually verifiable gradients

  • Opposing-force influence subtraction creates a signed map that identifies control zones and a “frontier”

  • Heat maps are demonstrated with a dungeon navigation example showing real-time obstacle avoidance through gradient following

  • The alien swarm project (Chapter 4 practical) demonstrates emergent path formation—an unintended collective coordination arising from individual pheromone-like heat modification

Logical Method: The chapter builds from a single-unit example through multi-force integration, then pivots to heat maps as a distinct tool. The alien swarm is used to illustrate emergence without defining it formally until after the practical section.

Logical Gaps:

  • The drop-off function (multiply by 0.5 per cell) is presented as an “example” but no guidance is given for selecting drop-off values for different game scenarios. The relationship between unit strength, drop-off rate, area of influence, and strategic AI behavior is left as practitioner intuition.

  • The claim that influence maps “can be used in any game that requires some form of terrain analysis” is too broad. For games with highly dynamic environments or large numbers of agents requiring individual queries, the processing cost and staleness of shared influence maps can become prohibitive. The chapter acknowledges refresh rate issues but treats them as implementation details rather than architectural trade-offs.

  • The heat map dungeon example is highly intuitive, but the agent’s decision rule—”move to the hottest neighboring cell”—can produce local maxima traps in sparse environments. This failure mode appears in the alien swarm practical (where an alien may become stuck if all directions score zero) and is addressed with a fallback, but the general vulnerability is not stated as a known limitation of the approach.

Methodological Soundness: Adequate. The conceptual models are sound. The practical section (alien swarm emergence) is one of the book’s most compelling demonstrations because it shows an unplanned system-level behavior arising from simple rules.

CHAPTER 5: Pathfinding

Core Claim: A* is the dominant game pathfinding algorithm because it produces reasonably optimal paths quickly enough for real-time use; its quality and speed are primarily determined by spatial representation choice, not algorithmic tuning.

Supporting Evidence:

  • The step-by-step A* walkthrough demonstrates 11 iterations from S=(0,0) to T=(4,10) with explicit OPEN and CLOSED list states

  • Manhattan and Euclidean heuristics are compared: Euclidean consistently underestimates, resulting in fewer expanded nodes but identical final paths

  • Four spatial representations (grid, waypoint, navmesh, object-corner) are described with explicit tradeoffs between authoring cost, node count, path quality, and processing time

  • The final path (Image 5.36) shows that unexplored branches would have been searched only if the main path were blocked—demonstrating the algorithm’s directed nature

Logical Method: The chapter makes the unusual pedagogical choice of an explicit trace-through at the iteration level. This is excellent for building intuition but means the complexity discussion is confined to the “Final Thoughts” section and is comparatively thin.

Logical Gaps:

  • The core claim that “spatial representation has a major impact upon performance” is illustrated qualitatively but not quantitatively. What is the typical node-count ratio between grid and waypoint approaches for the same environment? The London-to-Edinburgh train analogy gestures at orders of magnitude but provides no reference figures.

  • The A* algorithm as presented assumes static environments. The chapter acknowledges D* (dynamic A*) in the algorithm list but provides no discussion of how A* fails in dynamic environments or when replanning is necessary. This gap is significant—most game environments change at runtime.

  • The chapter concludes that pathfinding “is all about compromise” between quality and speed but does not provide a decision framework. When should a designer choose a grid vs. waypoints? The tradeoffs are listed; the selection criteria are not.

  • AlphaBeta pruning is mentioned for Chess (Chapter 8) as a search optimization; no analogous optimization techniques for pathfinding (e.g., bidirectional A*, Jump Point Search) are discussed despite being named in the introduction.

Methodological Soundness: Adequate for the core algorithm. The worked example is rigorous and useful. The surrounding context (alternatives, dynamic environments, selection criteria) is insufficient for production application.

CHAPTER 6: Decision-Making

Core Claim: Finite State Machines, their variants (Fuzzy, Stack-based, Hierarchical), and Behavior Trees provide a spectrum of decision-making architectures; the correct choice depends on the complexity of the AI’s behavioral requirements, not on using the most sophisticated approach.

Supporting Evidence:

  • The Pac-Man ghost FSM provides a canonical example with clearly defined states (CHASE, EVADE, AT_HOME, EATEN, MOVE_TO_HOME)

  • The Stack-based FSM is shown to solve the memory problem: after being eaten, a ghost correctly resumes EVADE if the power pill is still active

  • Behavior Trees address FSM limitations (predictability, single-state constraint) through composable, reusable nodes

  • The practical section implements both an FSM version and a Behavior Tree version of the ghost AI for direct comparison

Logical Method: The chapter uses escalating complexity—each approach is motivated by the limitations of the previous one. This is a pedagogically effective rhetorical structure.

Logical Gaps:

  • The Fuzzy State Machine (FuSM) section is presented as a solution to the tank problem (multiple simultaneous states) but receives the least development of any variant. The claim that it “can be tricky to balance and achieve the desired behaviours” is the entire critical analysis. For an architecture with significant implementation complexity, this is inadequate.

  • The Behavior Tree’s key advantage—modularity and reusability—is stated but not demonstrated. The ghost example shows a working tree, but no example shows reuse of a subtree across different agents. This is the most consequential claim about BTs and it remains illustrative rather than proven.

  • The leaf-node duration system in the practical (Leaf_MoveToPlayer runs for kMaxDuration = 10.0f seconds before returning SUCCESS) embeds a fixed time constant that will behave differently depending on game simulation speed. The chapter does not discuss time-scaling or the relationship between leaf duration and AI responsiveness.

  • The “Final Thoughts” section correctly advises “keep it simple” but provides no criteria for selecting among FSM, FuSM, Stack-based FSM, HFSM, and Behavior Trees. The advice reduces to: use FSM unless you need more, then use BT.

Methodological Soundness: Adequate. The chapter teaches the architectures correctly. The comparison is structured but informal—no evaluation criteria, no counterexamples, no failure modes for the Behavior Tree approach.

CHAPTER 7: Fuzzy Logic

Core Claim: Fuzzy Logic eliminates the arbitrary boundary problem in crisp categorical decisions by allowing partial set membership, producing more natural decision-making for problems with continuous input spaces.

Supporting Evidence:

  • The water pistol example demonstrates that crisp boundaries produce logically absurd edge cases (5.00m vs. 5.01m)

  • The Speedball example builds a complete three-input, one-output fuzzy system from expert heuristics through rules through defuzzification

  • Three defuzzification methods (Highest Membership Mean, Geometric Centroid, Averaged Degree of Membership) are compared, with the centroid explicitly identified as most accurate but most computationally expensive

  • The practical section correctly identifies that defuzzification is unnecessary when only the highest-scoring output set is needed—a legitimate optimization

Logical Method: The chapter moves carefully from the failure of crisp sets → fuzzy membership functions → fuzzy operators → rule tables → defuzzification. Each step is motivated by the inadequacy of the previous one. This is among the book’s most logically structured chapters.

Logical Gaps:

  • The Speedball expert testimony (”If I’m under lots of pressure...”) is treated as valid input to the system, but the chapter does not acknowledge that expert knowledge elicitation is itself a methodology with known failure modes (experts poorly articulate tacit knowledge; rules derived from stated preferences often conflict with revealed preferences in practice).

  • The “we do not defuzzify the results” choice in the artillery practical is pragmatically justified but not theoretically grounded. The chapter does not explain under what conditions skipping defuzzification introduces bias versus when it is genuinely equivalent.

  • The rule table methodology (all 27 rules from 3×3×3 inputs) is presented as the preferred complete approach, but this approach scales as a product of set counts—three inputs with five sets each would require 125 rules. The exponential scaling problem is not discussed.

  • The geometric centroid calculation is described as “expensive” without any specific claim about computational cost relative to the artillery game’s update requirements. This leaves the practitioner without actionable guidance on when to use each defuzzification method.

Methodological Soundness: This is the book’s strongest theoretical chapter. The fuzzy logic formalism is correctly presented. The Speedball worked example is thorough. The identified gaps are genuine but do not undermine the core pedagogy.

CHAPTER 8: Chess AI

Core Claim: Minimax with AlphaBeta pruning provides a complete framework for adversarial game tree search; the quality of Chess AI is primarily determined by the evaluation function’s sophistication, not the search algorithm’s depth.

Supporting Evidence:

  • The Minimax pseudocode is presented formally with explicit recursion

  • AlphaBeta pruning is demonstrated to produce identical results with fewer node evaluations, using the same game tree shown for Minimax

  • Four evaluation components are demonstrated: piece values, board positioning bonuses, mobility (moves available), and attack availability

  • The book acknowledges that Deep Blue uses approximately 8,000 evaluation heuristics, framing the presented system as a minimal viable evaluation

Logical Method: The chapter presents the theoretical algorithm, then derives AlphaBeta as an optimization, then addresses the evaluation function as the domain-specific component. This is the correct logical ordering.

Logical Gaps:

  • The claim that AlphaBeta pruning “will find the shortest route relatively quickly” describes Minimax+AlphaBeta but understates its practical limitations in Chess. With average branching factor ~35, searching 4 moves ahead requires evaluating approximately 35^4 = 1.5M nodes (before pruning). The chapter notes the AI “will take a very long time to make decisions” in the practical but does not quantify the time or suggest optimization strategies beyond the brief “Future Work” list.

  • The evaluation function components (piece value, position, mobility, attacks) are presented as additive. The chapter does not discuss interaction effects or weighting—should mobility be weighted equally to piece value? The worked examples use implicit equal weighting without justification.

  • The board positioning bonuses (central squares = +5) are stated as design choices without grounding in Chess theory or citation. This is a legitimate simplification for pedagogy, but presenting it as a general principle rather than one possible parameterization is misleading.

  • Playbooks (opening databases) are mentioned in Final Thoughts but not implemented in the practical. The chapter notes that real opening theory should respond to opponent moves, but the practical implementation uses “a random playbook” that ignores opponent responses—a significant limitation that is acknowledged but not resolved.

Methodological Soundness: The algorithm presentation is rigorous. The evaluation function section is more prescriptive than the rest of the book and would benefit from explicit acknowledgment that the presented weightings are illustrative, not optimal.

CHAPTER 9: Genetic Algorithms

Core Claim: Genetic algorithms are a search and optimization technique that can evolve solutions to problems where the correct answer is unknown in advance; they are most appropriate for game development use and testing rather than real-time runtime AI.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Five selection mechanisms are presented (Tournament, Roulette Wheel, Stochastic, Rank, Elitist) with visual demonstrations

  • Three crossover approaches (1-point, n-point, uniform) are shown with explicit bit-level diagrams

  • The chapter explicitly states that genetic algorithms are inappropriate for released titles due to training time but useful for development to find edge cases or evolve NPC behaviors

  • The Final Thoughts section honestly acknowledges: “the solutions evolved may be somewhat unpredictable”

Logical Method: Biological analogy → encoding → fitness → selection → crossover → mutation. Each stage is motivated before being specified. The Royal Battle 1866 example tracks a single population through all five stages, providing continuity across the chapter.

Logical Gaps:

  • The chapter presents the giraffe example as an illustration of natural selection but does not formally define the conditions under which genetic algorithms are expected to converge. Without characterizing the fitness landscape, the claim that “over multiple generations we can evolve to a good solution” is an assertion, not a theorem.

  • The n-point crossover section correctly identifies a design trap: crossing over at a fixed boundary between functional groups prevents intra-group evolution. This insight is valuable but buried. The general principle—that crossover point placement must reflect the independence or dependence structure of the chromosome—deserves explicit treatment.

  • The practical section implements Elitist + Tournament selection but provides no guidance for selecting among the five approaches. The chapter presents them as a menu without a decision framework.

  • “Mutation should be used sparingly” (1–2% of the time) is presented as a general rule without citation or derivation. The appropriate mutation rate depends on chromosome length, population size, selection pressure, and fitness landscape topology—none of which are discussed.

Methodological Soundness: Adequate. The chapter correctly implements and explains the algorithm. The evolutionary analogy is maintained throughout without being misleading. The absence of convergence criteria is the primary theoretical gap.

CHAPTER 10: Neural Networks

Core Claim: Neural networks can learn to play games through genetic algorithm-evolved weights; back propagation is an alternative when desired outputs are known, but for games the GA approach is preferred because game outcomes are not predetermined.

Supporting Evidence:

  • The XOR gate problem is used to demonstrate that single-layer perceptrons cannot solve non-linearly separable problems, motivating multi-layer architecture

  • A complete 12-input, 4-hidden-node, 4-output network is designed for SNAKE with explicit input semantics (movement validity, pick-up distance, collision distance)

  • The chapter honestly states that neural networks are “not a particularly useful approach to use for game AI” due to unpredictability

  • The practical implements all 192 weights as a GA chromosome, connecting Chapter 9’s framework to the network architecture

Logical Method: Biology → single-layer perceptron → multi-layer perceptron → learning (back prop vs GA) → game application. The XOR gate worked example is a pedagogical masterstroke—a case where the failure of the simpler model is provable.

Logical Gaps:

  • The chapter treats the architecture (4 hidden nodes, 1 hidden layer) as sufficient for SNAKE without addressing how to select the right architecture. “Start small and increase” is the only guidance. For practitioners, the absence of a structured approach to architecture search is the main practical gap in neural network deployment.

  • The 12 input semantics conflate information types: movement validity (binary), pick-up distance (0-1 continuous), and collision distance (0-1 continuous) have different measurement scales and sensitivities. The chapter notes that inputs of different scales “should be brought into line” but the collision and pick-up inputs are not normalized to the same reference frame—collision distance uses kSnakeBoardDimensions as a denominator while pick-up distance uses the same value but in a different geometric context (direction-filtered vs. omnidirectional).

  • Back propagation is described procedurally but not demonstrated with a worked example. Given that back prop is the more widely applicable supervised learning method, the disparity in treatment (full worked example for XOR via manual weights; no worked example for back prop) leaves a significant gap.

  • The chapter’s conclusion—that neural networks are not useful for game AI—is stated as settled practitioner wisdom but represents one practitioner’s view circa 2023. The rise of large language models and reinforcement learning in game contexts (OpenAI Five, AlphaStar) suggests the claim requires qualification by application domain.

Methodological Soundness: Adequate for the stated pedagogical goal. The XOR derivation and SNAKE architecture are well-chosen. The claims about neural networks’ game utility should be read as bounded to behavior-scripting applications, not as universal claims about AI in games.

BRIDGE: Synthesizing the Logical Architecture

The book’s central argument is a hierarchy: computation constrains design, which constrains implementation, which determines player experience. Every chapter flows from this: you cannot use expensive algorithms (constraint), so you must design cleverly (affordance), so you must implement efficiently (method), so players will enjoy the game (outcome). This is an intellectually coherent framework that the author maintains consistently.

Three tensions run through the entire book:

Tension 1: Pedagogical clarity vs. production readiness. The book consistently simplifies—fixed constants where adaptive parameters are needed, single-environment demonstrations where multi-environment validation is appropriate, small-n examples where full-scale complexity would change the design calculus. This is appropriate for a teaching text but is never explicitly flagged. Students who proceed directly to production application will encounter failure modes the book does not prepare them for.

Tension 2: The “keep it simple” prescription vs. the complexity of the practical implementations. The Chapter 6 “Final Thoughts” advises against Behavior Trees unless necessary; Chapter 6’s practical implements a full Behavior Tree. The Chapter 10 conclusion advises against neural networks; Chapter 10’s practical implements a GA-evolved neural network. The tension between the prescriptive advice and the demonstrated content is real and unresolved.

Tension 3: Game AI as craft vs. game AI as engineering. The book presents game AI as experienced craft knowledge—”we always use A*,” “neural networks aren’t reliable,” “keep it simple”—without providing the theoretical basis for these claims. For topics where these heuristics are genuinely robust (e.g., A* for grid pathfinding), this is acceptable shorthand. For topics where the heuristics are more contested (e.g., neural networks in games), the lack of theoretical grounding produces overconfident claims.

The book’s most proven claims:

  • Vector mathematics is a sufficient basis for all movement-related AI (demonstrated)

  • A* finds near-optimal paths in reasonable time for typical game environments (demonstrated)

  • Behavior Trees are more maintainable than FSMs at scale (argued, not demonstrated)

  • Genetic algorithms are better suited to development-time AI optimization than runtime AI (correctly argued with appropriate caveats)

The book’s most significant unproven claims:

  • Neural networks are “not as reliable” as other approaches in games (asserted without evidence)

  • The “illusion of intelligence” is the correct design target (definitional, not empirical)

  • The presented evaluation function weights for Chess AI produce “good” play (presented without any game-theoretic grounding)

The book’s primary pedagogical achievement: The practical tutorial sections are exceptionally well-structured. The pattern of theory → pseudocode → worked example → guided coding → future work is executed consistently across 10 chapters. Students who complete all tutorials will have a working implementation of every major classical game AI technique—this is not a trivial accomplishment.

PART 2: LITERARY REVIEW ESSAY

The Honest Limit

Every technical textbook contains a silent argument about what matters. Paul Roberts’ Artificial Intelligence in Games contains an unusually honest one. Its thesis—stated plainly in the opening chapter and never abandoned—is that game AI is not real AI. It is the appearance of intelligence, manufactured under tight computational constraints, for the specific purpose of making a game feel alive to the human playing it. Nothing else.

This is a more sophisticated position than it first appears. And its honesty about what game AI isn’t produces, by the end of ten chapters and ten practical tutorials, an unusually useful book—one whose value comes precisely from refusing to oversell itself.

The book opens with a philosophical distinction that deserves to be read carefully. Academic AI, Roberts writes, pursues genuine intelligence. Game AI pursues the “illusion of intelligence.” The difference is not merely technical. It is motivational. Academic AI succeeds when its systems are actually better at something—winning at Go, classifying images, predicting protein structures. Game AI succeeds when a player believes the AI is behaving purposefully, regardless of whether it is. Pac-Man’s ghosts do not reason. They follow deterministic rules. But if those rules produce behavior that a player interprets as tactical, the game AI has succeeded.

This reframing matters because it changes the evaluation criterion for every technique in the book. The question Roberts is perpetually asking is not “is this the best algorithm?” but “does this produce behavior that players will find credible at sixty frames per second?” A* is not used because it is provably optimal among pathfinding algorithms—it is used because it is good enough, fast enough, and understood well enough to debug when it fails. Neural networks are handled with notable skepticism not because Roberts cannot implement them, but because their outputs are unpredictable in a domain where designers require authorial control over agent behavior. Whether or not you agree with his assessment of neural networks in games circa 2023, the reasoning is principled: unpredictability is a design defect when the product is an authored experience.

The strongest chapters—Fuzzy Logic and Chess AI—succeed precisely because they hold this tension without resolving it dishonestly. The Fuzzy Logic chapter correctly establishes that the goal is not perfect mathematical modeling of decision space; it is producing decisions that vary smoothly with continuously changing inputs rather than exhibiting the binary discontinuities that produce absurd edge cases. The real-world benefit is behavioral plausibility, not formal precision. Similarly, the Chess AI chapter is refreshingly direct about the evaluation function’s limitations, acknowledging that Deep Blue uses approximately 8,000 heuristics while the book implements four. The gap is acknowledged as an opportunity for student exploration, not papered over with false confidence.

The book’s logical architecture, however, contains a gap worth examining directly.

The three-constraint framework introduced in Chapter 1—believability, fun, framerate—is intellectually coherent, but Roberts treats it as a complete account of game AI design constraints. It is not. The framework omits predictability as an independent design constraint. This is not the same as believability. A perfectly believable agent (one whose behavior a player can explain) can still be unpredictable in the sense that the designer cannot guarantee what it will do in untested scenarios. The Steering Behaviors chapter briefly surfaces this concern through the collision avoidance orbiting failure mode (unmentioned but implicit in the repulsion logic). The Genetic Algorithms chapter surfaces it explicitly when Roberts acknowledges that “the solutions evolved may be somewhat unpredictable.” But the book never integrates predictability into its framework, leaving a significant design constraint perpetually in the margins.

This matters for the neural networks conclusion. Roberts argues that neural networks are unsuitable for game AI because their outputs are unpredictable. This is a valid claim in the context he describes—hand-authored agent behavior for designed experiences. But the argument conflates two different kinds of unpredictability: outputs the designer cannot anticipate and outputs that will surprise the player in ways that break immersion. These are not the same thing. A Behavior Tree with hundreds of nodes and complex condition logic can produce outcomes the designer fails to anticipate; a simple neural network controller for a specific locomotion task may produce highly consistent behavior that players experience as natural. The framework does not give Roberts the conceptual tools to make this distinction, and the chapter suffers for it.

The practical tutorial sections are this book’s genuine achievement, and they deserve acknowledgment as such.

Across ten chapters, Roberts maintains a consistent pattern: theoretical framework → mathematical derivation → code walkthrough → extensions. Each chapter’s practical builds on a single example game that isolates the chapter’s core technique, runs correctly out of the box before the student adds code, and provides solution files to compare against. The step-by-step guides are written with the patience of someone who has watched students fail at predictable points and engineered those failure modes out of the material. The zombie shopping mall (Chapter 3), the alien swarm (Chapter 4), the Pac-Man implementation (Chapter 6), the artillery tank (Chapter 7), the snake (Chapter 10)—each is memorable, each isolates a specific technique, and each produces visible behavior that validates the student’s implementation.

This is harder to execute than it looks. The worked examples in Chapters 2 and 5—the dot product derivation and the A* trace-through—are pedagogical models. The A* trace in particular, eleven iterations with explicit OPEN and CLOSED list states at each step, is the kind of content that separates textbooks from tutorials. Most authors would skip it. Roberts commits to it. The result is twelve pages of dense, accurate, genuinely useful material that could not be produced by someone who had not implemented the algorithm repeatedly and taught it to students who were confused at specific steps.

The alien swarm emergence in Chapter 4 stands out as the book’s most intellectually interesting demonstration. The emergent path formation—aliens collectively developing routes to food sources without any individual being programmed to cooperate—was not explicitly designed into the system. It arises from the interaction of individual heat-marking behaviors with the heat-following return logic. Roberts notes this honestly: “a behaviour observed in an agent that is not coded for is termed ‘emergent’.” That a textbook example produces genuine emergent behavior, however simple, is not a trivial pedagogical outcome.

Where does this leave the book at the end of ten chapters?

Roberts has built a working toolkit for classical game AI. Every technique covered—steering, pathfinding, decision-making, fuzzy logic, adversarial search, evolutionary computation, neural networks—is presented with sufficient theoretical grounding for implementation and sufficient worked example for debugging. The book delivers exactly what it promises: a grounding in game AI techniques for students and aspiring games programmers.

What it does not deliver is a framework for extending these techniques into more complex scenarios. The gaps are consistent: no dynamic pathfinding, no multi-agent coordination beyond flocking, no treatment of perception and memory as system-level concerns, no discussion of AI debugging methodologies for emergent failures, no engagement with contemporary machine learning approaches beyond the pedagogically conservative treatment of Chapter 10. These are not criticisms of the book as it is; they are observations about the boundary of its ambition.

The book’s most important implicit claim is its scope constraint. Roberts does not attempt to cover reinforcement learning, planning systems, or modern game AI architectures. He covers the classical toolkit. For a student who intends to implement AI in a small-to-medium game, this toolkit is complete. For a student who intends to understand why major studio games made after 2018 behave as they do, it is a beginning.

That is an honest limit. It is the right limit for this book. And in a field where most textbooks either oversell their coverage or undersell their pedagogy, Roberts’ refusal to claim more than he can deliver is itself a form of intellectual rigor.
