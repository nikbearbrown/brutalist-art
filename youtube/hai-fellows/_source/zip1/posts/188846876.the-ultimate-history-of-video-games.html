There is a sentence buried in Stephen Kent’s The Ultimate History of Video Games that the book never quite recognizes as its own thesis. Toru Iwatani created Pac-Man and received a bonus the book describes, with careful delicacy, as “less than $3,500.” Ralph Baer invented the home video game console. Nolan Bushnell became the father of video games. Al Alcorn designed the segmented paddle, the ball acceleration, the distinctive sound—the mechanics that made Pong a product rather than a prototype. Bushnell named the game and built the company. Steve Russell created Space War and asked for nothing, which turned out to be approximately what he received.

Kent reports all of this. He reports it faithfully, across 800 pages and more than 500 interviews, with the discipline of a journalist who has learned that the facts, arranged carefully enough, will make the argument themselves. The problem is that they don’t. Facts arranged without argument produce a different kind of knowledge—wide, detailed, and strangely reluctant to mean anything.

I find myself wondering, reading this book, whether the reluctance is a feature. Whether a history this comprehensive, this scrupulously sourced, would be weakened by the argument it keeps almost making. Or whether the book’s genuine achievement—its extraordinary granularity, its willingness to let contradictory sources stand in unresolved tension—comes at the price of the synthesis it earns but withholds.

The Pattern the Book Won’t Name

The pattern is this: a person of genuine creative ability makes something remarkable. A person of superior commercial ability takes it, improves the packaging, builds a company around it, and becomes famous. The creator receives a modest reward. The entrepreneur becomes the father of the industry.

Kent traces this pattern across three decades. He traces it in the founding of Atari (Bushnell’s idea, Alcorn’s execution, Bushnell’s legend). He traces it in the founding of Activision (Atari’s programmers, Atari’s ownership, Atari’s decision to deny royalties, Activision’s eventual $150M in annual sales). He traces it in Nintendo’s development of the Famicom (Miyamoto’s games, Yamauchi’s platform control, Miyamoto’s creative genius becoming the company’s most valuable asset while the corporate machinery that deployed it became the company’s most powerful force).

The book does not name this pattern. It trusts readers to perceive it. But perception without argument is just mood—and the mood this book produces, for all its rigor, is something close to melancholy without explanation.

The most revealing episode is the founding of Activision, which Kent treats as a story about fair compensation. It is actually a story about something more fundamental: whether the organizational structure that underpaid creative labor was producing optimally. When Alan Miller, David Crane, Bob Whitehead, and Larry Kaplan left Atari, they took their skills and nothing else—Atari owned everything they had made. They started over. Within three years they reached $150 million in annual sales. Ray Kassar’s dismissal (”I can replace them for less”) was correct about the salaries and disastrously wrong about what the salaries were paying for. The Activision case did not prove that the programmers deserved more money, though they did. It proved that programmers who owned their own company massively outperformed programmers who didn’t.

This is the book’s deepest structural finding. Kent notes it and moves on.

What the Courts Understood

The book’s most intellectually disciplined section is its treatment of intellectual property law—nearly a decade of litigation from Magnavox versus Atari through the Alpex patent appeal—and here Kent’s journalistic training serves him best. He presents both sides of every dispute with their strongest arguments. He follows the courts’ reasoning carefully enough to identify where it is sound and where it strains.

The Sega versus Accolade case is particularly well-handled. When Accolade reverse-engineered the Genesis to publish unlicensed games, it was doing exactly what competition law is supposed to protect: entering an adjacent market by understanding how the incumbent’s technology worked. Judge Callfield’s initial ruling for Sega would have made compatibility a platform owner’s exclusive property forever—a monopoly on all software development for any hardware platform. Judge Reinhardt’s appeals court ruling for Accolade understood that such precedent would end the software market as a competitive space. This was the right decision, and Kent explains the reasoning with enough clarity that readers who have never considered fair use doctrine can understand why it mattered.

But the legal chapter’s single most important insight is not a court ruling. It is Ralph Baer’s notebooks.

Baer documented everything. He dated every design decision, sketched every prototype, recorded every meeting. This practice, which his contemporaries did not share, became the decisive factor in every IP dispute he was involved in. Not superior technology. Not prior invention in some abstract historical sense. Documentation. The person who dates their ideas defeats the person who doesn’t.

One imagines Bushnell understanding this lesson too late, standing in a courtroom where Baer’s notebooks established precedent and his own charisma proved inadmissible.

The Contingency That Built Everything

The book’s deepest structural argument emerges in a scene it presents as a near-miss: the 1983 Nintendo-Atari partnership negotiation.

In that year, Minoru Arakawa and Howard Lincoln traveled to Atari headquarters to negotiate a licensing deal. Nintendo would allow Atari to manufacture and sell the Famicom in North America under the Atari label. Ray Kassar was interested. The deal was nearly complete when Arnold Greenberg’s engineers showed Donkey Kong running on the Coleco Adam at the wrong trade show, demonstrating to Atari’s leadership that Nintendo had licensed to a competitor, killing the deal.

Kent presents this as a near-miss. It is actually the book’s strongest argument about how industries are made.

If that deal had closed, there would have been no Nintendo Entertainment System, no Nintendo of America as an independent entity, no Sega Genesis war, and almost certainly no PlayStation—which grew from Kutaragi’s humiliation at Nintendo’s hands, which required Nintendo to be Nintendo. Thirty years of platform history, hundreds of billions of dollars, the careers of everyone from Miyamoto to Bernie Stolar, turn on the fact that one company’s engineers showed a game running on a competitor’s hardware at a trade show in 1983.

This is not a triumph of strategy. It is not an inevitable market outcome. It is a sequence of contingent events that happened to occur in a particular order, producing an industry that came to seem like it could not have happened any other way.

The best platform technology did not always win. The most innovative company did not always survive. The person who became famous was not always the person who did the most important work. Kent’s book makes this argument through accumulation—through the sheer weight of documented evidence—more powerfully than any explicit thesis could. Its reluctance to state the argument directly is the only real failure of nerve in an otherwise meticulously reported work.

What This History Actually Proves

The book’s most honest quality is also its most frustrating: it presents irreconcilable accounts—on the Jobs-Wozniak bonus, on Nintendo’s role in instigating the 1993 Congressional hearings, on the cause of Sega Saturn’s disastrous launch—and declines to adjudicate. The journalist in Kent respects the conflicting testimony. The historian that the book requires him to be needs to make a judgment.

This matters most in the violence hearings chapters, where Kent presents contradictory accounts from Howard Lincoln and Senator Lieberman about who initiated Congressional scrutiny of video game violence. Both cannot be true. One party is either mistaken or lying. The book presents them with equal weight and moves on, which is a form of false balance—the same epistemological error it otherwise carefully avoids.

What the hearings chapters prove, despite themselves, is something about institutional maturity: the industry that faced Congress in 1993 was different from the industry that faced Congress in 1999. The earlier hearings produced the ESRB under genuine political pressure. The later hearings produced relatively little, partly because the industry had learned to speak in the language that legislatures respect—documented evidence, declining youth crime statistics, a self-regulatory apparatus that existed and functioned.

The industry grew up. Whether it grew up honestly is a different question, one the book raises through its documentation of Nintendo’s competitive weaponization of the Congressional process and does not answer.

The final image Kent leaves us with is Isawa Okawa, Sega’s chairman, dying in a hospital and returning $695 million in personal shares to the company he had led out of hardware and into software. Kent treats this as a kind of redemption. Perhaps it was. But it was also an acknowledgment that the hardware era was over—that the companies which had spent thirty years fighting for platform control would spend the next thirty years fighting for something harder to own: attention, loyalty, the willingness of players to return.

The people who actually made the games—who wrote the code, drew the sprites, composed music from 8-bit sound chips—received a smaller share of the rewards than the people who owned the platforms and distribution. This is the evidence Kent’s 800 pages accumulates, steadily and without comment, from first chapter to last.

He reports it. He trusts you to know what it means. You do.
