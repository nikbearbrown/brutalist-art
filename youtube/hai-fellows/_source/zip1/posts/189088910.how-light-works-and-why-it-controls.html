Foundation: What We’re Actually Talking About

light doesn’t travel to your eyes. Light bounces.

A photon leaves a source, hits a surface, loses some energy to that surface’s material properties, and continues to your eye. What you perceive as “the color of that wall” is actually the wall telling you which wavelengths it refused to absorb. Everything you see is rejection data.

This is not metaphor — it’s the physical basis for why lighting design is hard. You’re not painting objects. You’re engineering a transport system for reflected energy, and that system has to survive bouncing off dozens of surfaces before it earns the right to inform anyone’s eye.

From this foundation, two things follow immediately:

First: The same surface looks different under every light, because different lights contain different wavelengths. A red gel on a white wall makes the wall look red. Not because you colored the wall — because you removed every other wavelength before the photon left the source. The wall’s “color” was always conditional.

Second: What you see is never just the direct light. It’s the accumulation of every bounce in the room. Move a lamp three feet and you didn’t just change one highlight — you changed every secondary and tertiary bounce in the scene. This is why lighting a set takes hours and lighting a video game level can take weeks.

Those two facts are the foundation for everything that follows.

Development: Each Concept Follows from the Previous One

From Bouncing Light to Controllable Variables

If light is a transport system, then controlling it means controlling the transport. The industry has settled on a small set of variables that let you do this. They show up identically in cinematography textbooks and in physically based rendering (PBR) frameworks, because both are describing the same physics.

The variables are: color/temperature, intensity, contrast, direction, quality (hard vs soft), shadows, specular highlights, and global illumination. None of these are independent. Change one and you change several others. But they’re useful handles.

Color temperature is measured in Kelvin — not because light is literally that hot, but because we needed a standard way to describe “how orange or how blue” white light appears. 3,000K is candlelight-warm; 6,500K is overcast sky. This matters perceptually because the human visual system adapts to ambient color temperature, and anything that deviates from that adaptation reads as a mood signal. Warm deviations feel intimate. Cool deviations feel clinical. Neither is “correct” — both are communication.

Intensity and contrast work as a pair. Intensity is how much light arrives at a surface. Contrast is the ratio between the lit and unlit areas. High contrast separates objects, creates hierarchy, pulls the eye. Low contrast flattens. A cinematographer controlling fill light is controlling contrast, not intensity — the goal is usually to decide how dark the shadows are allowed to get, not to add more brightness.

Direction is the variable that reveals form. A sphere under top lighting looks like a coin. The same sphere under 45-degree side lighting looks like a planet. Direction rotates our interpretation of three-dimensional shape.

Quality — hard versus soft — follows directly from source size. A small source (bare bulb, direct sun) casts sharp shadows. A large source (overcast sky, bounced light through a diffusion panel) casts soft shadows with gradual transitions. Here’s the derivation: a shadow is hard when the source is small enough that most of it is occluded by the object. Increase source size and more of it wraps around the edge, filling in the shadow from multiple angles. Size, as apparent from the subject’s perspective, is what matters — which is why a 4-foot softbox close to a subject is softer than the same softbox 20 feet away.

Specular highlights are the mirror component: where the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, the surface sends a coherent image of the light source back at the camera. This is how we read material properties. Glossy surfaces have tight, bright highlights. Rough surfaces spread them into broad, dim ones. PBR systems formalize this with microfacet models — treating rough surfaces as fields of tiny perfect mirrors at random angles, each pointing a fraction of the light somewhere slightly different. The aggregate is a controllable highlight spread.

Global illumination (GI) is the accumulation of all the bounces. It’s why a room lit by a single window doesn’t have black shadows — every surface in the room is acting as a secondary source. GI is also where color bleeds: a red wall bounces red energy into adjacent surfaces. This is physically correct behavior, and it’s expensive to compute in real time, which is why game engines have spent decades building approximations.

Where the Two Fields Diverge

Here’s the question the foundation raises: if games and commercials are both solving the same physics problem, why do their workflows look so different?

The answer is: time of evaluation.

A film or commercial captures a finite number of shots. The lighting only needs to be correct for those exact camera positions, at those exact moments. That’s a solved set. You can spend hours prelighting a set for a 30-second commercial, because you will capture that 30 seconds in one day and never have to solve it again.

A game has to solve lighting continuously. A player who moves one step to the left has a new camera position that’s never been seen before. The lighting has to be correct — or convincingly approximate — for every possible viewpoint, in real time, at 30 or 60 frames per second.

This constraint explains every major technical choice in game lighting:

Baked lighting is the precomputed answer: calculate the light transport once offline (this can take hours), store the result as a texture or spherical harmonic approximation, and look it up at runtime instead of computing it. The cost is that nothing can change — a baked shadow stays baked even if you move the object casting it.

Light probes extend the baked approach to moving objects: place spherical captures throughout the level, and interpolate between them to approximate how much indirect light should be hitting a dynamic character. Unity’s documentation specifies L2 spherical harmonics — 27 floats per probe, 9 per color channel. That’s the mathematical representation of “how much light comes from each direction,” compressed into a form cheap enough to evaluate per frame.

Reflection captures are the same idea applied to specular: capture a cubemap from a point in the scene, and project it onto nearby reflective surfaces. Unreal’s documentation describes this as having “no runtime performance cost” for static captures — because you’re just sampling a pre-baked texture.

Real-time ray tracing removes the baked approximation and computes actual light transport at runtime. It’s accurate. It’s also extremely GPU-intensive, which is why Cyberpunk 2077’s path-traced “Overdrive” mode requires AI-assisted upscaling just to become practical. The path tracer accurately simulates light throughout the scene — and that accuracy costs several orders of magnitude more than rasterized approximations.

The commercial pipeline doesn’t face these constraints, so it can afford different tools. On-set lighting uses physical fixtures, modifiers, flags, and diffusion panels. Post-production color grading can “re-light” the captured image perceptually — not by changing the physics of what was captured, but by manipulating the luminance and chroma values in a way that reads as a different lighting decision. HDR mastering workflows formalize the relationship between the grade and the display: Dolby Vision documentation describes creating an HDR master, generating analysis metadata, and producing deliverables — ensuring the lighting intent survives across different screens.

Worked Examples: Testing the Concepts Against Real Productions

The Last of Us Part II and the Ambient Problem

Consider what happens when your world is almost entirely overcast.

Most lighting systems are built around a strong directional key light — the sun, a window, a lamp. That directional light creates the contrast and shadow that give objects form. An overcast sky is a nearly uniform hemisphere of light, which means almost no directionality and almost no contrast. Every object risks looking flat.

The Last of Us Part II shipped a world designed around exactly this condition — dark, wet, post-apocalyptic environments where strong sunlight would read as wrong. The solution was a baked ambient system that went unusually deep on probe quality: dominant lighting extraction per pixel and spherical harmonic hemisphere masking, aimed at improving how probe-lit dynamic objects integrated with baked static environments. The whole system was built to hit 30 fps on console hardware with limited memory.

The constraint here isn’t aesthetic — it’s that ambient light is your primary light source, which means probe accuracy is your primary quality driver. Every technical decision flows from that.

Fortnite and the Shipping Problem

Fully dynamic GI is computationally expensive. The standard advice has been to bake what you can. Fortnite’s deployment of a fully dynamic GI system through Unreal Engine 5 was notable specifically because it shipped in a large-scale live-service game with a massive concurrent player count — a context where “let’s rebake the lighting” is not a recovery option.

Epic’s technical documentation framing this as production improvements made during shipping indicates the real challenge: getting dynamic GI to production-stable, iteratable, live-serviceable quality. The goal wasn’t the demo. It was the daily update.

The Epic Split and the 15-Minute Window

Van Damme doing a split between two reversing Volvo trucks, filmed at sunrise. The entire lighting strategy for this commercial was: show up at the right time.

The spot was designed around early-morning sun — a large, warm, low-angle source creating long shadows and a cinematic quality-of-light that would have cost days to replicate with artificial fixtures. The constraint was a window of approximately 15 minutes before the sun angle changed enough to break the look. Post-production handled wire removal and grade refinement, but the light itself was won at the scheduling stage.

This is a worked example of the principle that lighting decisions are made before anyone touches a fixture. The right question is “when and where,” not just “how.”

The Man Your Man Could Smell Like and Lighting as Continuity Glue

The Old Spice commercial involved rapid practical transitions between distinct environments — a shower, a boat, a horse — in what appeared to be one continuous shot. The apparent magic was almost entirely a lighting consistency problem: match the exposure, color temperature, and quality across all the cuts, and the brain reads it as continuous. Fail at any of those three and the cut reads as an edit.

The reported business result (sales increases far exceeding initial targets) isn’t attributable to lighting alone. But the commercial’s core formal trick — selling impossible continuity — would have collapsed under inconsistent lighting. Stability across cuts, not visual interest within cuts, was the technical requirement.

Where It Breaks: Limits of the Simple Model

The HDR Problem Is Harder Than It Looks

The models above assume that what the artist intends and what the viewer sees are the same thing. In practice, this breaks under HDR.

HDR game pipelines face an underappreciated fairness problem: different displays have different tone-mapping behaviors, and the game’s scene-referred values need to be mapped to display-referred values for each. A multi-company HDR gaming guideline identifies this as producing “gameplay-relevant visibility differences” between players on different displays — which means HDR inconsistency isn’t just an aesthetic issue. Two players may literally see different amounts of shadow detail in the same scene, on the same settings, because of display variance.

The same problem exists in commercials, but the delivery chain is more controlled. Dolby Vision mastering requires avoiding highlight clipping that can’t be recovered downstream — because unlike a game’s real-time tone mapper, a video deliverable is a fixed artifact.

Perceptual Claims Are Probabilistic, Not Deterministic

The research on lighting and psychology is real. Controlled studies show measurable mood and cognitive differences across illuminance and color temperature conditions. A game engine study found players completing mazes measurably faster under warm lighting than cool. Marketing research links brightness to intensified affective reactions and altered consumption choices.

But these are population-level effects with real variance. “Warm light increases pleasantness” is a probability, not a rule. In practice, what this means for production is that lighting choices carry genuine perceptual weight — not that you can predict individual response. The honest claim is: the expected value of your lighting choices is non-zero. That’s enough to take them seriously.

The Convergence That Hasn’t Quite Arrived

Games and film are converging — real-time engines are used in virtual production, LED volumes replace green screens, game cinematics are lit like films. But the latency constraint hasn’t disappeared. Path-traced rendering at cinematic quality in real time remains a problem of cost: the hardware is reaching a threshold where AI-assisted denoising and supersampling make it practical in limited contexts, but the architecture of “solve it once offline” versus “solve it continuously at 60fps” still defines fundamentally different workflow constraints.

A 2025 paper reframing single-image relighting as a video diffusion problem suggests one direction: instead of computing the physics of light transport, learn a mapping from “this image under condition A” to “this image under condition B” from enough training data to make the learned mapping trustworthy. That’s a different kind of approximation — not a physical shortcut, but a statistical one. It works until the distribution shifts, and it shifts in ways that are harder to predict than a mis-placed probe.

Synthesis: What’s Proven, What’s Open

Proven:

Light transport is physics, and every controllable variable in lighting design is a handle on some part of that physics. The frameworks developed across cinematography, rendering research, and environmental psychology are consistent with each other because they’re describing the same phenomenon. The baked/dynamic/hybrid pipeline choices in games are principled engineering responses to the real-time constraint, not aesthetic preferences. Lighting measurably affects mood, attention, and behavioral outcomes in controlled conditions.

Still open:

How much of the perceptual research transfers directly to production decisions is not fully established — the gap between “controlled illuminance experiment” and “shot-by-shot creative choices” involves too many confounds to close cleanly. The HDR consistency problem across heterogeneous consumer displays remains unsolved at the infrastructure level. And the question of whether learned relighting approaches will replace physics-based ones in production — or complement them — is genuinely open, contingent on how distribution-shift failures are managed in practice.

The pipeline discipline is proven. The psychological weight of lighting choices is supported. The edge cases at the hardware-consumer interface and the generalization of learned models remain active problems.

That’s what the evidence actually shows.
