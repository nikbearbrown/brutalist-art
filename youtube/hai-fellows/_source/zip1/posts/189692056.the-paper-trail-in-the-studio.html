Opening

You have 568,707 SEC Form D filings, a recording industry worth $29.6 billion, and a technology that can generate a finished song in under thirty seconds. Now decide who owns it.

That is not a rhetorical question. It is the specific legal and commercial problem that has been handed to courts, regulators, a Copyright Office, and a Supreme Court that declined to answer it—leaving the question sitting, unresolved, at the center of a market projected to grow from $569.7 million in 2024 to $2.79 billion by 2030.

The stakes are concrete. CISAC, the global body representing music creators, ran the numbers: under current conditions, 24% of music creators’ revenues are at risk by 2028. That is not an estimate of disruption. That is an estimate of transfer—money moving from one set of hands to another.

The question is: which direction is it moving, and who decided that was acceptable?

Genesis

The story does not start with Suno or Udio or any consumer platform. It starts with a technical problem: how do you teach a machine to understand music?

The answer, developed across decades of academic research, was to convert audio into discrete tokens—numbers that represent compressed audio chunks—and train Transformer models to predict which token comes next, given everything that came before. The same architecture that predicts the next word in a sentence predicts the next fragment of sound in a song. MusicGen, released by Meta in June 2023, demonstrated this cleanly: a single-stage Transformer operating on multiple “codebooks” of audio tokens, conditioned on a text prompt. You type “upbeat jazz with trumpet,” it predicts a sequence of tokens, and a decoder converts those tokens back into audio.

MusicLM, from Google, added a hierarchical layer—a second model operating at a coarser timescale to maintain coherence across longer passages, not just second-to-second prediction. Stability AI took a different route with Stable Audio, using latent diffusion rather than token prediction—generating audio by iteratively denoising a random signal toward a target distribution learned from real recordings.

Three architectures. One shared dependency: training data. Specifically, copyrighted recordings, obtained without documented licensing agreements.

This is where the technical story and the legal story become the same story.

Method

The approach that produced the consumer AI music boom was straightforward and, it turns out, legally contested at every step.

Step one: scrape or license a large corpus of audio. For research systems like MusicCaps—a dataset of music clips with expert-written captions—this was explicitly documented. For commercial systems like Suno and Udio, the training data sourcing was not publicly disclosed in detail.

Step two: train a generative model on that corpus. The model learns the statistical structure of music—the relationship between text descriptions and sonic qualities, between harmonic progressions and melodic patterns.

Step three: deploy via subscription. Suno launched its web app in December 2023. Udio followed in April 2024.

Step four: get sued.

In June 2024, major record labels—Universal Music Group, Sony Music, Warner Music Group—filed suit against Suno and Udio, alleging unlicensed copying of sound recordings for training purposes. In 2025, the suits expanded to allege that Suno’s training data included content obtained by circumventing YouTube’s technical protection measures, adding DMCA anti-circumvention claims to the copyright infringement allegations.

This sequence—build fast, get sued, settle—is not new in technology. What is new is what the settlements required: direct operational changes to the products. Universal Music Group’s settlement with Udio involved disabling song downloads. Warner Music Group’s settlement with Suno framed a move toward “licensed models” and described future download restrictions and paid-tier requirements. Users who had paid for access to downloadable content discovered that the terms had changed.

The “move fast” phase ended. The “move carefully, within negotiated constraints” phase began.

Impact

The documented outcomes of AI music deployment are not theoretical.

Spotify removed “tens of millions” of spam tracks in a single year, the result of mass-generated audio being uploaded to extract streaming royalties through artificial play counts. The mechanism is straightforward: generate 10,000 tracks overnight, upload them under fictional artists, pay for artificial streams, collect the per-stream royalty. At scale, this is not marginal. Deezer reported detecting AI-generated content at volume and removing it from recommendations—then commercialized the detection technology, licensing it to rights organizations.

The detection business existing is evidence the fraud problem is real.

At the other end of the market, the case for legitimate AI music tools is also documented. The Beatles released “Now and Then” after ML-assisted separation allowed clearer vocals from a decades-old demo tape—enhancement, not generation, but still machine learning applied to commercial music at the highest levels of the industry. A fast-food brand produced a music-led advertising campaign using Suno in a matter of days, where the same work would previously have taken weeks. YouTube built Dream Track—a constrained, creator-facing AI music tool for Shorts—with watermarking baked in from the start via Google DeepMind’s SynthID system.

Grimes launched a licensed voice cloning platform, Elf.Tech, splitting royalties 50/50 with anyone who created tracks using her AI voice model. More than 1,000 songs were created through the prototype. This is a data point about demand: significant, but also small relative to the unlicensed alternative.

The legitimate use cases are real. So is the damage. The question is whether the governance structures being built are fast enough to separate one from the other before the damage compounds.

Complications

Three honest limitations of the current picture.

First, the copyright question is not fully resolved. The U.S. Copyright Office’s January 2025 report concluded that purely AI-generated outputs are not protectable under existing law—but that human selection, arrangement, and modification can be. The practical boundary between “AI-generated” and “human-modified” is genuinely unclear for most production workflows. The Supreme Court declined in 2026 to take up the core “AI cannot be the author” case, leaving lower-court decisions intact but also leaving the detailed line-drawing to future litigation and regulation.

Second, the market size numbers require interpretation. The $569.7 million figure for 2024 generative AI in music comes from a single market research dataset with a specific methodology. A separate report using different assumptions estimated $419.85 million for the same year. Neither figure can be independently verified from public financial disclosures. These are estimates of a market segment that did not exist five years ago, built on survey data and top-down modeling. The 30.5% CAGR projection to 2030 compounds whatever error is in the 2024 baseline.

Third, the training data bias problem has not been solved. A NAACL 2025 paper quantified the imbalance in public music datasets and found that non-Western genres represent a small fraction of dataset hours. Models trained on these corpora generate Western-dominant music by default and require explicit fine-tuning to handle other traditions—fine-tuning that requires data that largely does not exist in licensed, accessible form. The platforms selling “global” music generation are, in most cases, selling Western-dominant music generation with a global price tag.

Closing

The paper trail in AI music leads somewhere specific.

It leads to a U.S. Copyright Office report concluding that human authorship is required for protection, and that prompts alone do not provide it. It leads to major-label settlements that rewrote product terms mid-subscription. It leads to streaming fraud at a scale that required industry-wide detection infrastructure to contain. It leads to a CISAC forecast of 24% revenue exposure for creators by 2028.

What follows logically from this evidence: AI music tools are not neutral productivity software. They are infrastructure that redistributes value—from creators to platforms, from small operators to major labels who can afford to litigate, from catalog-rich incumbents to anyone willing to negotiate a licensing deal. The beneficiaries are determined by who controls the training data licensing, the detection systems, and the settlement terms.

The tools will not stop improving. The question that remains precisely unanswered is whether the governance infrastructure—copyright doctrine, platform policy, collective licensing frameworks, detection-as-a-service—can reach the same capability level as the generation systems before the redistribution becomes permanent.

Beatoven.ai’s “licensed model” positioning, Sweden’s STIM AI music license experiment, and SynthID-style watermarking are early evidence that the answer might be yes. CISAC’s 24% figure is evidence that right now, the answer is no.
