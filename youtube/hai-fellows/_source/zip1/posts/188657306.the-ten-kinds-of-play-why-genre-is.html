Not literally. You’re holding a controller, staring at a screen. But the fog is there, conceptual, hanging between you and the thing you’re actually doing. The box said “real-time strategy.” The algorithm said “platformer.” The genre tag said “RPG.” None of those words describe what’s happening in your nervous system right now—the tight anticipation before a move, the collapse of uncertainty into outcome, the specific texture of triumph that has nothing to do with pixels and everything to do with play.

Game designers Colleen Macklin and John Sharp noticed this fog. And in their work on iterative design, they proposed something that sounds simple until you realize how much it dismantles: stop naming games by what they look like. Start naming them by what they feel like.

The result is a taxonomy of ten kinds of play. Competitive, cooperative, skill-based, experience-based, chance-based, whimsical, role-playing, performative, expressive, and simulation-based. Not genres. Not market categories. Kinds of experience. The difference is the difference between classifying music by the shape of the instrument and classifying it by what it does to your body when you hear it.

This is Chapter 3 of their framework. Here’s what it actually claims, what evidence it marshals, where it succeeds, and where it quietly hopes you won’t press too hard.

The Claim: Genre Is Producing the Wrong Map

The core argument is surgical. Genre labels—”first-person shooter,” “platformer,” “city-builder”—describe production decisions. They tell you what engine was used, what lineage the game inherits, what shelf at GameStop it occupies. They tell you almost nothing about what the player is actually doing with their mind and body.

Play types do something different. They categorize by experience architecture—by the cognitive, physical, and emotional states the design is trying to produce. When you shift from “this is a fighting game” to “this is a competitive game prioritizing moment-to-moment territorial pressure,” you’ve moved from describing the artifact to describing the intended human encounter with it.

This is not a minor reframe. It’s a design compass reorientation.

The taxonomy Macklin and Sharp propose has ten axes, and each one names something real about what happens when a person sits down to play. Before we go deep, here they are — all ten, each one a lens, each one a different answer to the question what is this player actually doing?

1. Competitive — You want something the other player also wants, and only one of you can have it. Chess. Two players, one winner, zero ambiguity about the structure of conflict.

2. Cooperative — You and your partner need each other to survive. Not want. Need. Separate, you lose. Pandemic makes this structural: no single player can track a global disease cascade alone.

3. Skill-Based — Mastery is the point. Either your hands get faster (Super Meat Boy) or your mind gets sharper (The Witness). The game is a mirror held up to your own capability.

4. Experience-Based — There is no win state. There is only the journey through a designed world. Journey asks you to cross a desert and feel something. Whether you feel it or not is entirely your responsibility.

5. Chance-Based — The outcome is uncertain in ways you cannot fully control, and that uncertainty is the engine. Every Slay the Spire run hands you a different deck. The skill is in adapting, not in predicting.

6. Whimsical — The game makes you look ridiculous, and that is the product. QWOP gives you four keys to control a sprinter’s individual leg muscles. Failure is not the obstacle. Failure is the joke you’re both in on.

7. Role-Playing — You become someone else, temporarily and deliberately. The Witcher 3 asks you to inhabit Geralt’s moral universe, to make decisions as him, to feel the weight of a world built around his specific kind of weariness.

8. Performative — Your actions are a show, as much for the audience as for the system. Beat Saber is exercise. It is also, unavoidably, a spectacle. Nobody in the room is looking at the screen.

9. Expressive — The game gives you a brief, high-stakes window to put something of yourself into the work. Minecraft hands you an infinite canvas and asks: what do you make when nothing is required of you?

10. Simulation-Based — You manage a system. You read feedback loops, balance competing pressures, and feel the satisfaction of a city that finally stops flooding. Cities: Skylines is not a game about buildings. It is a game about understanding cause and effect at scale.

Ten lenses. Not mutually exclusive — The Witcher 3 is role-playing and skill-based and experience-based simultaneously. The point is not to pick one box. The point is to know which experiences you’re designing for. Let me walk through the ones that do the most work.

Competition: The Architecture of Wanting to Win

Competition is the oldest play type and the one most distorted by genre thinking. We call games “competitive” as if competition were a single thing. It isn’t.

Consider Nidhogg—technically a side-scrolling fighting game. But the competitive structure isn’t about health bars or combo strings. It’s territorial. You fight to move through the opponent’s space toward a final destination. Every kill is immediately reversed by respawn. The competition is positional, moment-to-moment, a constant negotiation of forward pressure. Genre says: fighting game. Play type says: high-tension zero-sum spatial struggle. These are not the same instruction to a designer.

Now consider Canabalt. You run. You jump. You die. The “competition” here has no opponent—unless you count your previous score, or the leaderboard sitting silently behind the interface. The game is cataloging your performance against a prior version of yourself. This is unilateral competition, a player against the abstracted ghost of their own capability. Genre says: endless runner. Play type says: internalized competition, the self as both player and rival.

Desert Golfing extends this into something almost philosophical. Hundreds of holes. A running stroke count that never resets. The competition is between you and the accumulated weight of your own decisions, stretched across an absurd, procedurally infinite landscape. You can’t win Desert Golfing. You can only discover how you respond when winning is structurally impossible.

Three games. One genre cluster. Three completely different competitive architectures. The play-type lens sees distinctions the genre label buries.

The taxonomy identifies five competitive subtypes: player versus player, team versus team, asymmetrical competition, unilateral competition, and multilateral competition. Each produces a different psychological environment. PvP creates direct psychological pressure. Asymmetrical competition—where players have different rules, different resources, or different goals—creates information disparity and specialized strategy. Multilateral competition, with three or more simultaneous rivals, creates diplomacy, shifting alliances, and the strange calculus of when to cooperate with your enemy against a greater threat.

These distinctions matter at the design level. They’re not interchangeable. You can’t swap them without changing the experience.

Cooperation: When the System Is the Enemy

Flip competition and you get cooperation—but it’s not a simple inversion. Cooperative play doesn’t just allow players to help each other. Good cooperative design makes cooperation structurally necessary. If you can win without relying on your partner, the cooperation is decorative. The design hasn’t actually committed.

Way committed. The game pairs strangers online and strips away all communication except gesture and shared movement. No voice chat. No text. You make your way through environments together using only the expressive vocabulary your bodies can invent in real time. Trust is not a feature of Way. Trust is the mechanic.

Portal 2‘s cooperative mode works differently. The solutions to puzzles are literally invisible to a single player—each player holds half the spatial information needed to see the answer. You cannot solve the puzzles alone. Not because the game punishes solo play, but because the architecture of the solution requires two perspectives. This is cooperative design as epistemic structure. Understanding is distributed.

Pandemic scales this further. The threat is systemic—a global cascade of disease outbreaks managed by a team of specialists. No single player can track all the moving parts. Cooperation here is a resource allocation problem, a long-horizon planning exercise where the “opponent” is the game’s own accelerating complexity. This is the “team versus system” cooperative subtype: all players aligned against the designed chaos of the ruleset.

What connects these is the Frame Layer—the social context surrounding the play. Cooperative games live or die not just by their rules but by the relationships those rules construct. Way builds intimacy between strangers. Portal 2 builds collaborative triumph. Pandemic builds collective anxiety and shared relief. Genre says: puzzle game, cooperative game, board game. Play type says: trust construction, epistemic partnership, systemic collective struggle. The distinction tells you what to design for.

Skill, Chance, and the Texture of Uncertainty

Skill-based play divides into two clean categories that often blur in execution: active skill (reflexes, timing, physical precision) and mental skill (logic, pattern recognition, inference).

Super Meat Boy is almost pure active skill. The game gives you milliseconds to respond to obstacles. Mastery is muscular—you are training your body, not reasoning your way through. The satisfaction is the satisfaction of flow, the state where challenge and capability are so precisely matched that conscious thought drops out of the loop entirely. Failure is instant. Feedback is immediate. The loop is tight enough to approach ritual.

The Witness inverts this almost completely. No reflex required. Every puzzle is a logic problem embedded in an environment. The active skill is close to zero. The mental skill required—inferring rules from incomplete environmental evidence, holding multiple rule systems in memory simultaneously—is demanding in a completely different register. The fog here isn’t physical. It’s conceptual. And clearing it feels different from clearing a platform gauntlet.

Portal asks for both. You have to understand the spatial logic of portal placement before you act, and you have to execute that placement with precision under time pressure. The game is demanding a kind of real-time spatial reasoning that straddles the two categories—mental modeling translated into physical execution.

Chance interrupts all of this. And here the taxonomy does something subtle: it recasts chance not as the absence of skill but as a design tool for generating motivating uncertainty.

Android: Netrunner is the richest example. It’s an asymmetrical card game where one player attacks corporate servers and one player defends them. Both players build decks. Both players have skill-based decisions to make. But the hidden information—the face-down cards, the unknown hand, the bluff—creates a state where optimal strategy is impossible to compute. You must decide under uncertainty, and the desire to resolve that uncertainty is itself the engine of engagement. The researchers call this a “jolt of positive affect” when the unknown becomes known. The game is, in part, a machine for manufacturing small moments of revelation.

This matters because it challenges a common design assumption: that randomness undermines agency. In Netrunner, the uncertainty is the agency. Managing incomplete information is the skill. The chance isn’t noise. It’s the signal.

Whimsy, Bodies, and the Comedy of Failure

Whimsical play exists in a register most game design theory doesn’t have vocabulary for. It isn’t about winning. It isn’t about narrative. It isn’t about skill in any traditional sense. It’s about the hilarity of the body—specifically, your body, failing in spectacular, public ways.

QWOP is the canonical example. You control a sprinter using four keys that correspond to individual muscle groups in their thighs and calves. This is not a useful control scheme. It is a deliberately dysfunctional one. The point is not to run efficiently. The point is to watch yourself fail to run efficiently, to struggle against a system designed to make basic locomotion grotesque. The comedy is the gap between what you intend and what your avatar’s limbs actually do.

J.S. Joust is a physical game using motion controllers. Players try to jostle each other’s controllers while keeping their own steady. Slow baroque music plays; when it speeds up, the sensitivity of the controllers drops and sudden movements are permitted. The play is the choreography of bodies in social space, the negotiation of aggression and grace under an arbitrary musical constraint. Watching other people play J.S. Joust is better than playing it, which is the tell: this is performative by design.

Tenya Wanya Teens arms players with controllers covered in non-intuitive buttons and asks them to respond to ordinary social situations. The comedy is conceptual—the mismatch between the mundane task and the overwhelming mechanical chaos required to accomplish it.

What these games share is a willingness to locate the experience in the Frame Layer, in the social visibility of play. The player’s body becomes the spectacle. Failure is not a setback. It is the product.

Role-Playing: Emergence vs. Authorship

Role-playing, in Macklin and Sharp’s framework, is not about stats or leveling systems. It’s about the embodiment of perspective—the temporary adoption of another’s reality.

This puts it on a spectrum between two poles they call emergent and authored.

At the emergent end, games like Lesberation provide light structural scaffolding and let players collaboratively generate stories within that space. The rules create possibility; the players create meaning. This is what Jesper Juul calls a “game of emergence”—a small ruleset generating a large, player-constructed experience space.

At the authored end, The Path constructs a dense, pre-designed storyworld and asks the player to inhabit it. The agency is investigative, not generative. You’re reading the designers’ intentions, not writing your own. Howling Dogs takes this further, using fractured, impressionistic structure to put the player in the position of inferring a character’s reality rather than directing their actions.

The interesting design question this creates: what are you asking the player to do with their imagination? Emergent role-playing asks players to project outward, to build. Authored role-playing asks players to receive, to interpret, to be moved. Neither is superior. They are different invitations.

Experience, Expression, and the Two Ways of Feeling

The taxonomy’s thinnest distinction is also its most philosophically interesting. Macklin and Sharp separate experience-based play from expressive play, and the line between them is genuinely subtle.

In experience-based play, the player is a witness. Dear Esther asks you to walk through a Scottish island while fragments of a disintegrating narrative wash over you. The game is largely indifferent to your presence. You move through it; it exists in a state that does not require you to complete or change it. The play is the reading of an aesthetic object.

In expressive play, the player is an author, however briefly. Queers in Love at the End of the World gives you ten seconds—literally a countdown timer—to choose how to express affection to another character before the world ends. The outcome is invariant. The world ends regardless. But the choice of expression, compressed into ten seconds of urgency, is the entire play. You’re not reading. You’re writing.

The distinction is the direction of meaning-making. Inward, toward you, as the experience washes over you. Or outward, from you, as you inscribe something into the final moment.

Both prioritize designed emotional experience over traditional win-state mechanics. But they do it with different architectures of agency. This is a real distinction. It just required that precise framing to become visible.

Simulation: Systems, Feedback, and the Pleasure of Management

Simulation-based play is the play of systems management—the pleasure of manipulating complex rule-sets toward desired outcomes. SimCity and Rollercoaster Tycoon ask you to balance infrastructure, economics, and visitor satisfaction simultaneously, decoding dense feedback loops of information to make resource decisions. Consentacle abstracts physical and social intimacy into a card-based vocabulary-building exercise. Medical and aviation training simulations leverage the same cognitive infrastructure for professional skill acquisition.

What connects them is the relationship to the Information Layer: the density of data the player must decode, model, and respond to. The pleasure is the pleasure of understanding a system well enough to control it—which is, when you think about it, the pleasure of competence itself.

Where the Taxonomy Succeeds—and Where It Quietly Hopes You Won’t Press

This framework does real work. It gives designers a vocabulary for asking the right question: not “what genre is this?” but “what state am I trying to produce in the player, and what structural choices will produce that state?”

The case studies are well-chosen. Nidhogg, Way, Super Meat Boy, QWOP—each one is doing something precise, and the play-type lens makes that precision visible. The taxonomy earns its status as a reference framework.

But it stops short of being a theory.

The framework is descriptive, not generative. It catalogs play types that have already been observed in independent and mainstream games. It does not provide a first-principle test for identifying new play types, or a rigorous criterion for when two apparent types are genuinely distinct versus variants of the same underlying structure. The experience-based/expressive distinction is the thinnest seam in the taxonomy—the examples partially overlap, and the operative difference (direction of meaning-making) is not made explicit in the text. Without that explicit principle, the line could be drawn differently by different readers, which means it isn’t quite a line yet. It’s a heuristic.

There’s a stronger methodological challenge embedded in the framework’s introductory framing. Macklin and Sharp assert that genre thinking is limiting and play-type thinking is liberating. This is probably true. But it’s asserted, not demonstrated. To prove it, you’d need to show that designers consciously using a play-type lens produce measurably more innovative or effective work than designers using genre as a starting point. No such evidence is offered. The argument rests on the aesthetic success of specific case studies—which is suggestive but not causal. It tells you the play-type lens can identify good design in retrospect. It doesn’t prove that applying the lens prospectively produces better design.

This is a common limitation of design taxonomies: they organize after the fact better than they generate before it.

What You Keep

You keep the vocabulary. You keep the habit of asking: what experience am I designing for, not just what mechanics am I assembling toward.

You keep the competitive subtypes, because PvP and unilateral competition and multilateral competition really do produce different cognitive environments, and treating them as interchangeable is how you get a game that’s technically competitive and experientially flat.

You keep the cooperation insight: that cooperation must be structural to be meaningful—that if you can win without your partner, the design hasn’t actually committed to the play type it claims to be.

You keep the motivating uncertainty principle: that chance, properly deployed, is not noise but signal, that the desire to resolve the unknown is itself a driver of engagement.

You keep the Frame Layer awareness: that some games are fundamentally about their social context, about what it looks like to watch someone play QWOP, and that designing for that Frame Layer is as legitimate as designing for the Information Layer or the Interaction Layer.

What you give back: the claim that this taxonomy is complete, or that its categories are theoretically grounded rather than empirically observed. It’s a working map, not a territory.

And a working map, used with clear eyes about its edges, is often exactly what you need.

The fog doesn’t fully clear. But you can see further than you could before. That’s what the ten kinds of play actually give you.
