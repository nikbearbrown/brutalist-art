Title

Pong

Gameplay Description

Pong is a two-player twitch-style local-multiplayer ballgame with a dose of strategic decision-making played in an arcade cabinet. The play experience transposes tennis and ping pong to a two-dimensional videogame. Players are positioned on opposite sides of a court from which they serve and volley.

Play begins with one side serving the ball to the receiving side. A volley continues as long as each side hits the ball back to the other player without missing it. Players can only move up and down (not left and right). The ball may bounce off the top or bottom of the screen as part of legal play, but if a player misses the ball, it will exit on the player’s side and the other player receives a point.

Goal

The goal of the game is for a player to reach a score of 11 before the other player.

Basic Elements

Players

Two players, each represented by a rectangular paddle that moves vertically.

Paddles

Divided into eight segments that determine the angle at which the ball bounces.

Ball

Bounces in the opposite direction when hit, depending on which segment of the paddle makes contact and the angle of approach. The ball speeds up with every successful hit.

Walls

Run along the top and bottom of the screen. The ball may bounce off them; they prevent the paddle from moving off-screen.

Scoreboard

One score per side, tracking up to 11. Example display: 09 | 11

Scoring

Points scored as single increments. Games played to 11; can be won by a single point (e.g., 11–10). A point is scored each time a player fails to return the opponent’s volley.

Interface & Controls

Controls: Each player has an analog joystick that controls vertical movement of their paddle along the baseline.

Screen Layout: Court viewed from the top. Players on opposing sides (left and right). Scoreboard along the top of the screen; a dotted line divides the two sides.

Game Flow

START
  └─> Serve (random angle)
        └─> Volley back and forth
              ├─> Ball missed → Point awarded → Return to Serve
              └─> First to 11 → GAME OVER

Art Direction

Simplicity. White on black.

Technical Overview

Transistor-to-transistor logic. (It’s 1972!)

1. The Vision

One-Sentence Pitch: Two players, one ball, a court the size of a television screen, first to eleven.

This is a game about the gap between intention and execution. You want to put the ball where your opponent isn’t. You almost never do. That tension—between the trajectory you planned and the one you get—is the entire game. Everything else is decoration.

Design Pillars:

Twitch Primacy. The game lives in the space between seeing the ball move and moving your paddle to meet it. Reaction speed is the skill. Everything we build either tests that skill or gets out of its way.

Transparent Complexity. The ball speeds up. The angle changes based on where it hits the paddle. Players learn this by playing, not by reading. No tutorial. No explanation. The rules teach themselves.

Social Stakes. Your opponent is standing next to you. They can see your face. This is not a game you play alone in a dark room. The cabinet is a communal object and the design respects that.

Constraint as Craft. Players cannot move left or right. The ball cannot go sideways. The paddles cannot reach the very top of the screen. Every constraint exists because removing it makes the game worse, not because we ran out of time.

Non-Goals:

This is not a simulation of real tennis. We are not modeling gravity, spin, net height, or serve physics. Players who want tennis should play tennis.

This is not a single-player game. There is no AI opponent. A player alone with this cabinet is waiting for someone to join them.

This is not a spectacle. The court is white on black. There are no animations, no cutscenes, no sound effects beyond the collision pulses the sync generator produces anyway. Ornamentation costs chips. Chips cost money. More importantly: ornamentation would pull the player’s eye away from the ball.

This is not a game you can win passively. Luck determines the serve angle. Everything after the serve is on you.

Player Fantasy: The moment you cut the ball into the corner and your opponent dives for it and misses. That moment. We are building a machine for producing that moment, repeatedly, for as long as players keep feeding it quarters.

2. Core Loop & Mechanics

The Loop: Serve → Volley → Miss → Point → Serve again. Until someone reaches eleven.

That’s it. The loop is four steps. The depth lives inside the volley.

The 3Cs:

Character: The player is the paddle. There is no avatar, no name, no backstory. The paddle is a 16-pixel-tall rectangle that moves up and down. That is the entire player identity. It is enough.

Camera: Fixed. Top-down. The entire court is always visible. There is no scrolling, no zoom, no perspective shift. The player always knows where everything is. The design challenge is what they do with that knowledge.

Controls: One analog potentiometer per player. Rotating the dial moves the paddle vertically. The analog input matters—it gives players fine-grained control over position, which matters because position determines angle. A digital button would be a worse input here. We are not using digital buttons.

Primary Mechanics:

Paddle Movement: Constrained to the Y-axis only. Players cannot move horizontally. The paddle’s baseline is fixed at the left or right edge of the court. This simplification is not a limitation—it is the definition of the game. Horizontal movement would change the collision geometry entirely and require a different game.

The paddles cannot reach the very top of the screen. This is a hardware artifact from the original circuit that we are keeping intentionally. It creates a dead zone. A perfect player cannot cover the full court. That’s the point.

*The Eight Segments:* The paddle is 16 pixels tall, divided into eight segments of two pixels each. Each segment returns the ball at a different angle. The angle is produced by manipulating the vertical velocity component (vyvy​) while inverting the horizontal component (vxvx​):

θ=arctan⁡(vyvx)θ=arctan(vx​vy​​)

Center segments keep vyvy​ low or zero—the ball returns nearly horizontal. Outer segments increase vyvy​—the ball returns on a sharp diagonal. Players who understand this can aim. Players who don’t will learn by losing.

This is the mechanic that separates Pong from its predecessor. The Magnavox Odyssey ping-pong game returns the ball at a constant angle. Every volley in that game is predetermined. In this game, every volley is a decision.

*Ball Physics:* The ball moves at constant velocity until it hits something. When it hits a horizontal wall, vyvy​ inverts. When it hits a paddle, vxvx​ inverts and vyvy​ is modified by segment contact. That is the complete physics model.

*Ball Acceleration:* With each successful paddle contact, vxvx​ increments. The ball gets faster. This continues until a player misses, at which point speed resets for the next serve. The acceleration is not cosmetic—it is the mechanism that guarantees the game ends. Without it, two skilled players could volley forever. With it, the game always escalates to a point where someone breaks.

The acceleration also creates a difficulty curve that the players generate themselves. An extended volley means both players are good. Both players being good means the ball is fast. A fast ball means someone will eventually make an error under pressure. The game produces its own climax.

Serve: The serve launches from center court at a random angle. The randomness is intentional—it prevents the server from having a predictable advantage and ensures no two games begin identically.

Scoring: When the ball exits the screen past a paddle, the opponent scores one point. First to eleven wins. The margin doesn’t matter; 11–10 is a win. The game resets immediately.

Edge Cases:

If the ball contacts the very corner of the paddle—the outermost pixel of the outermost segment—the return angle is maximum. This produces a nearly vertical trajectory. The opponent may not be able to reach it. This is not a bug. This is the highest expression of skill in the game.

If a player’s paddle is at its vertical limit (top or bottom of its travel range) and the ball arrives at that extreme, the ball may pass untouched. The dead zone at the top of the screen is unreachable. This is, again, intentional. There is no perfect defense.

3. Narrative & World

There is no narrative. This needs to be said clearly so it doesn’t become a conversation later.

There are no characters with names. There is no setting beyond “a court.” There is no story before the game or after it. The game begins when the coin drops and ends when someone reaches eleven. The drama inside that frame is the only drama we are providing.

World Rules:

The court is a bounded rectangle. The top and bottom walls are solid—the ball bounces off them. The left and right edges are open—the ball exits through them and triggers scoring. This asymmetry (solid horizontal, open vertical) is what makes the game possible. It is the entire spatial logic of the world.

Objects in this world obey three laws: the ball moves in straight lines until it contacts a surface; contact with a horizontal surface inverts vertical velocity; contact with a paddle inverts horizontal velocity and modifies vertical velocity by segment. These laws do not change. They do not have exceptions. Players who understand them play better than players who don’t.

Tone: There is none. Or rather: the tone is whatever the two people playing bring to it. The machine provides the court and the rules. The players provide the tension, the trash talk, the celebration, the frustration. The design trusts that two people competing for money in a public space will generate all the emotional content the game needs.

What the Sounds Mean: The collision pulses—”ping,” “pong,” “clack”—are not designed sounds. They are artifacts of the sync generator repurposed by Allan Alcorn because they were available and appropriate. The “ping” when the ball hits a paddle. The “pong” when it hits a wall. The “clack” when a point is scored. Players will learn these sounds within thirty seconds. After that, the sounds carry information. A player who hears “clack” and doesn’t see the ball knows a point was scored. The audio is a second feedback channel, and it costs nothing extra because it was already there.

4. Level Design & Progression

There is one level. It is the court. It does not change.

The “progression” in Pong is not spatial—it is temporal. The game gets harder as it goes on because the ball gets faster. The court does not change. The rules do not change. The player’s nervous system is the variable. By the time the ball is at maximum speed, the game is testing the outer edge of human reaction time. That is the difficulty curve. It is generated by the mechanics, not by level design.

What the Court Teaches:

The first thirty seconds: Players learn that the paddle moves vertically only. They learn that the ball bounces off walls. They learn that missing the ball gives the opponent a point.

The first two minutes: Players learn that where the ball hits the paddle changes where it goes. They begin experimenting with paddle positioning. Some will do this consciously. Most will do it instinctively and won’t be able to explain what they’re doing.

After the first game: Players learn that the ball accelerates. They begin managing the volley differently—trying to end exchanges quickly before the ball becomes uncontrollable, or trying to extend them to tire the opponent out.

The court teaches all of this without text, without prompts, without instruction beyond “Avoid missing ball for high score.” That instruction appears on the cabinet. It is the complete tutorial.

5. UI/UX & Player Interface

Interface Philosophy: Diegetic. Everything visible on screen exists within the world of the game. There is no meta-layer, no menu, no pause screen, no settings. The scoreboard is part of the court. The dotted midline is part of the court. The paddles and the ball are the court. The player is never looking at an interface. They are looking at the game.

Information Hierarchy:

Always visible: both paddles, the ball, the scoreboard, the dotted midline.

Never visible: anything else.

The scoreboard displays single-digit increments up to eleven, one per side. Format: 09 | 11. The dotted midline provides spatial orientation—it tells the player where center court is, which matters for reading ball trajectory. That is its entire function. It is not decorative.

The Potentiometer: The analog dial input translates physical rotation into precise vertical movement. This is the correct input for this game. Fine motor control is the skill being tested—the paddle needs to move exactly as far as the player intends, not just “up” or “down.” A digital button would produce binary movement. Binary movement cannot express the segment-targeting precision the game is designed around. We are not switching to buttons.

Accessibility: There are two inputs. Both are identical in form and function. A player with one hand can play. A player who has never held a game controller can play. The physical metaphor—turn the dial, the paddle moves—is immediately legible to anyone who has ever turned a knob.

The Fourth Wall: The screen is it. The glass separates the physical players from the digital court. Everything the player needs to know is on the other side of that glass. Everything they need to do is in their hand on this side of it.

6. Technical Constraints & Platform Specs

Platform: Dedicated arcade cabinet. There is no other platform. This game will not be ported. It cannot be ported. It is the hardware.

Logic Architecture: Discrete transistor-to-transistor logic (TTL) chips. There is no CPU. There is no software. Every rule in this document is implemented as a physical arrangement of logic gates and timing circuits. When we say “if the ball hits the paddle,” we mean: when two electrical signals are simultaneously high, an AND gate triggers and inverts a counter. The game is the circuit.

Master Clock: Crystal oscillator at 14.318 MHz14.318 MHz, halved to 7.159 MHz7.159 MHz. This is four times the NTSC colorburst frequency, chosen for synchronization with consumer television standards.

Horizontal Timing: 7493 ripple counters. Reset every 455 clock cycles.

fH=7.159 MHz455≈15.734 kHzfH​=4557.159 MHz​≈15.734 kHz

This aligns with NTSC horizontal scan frequency. The ripple counters have propagation delay—their timing is “sloppy” by precision standards. This is acceptable. The human eye cannot perceive the difference.

Vertical Timing: 74107 JK flip-flops. Reset after 262.5 scan lines to complete one NTSC field.

Collision Detection: Not an algorithm. A physical AND operation. The ball signal and the paddle signal are fed into an AND gate. If both are simultaneously high, the objects have overlapped. The gate triggers. Velocity inverts. This happens at the speed of TTL logic—nanoseconds. There is no perceptible latency between the ball hitting the paddle and the ball changing direction. The game feels responsive because it is. Electrically.

Display: Black-and-white CRT. Color is not being added. Color requires chrominance signal management—more chips, more cost, more failure points. The high-contrast white-on-black aesthetic is a technical constraint that became a design identity. We are not apologizing for it.

Sound: Sync generator pulses repurposed as audio. The original vision included a crowd-noise sound. Crowd noise is impossible to synthesize in discrete TTL logic at this cost point. What we have instead are three distinct collision tones produced by the same timing circuits that run the video. They are appropriate. We are keeping them.

What We Are Not Supporting: Color. Sound complexity. Sprite-based graphics. Programmable logic of any kind. Single-player mode. Saving scores. Networking. Any of this would require a microprocessor. Microprocessors are not in the budget and are not necessary for this game.

7. Economy & Monetization

Business Model: Coin-operated. One quarter per game, or as adjusted by the operator.

The economic model is transparent and embedded in the hardware. The coin mechanism is not an afterthought—it is part of the design constraint. Every design decision in this document exists inside the question: will a stranger pay twenty-five cents to play this for three minutes?

The answer, tested at Andy Capp’s Tavern in Sunnyvale, was yes. Repeatedly. The coin bucket overflowed. The machine was mistaken for broken. It was not broken. It was working precisely as designed.

What Drives Replays: Loss. When you lose, you want to play again. When you win, your opponent wants to play again. The fast reset loop—score reaches eleven, game resets, coins go in—is not accidental. There is no cool-down, no score screen that lingers, no ceremony around the ending. The game ends and immediately offers to begin again. This is the correct behavior for an income-generating machine.

What We Will Not Monetize: The game itself is the product. There are no upgrades, no unlocks, no premium paddles. The skill ceiling is open to any player willing to spend the time. A good player who has paid one quarter has access to the same mechanics as a player who has paid twenty. This is correct. We are selling competition, not advantage.

8. Non-Goals & Rejected Features

Single-player mode. We discussed it. An AI opponent would require logic that tracks the ball’s trajectory and moves the paddle accordingly. That logic, in TTL, would add significant complexity and cost without improving the core experience. The game is about human competition. A machine opponent is a different game.

Color graphics. Rejected on cost grounds, and on merit. High-contrast white-on-black directs attention exactly where it needs to go: the ball. Color would add visual noise around the thing the player needs to track. The constraint is the aesthetics.

Complex sound. Alcorn’s original design called for a cheering crowd. Impossible in discrete logic at this cost point. The sync pulses are better than silence and better than a failed attempt at crowd noise. We’re keeping the pulses.

Horizontal paddle movement. Tested in early iteration. When paddles can move horizontally, collision geometry becomes significantly more complex and the game loses its clarity. Players should be solving one problem: vertical positioning. Not two.

A longer winning condition. First to fifteen or twenty was considered. Rejected. Eleven produces games of appropriate length for the arcade context—long enough to feel contested, short enough that the loser wants to go again immediately. The number eleven is not arbitrary. It is tested.

What Might Return Later: A single-player mode with a CPU opponent becomes possible the moment a microprocessor is in the budget. If the industry moves toward programmable arcade hardware—and there are signs it will—this is the first feature to revisit.

A game design document is an act of testimony. This one testifies to a system where hardware constraints and design intent became, briefly, the same thing. The TTL chips could not do more than this. It turns out more than this was not needed.
