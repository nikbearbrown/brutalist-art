Start with a fact that should not be true. Traditional survival horror is structurally built on isolation. Strip a player of company, of light, of certainty, and the nervous system does the rest. So if you put a second person in the room — a friend, a voice, a body that can absorb half the danger — the genre should, by its own logic, stop working.

It did not stop working. It became one of the most lucrative formats in interactive entertainment. Phasmophobia has sold over 23 million copies. Lethal Company, built by a single developer, grossed roughly $113.9 million by January 2024. Something here does not add up — and the gap between “horror needs isolation” and “co-op horror prints money” is exactly the thing worth teaching.

This piece is a proof, not a tour. Each claim will rest on the one before it. We begin with a single axiom about the body, and we will not assert anything we cannot trace back to it.

Step 01 — The Axiom

Fear is a measurable physical state, and company changes it

Before we can explain a genre, we need a foundation that does not depend on taste or opinion. Here it is.

Axiom — Established, not assumed

Fear under threat is a physiological event: the sympathetic nervous system activates, heart rate climbs, skin conductance rises, and the body releases cortisol. This is measurable. It is not a metaphor.

Now the second fact, which follows from biology rather than game design. When a person faces a stressor alone, they carry the entire load — every sensory input, every decision, every threat to track. When they face it beside a trusted companion, a mechanism called social buffering activates. The presence of an ally triggers oxytocin release, which down-regulates the HPA axis, which lowers cortisol secretion. The threat response is dampened. Not by willpower — by neurochemistry.

So here is the worked consequence, and it is uncomfortable for a horror designer: the mere presence of a friend makes a terrifying situation objectively less terrifying. Not “feels” less terrifying as a vague impression. The stress hormone load is lower. The game has handed the player a biological cheat code simply by letting them bring someone along.

If that were the end of the chain, co-op horror would be impossible. It is not the end of the chain.

Step 02 — The Complication

Two facts about groups that the axiom did not predict

Social buffering tells us groups are calmer. But it is incomplete, because it treats a group as one averaged emotional state. A group is not that. It is several nervous systems wired into each other through sound and sight. That wiring carries two effects the buffering model alone cannot give us.

First effect — context-dependence. Physiological studies show that during low-threat, fiddly tasks, cooperative play can actually raise arousal and cognitive load: you are now spending attention on coordination, on staying together, on not losing the other person. The buffer only pays out during genuinely high-threat moments. So co-op does not simply lower fear — it reshapes its timing, flattening the quiet parts and concentrating the spikes.

Second effect — contagion. Emotion does not stay inside one skull. If one player hits acute panic, their scream, their erratic movement, their broken voice transmit an anxiogenic state to teammates who have not even seen the threat yet. Fear becomes externalized and contagious. One person’s terror is now an input to everyone else’s.

Hold those two facts next to the axiom. We now have a system with a safety net (buffering) and two ways that net behaves unpredictably (timing and contagion). That is enough structure to write the relationship down precisely.

A model — derived from the facts above, not invented

TPI = Ψ · HcN · ( 1 + β · Σ δij · e−λdij )

Read it as a teaching tool, not a law of physics. The Threat Perception Index rises with raw environmental stress (Ψ) and with the player’s information deficit (Hc — missing map knowledge, hidden mechanics, spatial uncertainty). It falls as the number of nearby living players (N) rises and as the group’s buffering coefficient (β) does its work. The crucial term is the communication one: δij is communication fidelity, and it decays with distance (dij) at rate λ. Push two players apart, or put a concrete wall between them, and the denominator shrinks — so TPI climbs. The equation says, in symbols, what the next section says in design terms.

The model earns its place because it makes the design problem legible. Fear is not something you add. It is something you get back by attacking the denominator — by lowering N, by degrading δ. The whole craft of co-op horror is hidden in that fraction.

“You do not design fear into a co-op horror game. You design the safety net, and then you spend the rest of development taking it away.”

Step 03 — The First Application

Why the obvious approach — arming the players — fails

Now we can test the model against a real design decision. Suppose you took a classic action-horror template and added co-op. You hand each player an alpha hero: Leon Kennedy, Isaac Clarke, the Doom Marine — capable, armed, built to fight back.

A solo player controlling an alpha hero can still feel fear, because isolation alone keeps N at one. But add the second player and watch the equation collapse. N rises. δ stays high, because nothing is pulling the players apart — the optimal strategy is to stand back-to-back and shoot. The denominator balloons. TPI craters. The game becomes a tactical brawler.

Designers have tried to patch this from the numerator side — capping ammunition, splitting resources — hoping artificial scarcity raises Ψ enough to compensate. It does not produce fear. It produces mechanical frustration, which is a different feeling that happens to be unpleasant. This is the Alpha Hero Problem, and the model tells us exactly why patching the numerator cannot fix a denominator that is too large.

The fix is not a patch. It is a different starting premise: the survivor. Survivors are weak. They have no weapons, or useless ones. They cannot dominate the environment. Their entire objective is to scavenge, solve, and escape. A single mistake incapacitates a player, which drops N, which spikes everyone’s TPI in the same instant. The horror is not produced by the monster. It is produced by the players’ structural inability to fight back.

Step 04 — The Worked Examples

Three games that each attack a different term

A model is only as good as the cases it explains. Here are three, and each one targets a different variable in the fraction.

Phasmophobia attacks Hc — the information deficit

Phasmophobia is structurally an investigative puzzle: gather evidence — EMF readings, fingerprints, freezing temperatures — classify the entity, and escape before it hunts. The fear lives in Hc. You are in a dark room with incomplete knowledge, and the game withholds the one fact that would make you safe.

The model also predicts this game’s known weakness. Because the loop relies on deductive classification, the entity must behave predictably enough to be deduced — so Ψ is capped, and players spend long stretches waiting for passive interactions, which flattens tension. Kinetic Games’ answer, the “Horror 2.0” overhaul with custom assets replacing Unity Store kits, is an attempt to raise the Ψ ceiling without breaking the deduction loop. That is a hard problem, and the equation shows why it is hard.

Lethal Company attacks δ — communication fidelity

Lethal Company, built by solo developer Zeekerss after experimental work on Roblox and itch.io, lives in the δ term. Its monsters are not just obstacles; they are instruments for degrading communication and isolating players. The Bracken stalks silently and retreats when looked at — but stare too long and it turns lethal, forcing players to keep glancing away from their own path. The Coil-Head freezes when observed and moves at near-instant speed the moment you look away. Each one weaponizes attention, and attention is the resource players need to stay coordinated.

The clearest expression of the δ attack is proximity voice chat. Distance and geometry physically degrade volume and clarity — that is λdij made audible. Huddling together restores the buffer; wandering out of range produces sudden, unannounced isolation. The muffled distant scream through concrete, the friend who has simply gone quiet — that is the denominator shrinking in real time, and the player feels it as panic.

Haunt & Harvest proves the model is not about graphics

The strongest test of a claim is a case that strips away everything incidental. Haunt & Harvest by Witchway Games is a tabletop game — no rendering, no audio engine, no monster AI. Players are scarecrows racing to secure 13 victory points’ worth of crows before winter, on farmland where the crops they plant wake at night and rampage.

Its core mechanic is face-down drafting: crop actions and seed allocations are hidden from your teammate. Because no player has perfect information, the board cannot be mathematically optimized. Players must verbally negotiate instead. That is Hc held high and δ degraded — using nothing but cardboard. The lesson lands hard: fear in cooperative design does not require graphical fidelity. It requires structural uncertainty that denies players cognitive control. The variables in our fraction are about information and connection, not polygons.

Three cases, three terms of the fraction under attack

Step 05 — Where the Simple Model Breaks

The players are not rational nodes

Honest teaching means naming where the model stops working. The TPI fraction treats players as clean variables. Real groups under stress behave in ways the equation does not capture — and good designers exploit exactly those gaps.

The model assumes players will act. They often do not. The bystander effect — diffusion of responsibility — means that when a teammate is captured, each survivor quietly assumes someone better-positioned will handle it. In Dead by Daylight this becomes a volunteer’s dilemma: if nobody rescues, the team loses efficacy; if everybody rushes, generators go unattended and the team wipes; if one person volunteers, they alone carry the risk. The equation has no term for that hesitation, yet the hesitation is where much of the drama lives.

The model assumes players read their own competence correctly. They do not. The Dunning–Kruger effect leads less-experienced players to overestimate their skill and take irrational risks. When those fail, cognitive dissonance and loss aversion push them to blame the monster (”overpowered”) or teammates (”amateurs”) — and sometimes to rage-quit or go AFK, which removes a player and spikes everyone’s TPI for a reason the equation cannot see coming.

And the model has no term for laughter. This is the most interesting failure. Co-op horror pivots constantly between dread and hysterical comedy — and the comedy is never scripted. It is emergent, born from spectacular failure under pressure: a friend sprinting confidently into a facility and getting snatched mid-stride, a misjudged jump into a meat grinder narrated live over proximity chat. The game stays mechanically a survival horror experience, but the players supply a comedic release valve the designers never wrote. The fraction measures fear. It cannot measure the joy of watching fear go wrong.

Step 06 — Synthesis

What is proven, and what to do with it

Trace the chain back. We started with one measurable fact: fear is physiological, and company dampens it through social buffering. That single axiom, followed honestly, forced every conclusion that came after — that co-op reshapes the timing of fear rather than removing it, that the design problem is a fraction with a denominator to attack, that arming players collapses the denominator, that the survivor premise and the degradation of N and δ restore it, and that three very different games are really three attacks on the same three variables.

What is not proven: that the TPI equation is anything more than a teaching scaffold. It cannot account for hesitation, ego, or laughter — and those are not small omissions. They are where the genre’s texture actually comes from. Treat the model as a lens that sharpens the design questions, not as a formula that answers them.

The recommendations follow from the proof — each one attacks a named term

1

Design dynamic information asymmetry

Never give every player the same clean picture. One player gets telemetry and no vision; others get direct exposure and no map. This holds Hc high by construction.

2

Make spatial audio a structural mechanic

Proximity voice is not cosmetic. Let distance, walls, and density degrade δ. Let enemies hunt by real microphone feedback. Coordination must be a skill that can fail.

3

Refuse the alpha hero

Keep players weak, vulnerable, dependent on evasion. If weapons exist, make them volatile or costly. A large N with no offsetting deficit kills the fraction.

4

Build for shared, visible failure

Design deaths that are sudden, dramatic, and clippable. This is where the model’s blind spot — emergent comedy — becomes a marketing engine. The failure the equation cannot measure is the part that travels.

The genre’s commercial scale is real — 23 million copies for one title, a Blumhouse film adaptation, a publishing arm funding the next wave of indie developers. But the scale is downstream of the proof. These games sell because they solved the fraction: they keep friends in the room and find ways, every single match, to take that comfort away.

That is the whole argument, and it stands on one fact about the body. Horror did need isolation. Co-op horror did not abandon that requirement — it learned to manufacture isolation on demand, inside a room full of people. The terror was never in the monster. It was always in the distance between you and the person who was supposed to have your back.
