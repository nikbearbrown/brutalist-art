BOOKMAP: Artificial Intelligence in Games

Paul Roberts (2023) | CRC Press / Routledge

PART 1: SECTION-BY-SECTION LOGICAL MAPPING

CHAPTER 1: Introduction

Core Claim: Game AI and academic AI are fundamentally different disciplines sharing common tools but diverging radically in purpose: academic AI pursues maximum intelligence; game AI pursues the illusion of intelligence at playable framerates.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Author draws on production credits across Activision, Sumo Digital, Team17, Traveller’s Tales

  • Three-axis game AI goal: believable, fun, performant (60fps target)

  • Historical grounding: Von Neumann’s 1928 Minimax; Turing’s 1950 chess program requiring 30 minutes per move

  • Genre-behavior matrix (Image 1.1) mapping techniques to game types

Logical Method: Definitional contrast followed by practical decomposition. The author distinguishes academic AI (maximize intelligence, time irrelevant) from game AI (create illusion, time critical) and then breaks game AI into three sub-problems.

Logical Gaps:

  • The “illusion of intelligence” claim—that believability to the player is the only measure that matters—is asserted rather than proven. The sniper example (enemies running between cover positions) is anecdotal. The author does not distinguish between games where player immersion is primary versus competitive games where optimal play is explicitly desired (e.g., fighting game AI that must challenge skilled players).

  • The framerate performance section (reduce/spread/remove the problem) is a design heuristic list, not a framework with criteria for choosing between approaches. When do you reduce versus spread versus remove?

  • The genre cross-reference table claims techniques “may be used” without evidence of when they actually improve outcomes versus when simpler approaches suffice.

Methodological Soundness: Adequate as orientation. The chapter functions as advocacy for a specific professional worldview, not as rigorous analysis. This is appropriate for an introductory textbook but should be understood as such.

CHAPTER 2: Math

Core Claim: Three mathematical constructs—vector magnitude, dot product, and rotation—form the foundational toolkit for game AI spatial reasoning.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Pythagorean theorem derivation for magnitude with practical optimization (squared distance avoids square root)

  • Dot product formula derived step-by-step from first principles

  • Left/right turn determination via the right-facing perpendicular vector

  • Rotation formulas for both origin-centered and arbitrary-point rotation

Logical Method: Problem-first pedagogy: pose a concrete game scenario (sniper needs angle to target), then derive the mathematical tool. This is the book’s consistent pedagogical structure.

Logical Gaps:

  • The “squared distance” optimization (avoiding sqrt) is presented as unambiguously better, but the author acknowledges without resolving that some downstream computations require the actual distance. The decision of when to use squared vs. non-squared distance is never made explicit.

  • The rotation formula is presented without proof—the cos/sin rotation matrix is given as a recipe. A student following this book cannot derive it independently if they’ve forgotten it.

  • The chapter does not address 3D vectors despite noting that “everything we discuss here can be expanded to three dimensions.” This deferred treatment becomes a genuine gap when 3D games are the target.

Methodological Soundness: Sound for 2D foundations. The practical coding section directly implements only three functions (Magnitude, Normalize, Dot), which is a narrow slice of what the chapter covers. The gap between theoretical coverage and implementation scope is notable.

CHAPTER 3: Steering Behaviours

Core Claim: Complex agent movement emerges from combining simple, single-purpose steering forces rather than from designing complex behaviors directly.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Eight individual behaviors derived mathematically (Seek, Flee, Arrive, Wander, Collision Avoidance, Evasion, Intercept, and references to Hide/Interpose)

  • The accumulated force pattern: behaviors prioritized by order, capped to a maximum force magnitude

  • Reynolds’ (1987) flocking paper as theoretical foundation for group behaviors

  • Whisker penetration and obstacle-normal repulsion as a geometrically coherent avoidance mechanism

Logical Method: Decomposition principle: each behavior does exactly one thing. Complexity arises from composition. This is a defensible design philosophy with a clear logical structure.

Logical Gaps:

  • The Wander behavior generates a random angle using inverse cosine of a random dot product value. This is mathematically correct but computationally roundabout—directly generating a random angle in radians and rotating the facing vector would be simpler and equally correct. The author does not justify the chosen approach.

  • The Evasion and Intercept prediction formulas use accumulated speed in the denominator: distance / (maxSpeed + opponentSpeed). This gives a time estimate, but the author does not prove this produces better behavior than simpler alternatives (e.g., fixed lookahead, pure seek). The claim of “more intelligent appearance” is asserted.

  • The ordering/weighting of combined behaviors is described as “trial and error.” This is honest but means the chapter’s core composition framework has no principled guidance for practitioners facing novel combinations.

  • Flocking is presented as three independent forces (Separation, Alignment, Cohesion) with no evidence about their relative weightings or interaction effects. Reynolds’ original paper specified these; the book does not.

Methodological Soundness: The mathematical derivations for individual behaviors are correct and well-structured. The composition framework is a design pattern, not a proven algorithm—its effectiveness depends entirely on case-specific tuning.

CHAPTER 4: Terrain Analysis

Core Claim: Influence maps and heat maps provide spatial AI systems with dynamic, queryable terrain state that enables informed decision-making without per-agent exhaustive search.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Drop-off function with configurable decay rate and minimum cutoff

  • Compound influence when multiple units overlap (additive)

  • Real-time update through the practical project (alien swarm emergent behavior)

  • Cell granularity tradeoff: minimum cell size = single unit; practical recommendation is larger

Logical Method: Two-technique survey: influence maps (strategic, persistent, multi-team) versus heat maps (tactical, local, agent-specific). The distinction is presented as a design choice driven by problem type.

Logical Gaps:

  • The drop-off function is presented without discussion of whether linear, exponential, or other decay functions are appropriate for different game types. “0.9 will exert influence further” is true but insufficient guidance.

  • Emergent behavior is demonstrated in the practical project but the author acknowledges it is “a simple form”—no framework is given for when to expect or engineer emergence versus when it is unpredictable.

  • The shared vs. per-team influence map tradeoff (memory versus AI cheating) is correctly identified but the proposed solution (”at minimum”) lacks discussion of how to handle the memory cost at scale.

  • The refresh rate recommendations (once every 2-3 seconds for RTS) are heuristics from professional experience, not derived from any analysis of game state change rates.

Methodological Soundness: This chapter is largely descriptive and prescriptive. The techniques are well-explained; the guidance for applying them is experience-based rather than principled.

CHAPTER 5: Pathfinding

Core Claim: The A* algorithm, combined with appropriate spatial representation, provides the industry-standard solution to game pathfinding—not because it is optimal, but because it is good enough and well-understood.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Four spatial representation approaches compared (grid, waypoints, NavMesh, corner-node expansion) with explicit tradeoffs

  • A* step-by-step trace across 11 iterations with complete OPEN list state at each step

  • AlphaBeta pruning analogy correctly anticipated: A* vs. Dijkstra framing

  • Author’s own DRS algorithm cited as an example of research that industry ignores in favor of A*

Logical Method: The chapter’s logical spine is the cost-accuracy tradeoff: reducing the search space always speeds pathfinding but reduces path quality. This is stated early and demonstrated throughout.

Logical Gaps:

  • The claim “we always seem to use A*” is accurate as an industry observation but the author does not explain under what conditions A*’s known limitations (poor performance in dynamic environments, excessive node expansion when no path exists) become decision-relevant. The student reading this may over-apply A*.

  • The heuristic choice (Manhattan vs. Euclidean) is presented as producing “slightly different results” without characterizing when each is preferable. Euclidean consistently underestimates (admissible) while Manhattan may overestimate in 8-directional grids—an important distinction the book elides.

  • Path smoothing is mentioned as “an additional process” without any specification of how to implement it. This is a significant practical gap for students.

  • The step-by-step example uses only one scenario (no dead-ends, successful path found). A student gains no intuition about A*’s behavior when paths don’t exist.

Methodological Soundness: The A* derivation and trace are correct and unusually thorough for a textbook. The chapter’s practical limitations are honest acknowledgments of scope, not errors.

CHAPTER 6: Decision-Making

Core Claim: Four decision-making architectures—FSM, FuSM, Stack-Based FSM, and Behavior Trees—address progressively complex agent state management requirements, with Behavior Trees representing the current industry standard for complex agents.

Supporting Evidence:

  • FSM limitations correctly identified: predictability, single-state constraint, no memory

  • Stack-based FSM addresses memory directly with push/pop semantics (Pac-Man ghost example)

  • FuSM addresses multi-state concurrency (tank battle example)

  • Behavior Trees origin: Damian Isla, Halo 2, 2002—correctly cited, though the author dates it as “2002” in the chapter but the introduction calls it “Halo 2 by Damian Isla”

  • Alien: Isolation’s Xenomorph referenced for runtime behavior unlocking

Logical Method: Problem escalation: each architecture is introduced as a solution to the limitations of the previous one. FSM → Stack-based FSM (adds memory) → FuSM (adds concurrency) → BT (adds scalability and modularity).

Logical Gaps:

  • The relationship between FuSM and Behavior Trees is unclear. FuSM is presented as a solution to multi-state concurrency; Behavior Trees are presented as a solution to scalability. But do Behavior Trees also solve the concurrency problem? The chapter does not address how parallel composite nodes in BTs address what FuSM addresses.

  • The claim that “Behavior Trees have become a staple in AI game development” is not supported with evidence. The Halo 2 origin is documented; widespread adoption is asserted.

  • The practical tutorial implements only two of the four architectures (FSM + BT). FuSM and Stack-based FSM are explained theoretically but never implemented, which creates an asymmetry between theory and code for these techniques.

  • State thrashing is identified as an FSM failure mode but no solution is provided within the FSM framework (hysteresis thresholds, for example).

Methodological Soundness: The escalation logic is pedagogically sound. The theoretical coverage is complete; the practical coverage is selective.

CHAPTER 7: Fuzzy Logic

Core Claim: Fuzzy Logic replaces binary decision boundaries with continuous membership functions, producing smoother and more linguistically natural agent behavior at the cost of increased authoring complexity.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Complete worked example (Speedball throw risk) traced through all three stages: fuzzification → rule application → defuzzification

  • Three defuzzification methods: Highest Membership Mean, Geometric Centroid, Averaged Degree

  • Rule table of 27 combinations systematically derived

  • Hedges (Very, Somewhat, Extremely) shown as mathematical transformations (square, sqrt, cube)

  • Practical project (artillery tank) demonstrates a use case where defuzzification is explicitly skipped as unnecessary

Logical Method: This is the book’s most mathematically complete chapter. The Speedball example follows a complete logical chain from expert knowledge extraction through numerical computation to a crisp output.

Logical Gaps:

  • The rule tables (Images 7.17–7.19) are stated to be “based on the feedback we got from our fictional player”—which means the rules are entirely arbitrary. The chapter does not address how to elicit or validate fuzzy rules from real domain experts, which is the actual hard problem in deploying fuzzy systems.

  • The three defuzzification methods produce different values (68.75, 67.0, and 68.75 respectively in the worked example). The author does not discuss when the choice of defuzzification method matters—only that centroid is “more precise but more expensive.”

  • The practical project skips defuzzification because “we only need the highest scoring output set.” This is a significant departure from the theory that goes unexplained. In competitive scenarios (e.g., two outputs very close in value), this could produce discontinuous behavior identical to what fuzzy logic was meant to eliminate.

Methodological Soundness: The mathematical derivations are correct and well-annotated. The practical departure from theory (no defuzzification) is the chapter’s most significant logical tension—the author acknowledges it without resolving it.

CHAPTER 8: Chess AI

Core Claim: The Minimax algorithm with AlphaBeta pruning provides optimal move selection for perfect-information adversarial games; board evaluation functions determine the character and skill of the resulting AI.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Minimax derivation traced through a 4-ply game tree with explicit node scoring

  • AlphaBeta pruning mechanism explained through sequential image trace, demonstrating why pruned branches are provably irrelevant

  • Evaluation function components: piece value, board positioning, moves available, attacks available

  • Historical anchoring: Claude Shannon, 1949; Deep Blue’s ~8000 evaluation features

Logical Method: The Minimax chapter is the most logically rigorous in the book. The pruning correctness argument is presented informally but correctly: a min node’s value can only decrease; if it’s already below the current best of its max parent, further search cannot change the outcome.

Logical Gaps:

  • The evaluation functions are presented additively (piece value + position bonus + moves available + attack value) without discussion of interaction effects or relative scaling. Adding piece value (5 for a Knight) to moves available (1 per move) to attack value (1–5 per attack) produces a sum where the contributions are not commensurable. The author notes Deep Blue uses ~8000 features, implicitly acknowledging that the presented evaluation is primitive, but does not address scaling.

  • Iterative deepening is presented as a “possible optimization” but the repeated search cost is acknowledged without quantification. The student has no way to judge when iterative deepening pays off.

  • The playbook solution to the opening problem is correct but the recommendation to “switch to Minimax when the opponent deviates from expected moves” requires detecting deviation—a non-trivial problem left entirely to the reader.

  • The practical project explicitly notes the AI will be “extremely slow” due to the choice of readable over optimized code. This is pedagogically honest but creates a product that cannot be experienced as a game, limiting the student’s ability to assess whether the evaluation function produces good play.

Methodological Soundness: The algorithm exposition is correct. The evaluation function discussion is illustrative rather than complete—appropriate for a textbook introduction.

CHAPTER 9: Genetic Algorithms

Core Claim: Genetic Algorithms are search and optimization techniques that evolve solutions to problems where the correct solution is unknown or the problem space is too large for exhaustive search, by applying selection, crossover, and mutation to populations of candidate solutions.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Five selection methods: Tournament, Roulette Wheel, Stochastic Universal Sampling, Rank, Linear Ranking, Elitist

  • Three crossover methods: 1-point, n-point, Uniform

  • Royal Battle 1866 example traced through complete lifecycle (initial population → fitness → selection → crossover → mutation)

  • Practical project: chromosome = sequence of movement instructions; fitness = distance to finish line

Logical Method: The chapter mirrors the AutoTutor paper structure in the bookmap document—systematic enumeration of variants with acknowledgment of tradeoffs, followed by practical demonstration. The logical structure is: here is a technique → here are its variants → here is when each applies.

Logical Gaps:

  • The selection method descriptions note tradeoffs but provide no decision criteria. A student faces 5+ selection methods with no principled basis for choosing. “It depends on the problem” is acknowledged but never operationalized.

  • The chromosome design warning (crossover between utilities and weapons means weapon genes can never be recombined) is the chapter’s most important practical insight—but it’s buried mid-chapter and not elevated as a design principle.

  • Mutation rate guidance (”1% or 2%”) is a heuristic without theoretical justification. The “50 minutes per generation” time estimate for the racing example should prompt the student to ask when genetic algorithms are worth the cost. The chapter ends with “genetic algorithms are not a good option for a released title”—a significant caveat that undermines much of what preceded it without specifying what they are good for in shipped products.

  • The practical project uses chromosomes of ship movement instructions, which is a neuroevolution-adjacent problem. The fitness function (distance to finish line) creates a local optimum problem (ships that crash just past the midpoint score better than ships that don’t start) that is not acknowledged.

Methodological Soundness: The technique survey is comprehensive. The practical guidance is thin. The chapter’s most valuable insight (chromosome design determines what can be evolved) receives the least emphasis.

CHAPTER 10: Neural Networks

Core Claim: Artificial neural networks—single-layer and multi-layer perceptrons—can learn to solve classification and control problems through weight adjustment, but are unreliable as game AI behavior systems due to unpredictability.

Supporting Evidence:

  • Single-layer perceptron derivation: AND and OR logic gate solutions with explicit weight notation

  • Multi-layer perceptron necessity proven through XOR non-linear separability

  • XOR network fully solved with manual forward pass verification across all four input combinations

  • Snake game neural network: 12 inputs × 4 hidden × 4 outputs = 192 weights

  • Final verdict: “neural networks are not a particularly useful approach for game AI”

Logical Method: This is the chapter with the most explicit negative claim in the book. The author builds up to the technique, demonstrates it completely, and then argues against its use—a rare structure in a textbook that typically advocates for every technique it covers.

Logical Gaps:

  • The back propagation presentation is conceptually correct but incomplete. The adjustment formula (Learning rate * error * activation value) is given without the chain rule derivation that motivates it. A student implementing back propagation from this description will not understand why the formula is correct—only that it is.

  • The genetic algorithm approach to weight learning is presented as an alternative to back propagation, justified because “we don’t know the desired output.” This reasoning applies equally to reinforcement learning (Q-learning, policy gradients), which is not mentioned despite being the primary method for training game-playing agents in contemporary research.

  • The chapter’s negative verdict (”neural networks are not a particularly useful approach for game AI”) is not qualified by the significant contemporary counterexamples: AlphaGo/AlphaZero, OpenAI Five, neural network-based game AI in contemporary commercial titles. The book’s 2023 publication date makes this a meaningful omission.

  • The 12-input Snake network is designed with inputs that include movement legality (is the cell blocked?), pickup direction, and collision distance. The logic for these inputs is sound, but the author does not discuss why 4 hidden nodes is appropriate or what happens when the network has too few hidden nodes to represent the required decision boundary.

Methodological Soundness: The theoretical exposition is accurate. The negative verdict is underqualified given the state of the field at publication.

BRIDGE: Synthesizing the Logical Architecture

Three structural tensions run through the entire book:

Tension 1: Pedagogy versus Completeness. The book consistently chooses readable, demonstrable code over optimal or complete implementations. This is explicitly stated (Chapter 8 Chess project) and is a legitimate pedagogical choice. But it creates a pattern where every chapter ends with the implicit disclaimer “this is how to understand the technique, not how to ship it.” The student gains conceptual literacy without professional readiness—a gap the book does not fully acknowledge.

Tension 2: Heuristics presented as principles. Throughout, professional heuristics (5 whiskers is “usually adequate,” refresh influence maps every 2-3 seconds, 1-2% mutation rate, 4 hidden nodes for Snake) are presented as recommendations without the evidence that would allow a student to modify them intelligently. The book teaches what practitioners do, not why those practices are robust.

Tension 3: The illusion framing versus the optimization framing. The introduction argues game AI should create the illusion of intelligence rather than actual intelligence. But Chapters 8-10 (Chess, Genetic Algorithms, Neural Networks) cover techniques that pursue actual optimization. The book never resolves whether game AI programmers should be creating illusions or finding optimal solutions—the answer is “it depends on the game,” but this is never made explicit.

What the book proves with evidence:

  • Vector math, dot products, and rotation provide complete spatial computation foundations for 2D game AI

  • A* finds shortest paths in gridded environments when paths exist

  • Fuzzy Logic produces smooth, linguistically structured decision-making at specified computational cost

  • Minimax with AlphaBeta pruning finds optimal moves in perfect-information adversarial games

  • Genetic Algorithms can evolve solutions to optimization problems in development environments

What the book asserts without proof:

  • The chapter-closing “Final Thoughts” recommendations (e.g., “if FSM can do the job, use it”)

  • Refresh rate heuristics throughout terrain analysis

  • That the specific chromosome designs, selection methods, and weighting combinations in the practicals represent good choices rather than arbitrary ones

  • That neural networks are generally unsuitable for game AI behavior—argued by assertion, not demonstrated by comparison

The book’s most significant gap: No chapter addresses failure modes systematically. Each technique is taught as a tool that works when correctly applied; the conditions under which each technique fails, degrades, or is inappropriate are acknowledged only in passing. A practitioner needs to know when not to use A*, when influence maps become stale, when behavior trees become unmanageable—this book does not teach that.

PART 2: LITERARY REVIEW ESSAY

The Honest Textbook

There is a particular kind of intellectual honesty that is rarer in textbooks than it should be. It shows up when an author spends three hundred pages teaching you how to build something, then tells you in the final chapter that it probably won’t work the way you want.

Paul Roberts does this in Artificial Intelligence in Games. After nine chapters of careful exposition, working code, and painstaking mathematical derivation, he closes the neural networks chapter with the following: “It may come as a surprise, but neural networks are not a particularly useful approach to use for game AI. The results can be unpredictable, which goes against the idea of games being an authored experience.”

This is a significant thing to say. It is also, within the scope of this book’s argument, logically consistent. Whether it is empirically defensible in 2023 is a different question—one worth examining in detail.

Roberts establishes the book’s core premise in Chapter 1 with unusual precision. Game AI, he argues, has three requirements: it must appear intelligent, it must be fun to play against, and it must run at a tolerable framerate. These three requirements are in permanent tension, and the entire subsequent book is an exploration of how practitioners navigate that tension.

The first requirement—appearing intelligent—is the most interesting. Roberts distinguishes it explicitly from being intelligent. His sniper game anecdote makes the logical structure clear: enemies that run between cover positions during a firefight are technically “smart” (they’re repositioning tactically) but appear “dumb” because the player, operating from a static vantage point, can observe the behavior and find it absurd. The intelligence is real. The illusion has failed.

This framing does real analytical work. It explains why A* (Chapter 5) remains the industry pathfinding standard despite the existence of superior algorithms—the paths it produces are “reasonable to the player” even if not optimal, and its behavior under failure conditions is predictable. It explains why the book devotes an entire chapter to fuzzy logic rather than hard thresholds—because behavior that smoothly degrades as conditions change looks more intelligent than behavior that snaps between states. And it explains the neural network verdict: a technique that produces optimal play but unpredictably switches strategies depending on initialization is worse, by the book’s own metric, than a hand-tuned behavior tree that a designer understands completely.

The book’s most rigorous chapter is also its least typical. Chapter 8 on Chess AI is the only chapter that proves its central claim rather than demonstrating it. Minimax with AlphaBeta pruning guarantees optimal play within the search depth. This is not asserted—it follows from the algorithm’s structure, which Roberts traces through with commendable patience across eleven iterations and five tables of OPEN list states.

The pruning correctness argument deserves scrutiny. Roberts explains: a minimum node retains only the lowest value from its children. If the current best value for that minimum node is already lower than the current best value of its maximum parent, then all subsequent children of the minimum node—which can only make the minimum node’s value lower—are irrelevant to the maximum parent’s decision. Therefore they need not be searched.

This reasoning is correct. It is also informal. The mathematical proof of AlphaBeta optimality (that it produces identical results to exhaustive Minimax) is not given, but the intuition is accurately conveyed. For a practitioner, this is probably sufficient. For a student who encounters a bug in their implementation, it may not be.

The evaluation functions chapter exposes the book’s characteristic tension. After proving Minimax’s optimality, Roberts introduces evaluation components (piece value, board position, moves available, attacks available) as additive terms. The logic is clear: more pieces and more options are better. But these terms are incommensurable. A Knight is worth 5 points; an open Rook channel generates 14 points from “moves available” alone. No analysis is given of whether these scales produce sensible relative valuations. Roberts acknowledges this implicitly by noting Deep Blue uses approximately 8000 evaluation features—a figure that implies the simplified evaluation presented here is a starting point, not a solution.

This is honest. It is also incomplete. The gap between “here is a working evaluation function” and “here is how to know if your evaluation function is good” is one the book cannot bridge without significantly expanding its scope.

The genetic algorithms chapter (Chapter 9) contains the book’s most practically important insight, delivered with insufficient emphasis.

Discussing the n-point crossover design for the Royal Battle 1866 chromosome (8 bits, 4 utilities + 4 weapons), Roberts notes: “If we did a single-point crossover down the middle, child chromosomes would simply be switching around the whole weapon section and whole utilities section.” He then identifies the consequence: if the crossover never touches the internal structure of the weapon section, weapon configurations cannot evolve. Parents will pass along their weapon genes intact—potentially propagating suboptimal configurations indefinitely.

This is a fundamental constraint on what genetic algorithms can actually optimize. The chromosome encoding determines the topology of the search space. A crossover point that never bisects a group of related genes treats that group as an indivisible unit—which may or may not be appropriate depending on whether those genes interact. The author identifies this problem but does not generalize it to a design principle: the chromosome design is more consequential than the selection or crossover method, because the design determines what the algorithm can and cannot discover.

Instead, the chapter spends comparable space on five selection methods, three crossover methods, and mutation approaches—all of which are secondary to getting the chromosome structure right. This is a misallocation of analytical emphasis that a rigorous textbook should correct.

The final chapter’s negative verdict on neural networks requires more careful treatment than it receives.

Roberts argues that neural networks are inappropriate for game AI behavior because their results are unpredictable. This is true for vanilla multi-layer perceptrons trained with random initialization—different training runs may produce qualitatively different behaviors even when the fitness scores converge. A designer cannot read a weight matrix and understand what the network will do in an edge case. This is a genuine problem for authored game experiences.

But the verdict is overstated. Roberts acknowledges that “extremely complicated board games such as Go can use this approach”—which in 2023 undersells the situation considerably. Neural networks are the dominant technology for game-playing agents at the research frontier, including commercial applications. The book’s 2023 publication date places it after AlphaGo, AlphaZero, and significant work on neural network-based commercial game AI. Treating neural networks as categorically ill-suited to games, rather than ill-suited to specific game AI problems (authored NPC behavior), conflates two distinct use cases.

To be precise: Roberts is correct that neural networks are poor tools for designing NPCs with predictable authored behavior in narrative games. He is incorrect, or at least significantly incomplete, in extending that verdict to game AI as a field. The chapter teaches students to implement a specific architecture (multi-layer perceptron with genetic algorithm weight training) for a specific task (SNAKE control), demonstrates that it works, and then recommends against using the approach. What it does not do is help the student understand where neural networks would be the right choice—adversarial game opponents in competitive environments, procedural content generation, player behavior modeling—leaving them less equipped to reason about the most significant active development in their field.

What this book succeeds at, and where it quietly earns its place in a technical curriculum, is the middle chapters.

Chapters 3 through 6—Steering Behaviors, Terrain Analysis, Pathfinding, Decision-Making—represent the core of what a professional game AI programmer actually does most of the time. These chapters are taught with mathematical rigor (derivations are given, not just recipes), practical candor (the author states when trial and error is the only real approach), and appropriate scope (each technique is bounded clearly). The step-by-step A* trace in Chapter 5 is the best such exposition in any introductory textbook this reviewer has encountered. Eleven iterations, complete OPEN list states, explicit annotation of every decision—it is the kind of derivation that produces genuine understanding rather than pattern-matching.

Roberts is also honest in ways that many textbooks are not. He tells students which of his code examples are slow due to readability tradeoffs. He tells them when he doesn’t know the optimal solution. He identifies when techniques fail. The sniper AI that looks dumb, the Chess AI that freezes the game, the neural network that produces unpredictable behavior—these failures are taught, not hidden.

A rigorous evaluation of this book as a teaching instrument requires asking one question: what can a student do after reading it that they could not do before?

They can implement A*, steering behaviors, FSMs, behavior trees, fuzzy logic, Minimax with AlphaBeta pruning, genetic algorithms, and basic neural networks in Unity with C#. They can reason about spatial representations, terrain analysis, and pathfinding tradeoffs. They understand the distinction between academic and game AI as professional contexts.

What they cannot do: reason about when these techniques fail at scale, design chromosome encodings for novel genetic algorithm problems, evaluate whether their Minimax evaluation function is well-calibrated, or understand where neural networks are appropriate in contemporary game development.

The book describes itself as appropriate for “students and aspiring games programmers looking to gain a grounding in game AI techniques.” By this measure, it succeeds. A grounding. The gaps are not pedagogical failures—they are scope decisions. The honest textbook, it turns out, is also the bounded one.
